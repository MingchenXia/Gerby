11.1 Hilbert spaces and closed operator complexes3F2EA9D1CB5E

From Foundations of pluripotential theory · 11.1 Hilbert spaces and closed operator complexes

A complex Hilbert space is a complex vector space with an inner product $\langle \cdot ,\cdot \rangle $ that is complete for the norm $\| x\| =\langle x,x\rangle ^{1/2}$; our inner products are linear in the first variable and conjugate-linear in the second. A real Hilbert space is defined in the same way, with a real vector space and a real inner product, which is then symmetric and bilinear. All results of this section hold for real Hilbert spaces, with the same proofs: the completeness, proved below, of the spaces $L^2(\mu )$ of real-valued functions, and proposition 11.1.1, proposition 11.1.2, lemma 11.1.3, theorem 11.1.4. Indeed, these proofs use only the axioms of an inner product and completeness. For real spaces one omits all complex conjugations, reads “conjugate-linear” as “linear”, uses only real values of $t$ in the proof of proposition 11.1.1, and replaces the coefficients in $\mathbb Q+\mathrm i\mathbb Q$ in the proof of proposition 11.1.2 by rational ones.

The Hilbert spaces of this chapter are weighted $L^2$ spaces. For a positive measure $\mu $ on a measurable space, $L^2(\mu )$ carries the inner product $\langle f,g\rangle =\int f\bar g\, \mathrm d\mu $, and it is complete. Indeed, let $(f_ j)$ be a Cauchy sequence in $L^2(\mu )$, and choose $j_1<j_2<\cdots $ with $\| f_{j_{k+1}}-f_{j_ k}\| \le 2^{-k}$. By the triangle inequality and the monotone convergence theorem, $g=\sum _ k|f_{j_{k+1}}-f_{j_ k}|$ satisfies $\| g\| \le 1$, so $g<\infty $ almost everywhere. Hence $(f_{j_ k})$ converges almost everywhere to a function $f$, and the bound $|f-f_{j_ k}|\le \sum _{l\ge k}|f_{j_{l+1}}-f_{j_ l}|$ gives $\| f-f_{j_ k}\| \le 2^{1-k}$. A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges, so $f_ j\to f$ in $L^2(\mu )$. Applying this to each coefficient, we see that spaces of $L^2$ differential forms, whose elements are finite tuples of functions in $L^2(\mu )$, are complete as well.

Proposition 11.1.1. Let $H$ be a complex Hilbert space. Every nonempty closed convex subset of $H$ contains a unique point of smallest distance to a given point of $H$. If $M\subset H$ is a closed linear subspace, then $H=M\oplus M^\perp $ and $(M^\perp )^\perp =M$. Every bounded linear functional $L$ on $H$ has the form $L(x)=\langle x,y\rangle $ for a unique $y\in H$, and every bounded conjugate-linear functional $\ell $ on $H$ has the form $\ell (x)=\langle y,x\rangle $ for a unique $y\in H$; in both cases the norm of the functional is $\| y\| $.

Proof. Let $C$ be a nonempty closed convex set and $x\in H$. Replacing $C$ by $C-x$, we may assume that $x=0$. Let $d=\inf _{z\in C}\| z\| $, and let $(x_ j)$ be a sequence in $C$ with $\| x_ j\| \to d$. Since $(x_ j+x_ k)/2\in C$ by convexity, the parallelogram identity gives

\[ \| x_ j-x_ k\| ^2=2\| x_ j\| ^2+2\| x_ k\| ^2 -4\| (x_ j+x_ k)/2\| ^2 \le 2\| x_ j\| ^2+2\| x_ k\| ^2-4d^2. \]

The right side tends to zero as $j,k\to \infty $, so $(x_ j)$ is a Cauchy sequence. Its limit lies in $C$, because $C$ is closed, and has norm $d$. If $x,x'\in C$ both have norm $d$, the same inequality with $x_ j=x$ and $x_ k=x'$ gives $\| x-x'\| ^2\le 0$, which proves uniqueness.

Now let $M$ be a closed linear subspace and $x\in H$. Let $p$ be the point of $M$ closest to $x$, and put $r=x-p$. For $v\in M$ and $t\in \mathbb C$ we have $p+tv\in M$, and hence

\[ 0\le \| r-tv\| ^2-\| r\| ^2=-2\operatorname{Re}\bigl (\bar t\langle r,v\rangle \bigr )+|t|^2\| v\| ^2. \]

Taking small real $t$ of both signs gives $\operatorname{Re}\langle r,v\rangle =0$, and taking $t\in \mathrm i\mathbb R$ gives $\operatorname{Im}\langle r,v\rangle =0$. Thus $r\in M^\perp $ and $x=p+r$. Since $M\cap M^\perp =\{ 0\} $, this decomposition is unique, so $H=M\oplus M^\perp $. Every element of $M$ is orthogonal to $M^\perp $, so $M\subset (M^\perp )^\perp $. Conversely, if $x\in (M^\perp )^\perp $ and $x=p+r$ as above, then $r=x-p$ lies in the linear subspace $(M^\perp )^\perp $ and in $M^\perp $, so $\| r\| ^2=\langle r,r\rangle =0$ and $x=p\in M$. We call $p$ the orthogonal projection of $x$ onto $M$; the map $P\colon x\mapsto p$ is linear by the uniqueness of the decomposition, and $\| Px\| \le \| x\| $, because $\| x\| ^2=\| p\| ^2+\| r\| ^2$.

Finally, let $L$ be a bounded linear functional. If $L=0$ we take $y=0$. Otherwise $\ker L$ is a proper closed subspace. Choose $x_0\notin \ker L$, and let $e$ be its component in $(\ker L)^\perp $; then $e\ne 0$ and $L(e)=L(x_0)\ne 0$. For every $x\in H$ the vector $x-(L(x)/L(e))e$ lies in $\ker L$ and is therefore orthogonal to $e$, so that $\langle x,e\rangle =(L(x)/L(e))\| e\| ^2$. With $y=\overline{L(e)}\, e/\| e\| ^2$ this reads $L(x)=\langle x,y\rangle $. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality shows that $L$ has norm at most $\| y\| $, and $L(y)=\| y\| ^2$ shows equality. If $y'$ also represents $L$, then $\langle x,y-y'\rangle =0$ for all $x$, and $x=y-y'$ gives $y=y'$. If $\ell $ is a bounded conjugate-linear functional, then $\overline\ell \colon x\mapsto \overline{\ell (x)}$ is a bounded linear functional with the same norm, and its representation $\overline{\ell (x)}=\langle x,y\rangle $ reads $\ell (x)=\langle y,x\rangle $. $\square$

Proposition 11.1.2. Every bounded sequence in a Hilbert space $H$ has a weakly convergent subsequence, that is, a subsequence $(x_ j)$ for which there is $x\in H$ with $\langle x_ j,v\rangle \to \langle x,v\rangle $ for every $v\in H$. If $(x_ j)$ converges weakly to $x$, then $\| x\| \le \varliminf _ j\| x_ j\| $.

Proof. Let $(x_ j)$ be a sequence with $\| x_ j\| \le C$, and let $V$ be the closed linear span of its terms. The finite linear combinations of the $x_ j$ with coefficients in $\mathbb Q+\mathrm i\mathbb Q$ form a countable dense subset of $V$. Applying the Gram–Schmidt process to it, and discarding the vectors that depend linearly on earlier ones, we obtain a finite or countable orthonormal family $(e_ k)$ whose linear span is dense in $V$.

For each $k$ the sequence $(\langle x_ j,e_ k\rangle )_ j$ is bounded by $C$, so by a diagonal argument we may pass to a subsequence, still denoted $(x_ j)$, such that $\langle x_ j,e_ k\rangle $ converges to a number $a_ k$ for every $k$. For every $N$, Bessel's inequality gives $\sum _{k\le N}|\langle x_ j,e_ k\rangle |^2\le C^2$; letting $j\to \infty $ and then $N\to \infty $, we get $\sum _ k|a_ k|^2\le C^2$. Since $\| \sum _{N<k\le N'}a_ ke_ k\| ^2=\sum _{N<k\le N'}|a_ k|^2$ by Pythagoras' theorem, the partial sums of the series $x=\sum _ ka_ ke_ k$ form a Cauchy sequence; the series therefore converges in the closed, hence complete, subspace $V$, and $\| x\| ^2=\sum _ k|a_ k|^2\le C^2$.

By the continuity of the inner product and the orthonormality of the $e_ k$, we have $\langle x,e_ k\rangle =a_ k=\lim _ j\langle x_ j,e_ k\rangle $ for every $k$; hence $\langle x_ j,v\rangle \to \langle x,v\rangle $ for every $v$ in the linear span of the $e_ k$. If $v\in V$ and $v'$ lies in that span with $\| v-v'\| <\delta $, then $|\langle x_ j-x,v\rangle |\le |\langle x_ j-x,v'\rangle |+2C\delta $, so the convergence extends to all $v\in V$. For an arbitrary $v\in H$, write $v=v_1+v_2$ with $v_1\in V$ and $v_2\in V^\perp $ by proposition 11.1.1; since $x_ j$ and $x$ lie in $V$, we get $\langle x_ j-x,v\rangle =\langle x_ j-x,v_1\rangle \to 0$.

For the last assertion we may assume that $x\ne 0$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $\| x\| ^2=\lim _ j\langle x_ j,x\rangle \le \varliminf _ j\| x_ j\| \, \| x\| $, and we divide by $\| x\| $. $\square$

Differential operators are defined only on subspaces of $L^2$ spaces, so we need the following notions. Let $H_0$ and $H_1$ be Hilbert spaces. An operator $T$ from $H_0$ to $H_1$ is a linear map $T\colon \operatorname{Dom}T\to H_1$ defined on a linear subspace $\operatorname{Dom}T\subset H_0$, its domain. We write $\ker T$ for its kernel, $\operatorname {Ran}T=T(\operatorname{Dom}T)$ for its range, and $\operatorname {Gr}T=\{ (x,Tx):x\in \operatorname{Dom}T\} $ for its graph, a subspace of the Hilbert space $H_0\oplus H_1$ with the inner product $\langle (x,y),(x',y')\rangle =\langle x,x'\rangle +\langle y,y'\rangle $. The operator $T$ is densely defined if $\operatorname{Dom}T$ is dense in $H_0$, and closed if $\operatorname {Gr}T$ is closed; equivalently, $x_ j\in \operatorname{Dom}T$, $x_ j\to x$ and $Tx_ j\to y$ imply $x\in \operatorname{Dom}T$ and $Tx=y$. In particular, the kernel of a closed operator is a closed subspace.

If $T$ is densely defined, its Hilbert adjoint $T^*$, an operator from $H_1$ to $H_0$, is defined as follows: $y\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*$ if the linear functional $x\mapsto \langle Tx,y\rangle $ on $\operatorname{Dom}T$ is bounded with respect to the norm of $H_0$, and then $T^*y$ is the vector determined by

\[ \langle Tx,y\rangle =\langle x,T^*y\rangle \qquad (x\in \operatorname{Dom}T). \]

This vector is well defined: since $\operatorname{Dom}T$ is dense, the functional extends uniquely to a bounded linear functional on $H_0$, which proposition 11.1.1 represents by a unique vector.

Lemma 11.1.3. Let $T$ be a densely defined operator from $H_0$ to $H_1$. Then $T^*$ is closed and $\ker T^*=(\operatorname {Ran}T)^\perp $. If $T$ is also closed, then $T^*$ is densely defined and $T^{**}=T$.

Proof. If $y_ j\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*$, $y_ j\to y$ and $T^*y_ j\to w$, then for every $x\in \operatorname{Dom}T$

\[ \langle Tx,y\rangle =\lim _ j\langle Tx,y_ j\rangle =\lim _ j\langle x,T^*y_ j\rangle =\langle x,w\rangle ; \]

hence $y\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*$ and $T^*y=w$, so $T^*$ is closed. A vector $y\in H_1$ is orthogonal to $\operatorname {Ran}T$ exactly when the functional $x\mapsto \langle Tx,y\rangle $ vanishes, that is, when $y\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*$ and $T^*y=0$. This proves $\ker T^*=(\operatorname {Ran}T)^\perp $. In the same way, $(a,b)\in H_0\oplus H_1$ is orthogonal to $\operatorname {Gr}T$ if and only if $\langle Tx,b\rangle =\langle x,-a\rangle $ for all $x\in \operatorname{Dom}T$, so

\[ (\operatorname {Gr}T)^\perp =\{ (-T^*y,y):y\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*\} . \]

Now let $T$ be closed, and let $v\in H_1$ be orthogonal to $\operatorname{Dom}T^*$. Since $\operatorname {Gr}T$ is closed, proposition 11.1.1 and the description of $(\operatorname {Gr}T)^\perp $ decompose $(0,v)$ as $(x,Tx)+(-T^*y,y)$ with $x\in \operatorname{Dom}T$ and $y\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*$. Thus $x=T^*y$ and $v=Tx+y$. Since $v$ is orthogonal to $y\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*$, we obtain

\[ 0=\langle v,y\rangle =\langle Tx,y\rangle +\| y\| ^2=\langle x,T^*y\rangle +\| y\| ^2=\| x\| ^2+\| y\| ^2, \]

so $x=y=0$ and $v=0$. Hence $\operatorname{Dom}T^*$ is dense, and $T^{**}$ is defined. Let $J\colon H_1\oplus H_0\to H_0\oplus H_1$ and $J'\colon H_0\oplus H_1\to H_1\oplus H_0$ be the unitary maps $J(b,a)=(-a,b)$ and $J'(a,b)=(-b,a)$. The description of the orthogonal complement of a graph, applied to $T$ and to the densely defined operator $T^*$, reads $(\operatorname {Gr}T)^\perp =J(\operatorname {Gr}T^*)$ and $(\operatorname {Gr}T^*)^\perp =J'(\operatorname {Gr}T^{**})$. For a closed subspace $M$ we have $(M^\perp )^\perp =M$ by proposition 11.1.1, and unitary maps carry orthogonal complements to orthogonal complements. Therefore

\[ \operatorname {Gr}T=\bigl ((\operatorname {Gr}T)^\perp \bigr )^\perp =J\bigl ((\operatorname {Gr}T^*)^\perp \bigr ) =JJ'(\operatorname {Gr}T^{**})=\operatorname {Gr}T^{**}, \]

because $JJ'=-\mathrm{id}$ and graphs are linear subspaces. Thus $T^{**}=T$. $\square$

The next theorem, the abstract form of Hörmander's method, produces a solution of $Tu=g$ from an inequality for the adjoint operator. The second operator $S$ allows us to require the inequality only on $\operatorname{Dom}T^*\cap \operatorname{Dom}S$; in the application in section 11.3, smooth compactly supported forms are dense in this space, and for them the inequality follows from an integration by parts.

Theorem 11.1.4. Let $H_0,H_1,H_2$ be Hilbert spaces, let $T$ from $H_0$ to $H_1$ be a closed densely defined operator, and let $S$ from $H_1$ to $H_2$ be a closed operator with $\operatorname {Ran}T\subset \ker S$. Let $g\in \ker S$ and $0\le M<\infty $, and assume that

\[ |\langle g,f\rangle |^2\le M\bigl (\| T^*f\| ^2+\| Sf\| ^2\bigr ) \qquad (f\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*\cap \operatorname{Dom}S). \]

Then the equation $Tu=g$ has a solution $u\in \operatorname{Dom}T$ with $\| u\| ^2\le M$. Among all solutions there is a unique one of smallest norm, and it is orthogonal to $\ker T$.

Proof. Since $S$ is closed, $\ker S$ is a closed subspace, and $H_1=\ker S\oplus (\ker S)^\perp $ by proposition 11.1.1. Since $\operatorname {Ran}T\subset \ker S$, lemma 11.1.3 gives $(\ker S)^\perp \subset (\operatorname {Ran}T)^\perp =\ker T^*$. Let $f\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*$, and write $f=f_0+f_1$ with $f_0\in \ker S$ and $f_1\in (\ker S)^\perp $. Then $f_1\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*$ and $T^*f_1=0$, so $f_0\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*\cap \operatorname{Dom}S$, $T^*f_0=T^*f$ and $Sf_0=0$. Moreover $\langle g,f\rangle =\langle g,f_0\rangle $, because $g\in \ker S$. The hypothesis applied to $f_0$ therefore gives

\[ |\langle g,f\rangle |\le \sqrt M\, \| T^*f\| \qquad (f\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*). \]

Consequently the map $\lambda \colon T^*f\mapsto \langle g,f\rangle $ is well defined on $\operatorname {Ran}T^*$: if $T^*f=T^*f'$, then $|\langle g,f-f'\rangle |\le \sqrt M\, \| T^*(f-f')\| =0$. It is conjugate-linear and bounded by $\sqrt M$, hence uniformly continuous, so it extends uniquely to a conjugate-linear functional $\tilde\lambda $ on the closed subspace $W=\overline{\operatorname {Ran}T^*}$, again bounded by $\sqrt M$. If $P_ W$ is the orthogonal projection onto $W$, then $x\mapsto \tilde\lambda (P_ Wx)$ is a conjugate-linear functional on $H_0$ of norm at most $\sqrt M$, because $\| P_ Wx\| \le \| x\| $. By the representation of conjugate-linear functionals in proposition 11.1.1, there is $u\in H_0$ with $\| u\| \le \sqrt M$ and

\[ \langle u,T^*f\rangle =\langle g,f\rangle \qquad (f\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*). \]

Taking complex conjugates, we get $\langle T^*f,u\rangle =\langle f,g\rangle $ for all $f\in \operatorname{Dom}T^*$. Thus the functional $f\mapsto \langle T^*f,u\rangle $ is bounded on $\operatorname{Dom}T^*$, so $u\in \operatorname{Dom}T^{**}$ and $T^{**}u=g$. Since $T^{**}=T$ by lemma 11.1.3, we conclude that $u\in \operatorname{Dom}T$ and $Tu=g$.

The solutions of $Tu=g$ form the affine subspace $u+\ker T$, which is closed because $\ker T$ is closed. If $P$ denotes the orthogonal projection onto $\ker T$, then $\| u+k\| ^2=\| u-Pu\| ^2+\| Pu+k\| ^2$ for $k\in \ker T$. Hence $u-Pu$ is the unique solution of smallest norm; it is orthogonal to $\ker T$, and its norm is at most $\| u\| \le \sqrt M$. $\square$
