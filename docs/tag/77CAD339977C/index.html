From Foundations of pluripotential theory · Introduction to Part II

In several complex variables a positive Laplacian carries too little information. It is only the trace of the complex Hessian, whereas plurisubharmonicity requires positivity in every complex direction. Complex differential forms retain this directional information in a form that is invariant under holomorphic changes of coordinates, and currents extend the calculus to singular potentials. The first task of this part (chapter 7, chapter 8) is to connect these invariant derivatives with the pointwise submean inequalities of 1.

Once plurisubharmonic functions are available, the main difficulty is nonlinear: measures cannot in general be multiplied. For locally bounded potentials, the recursive construction of Bedford and Taylor (chapter 9) defines products of their curvature currents, and continuity along decreasing sequences yields the symmetry of these products and the comparison principle. Plurisubharmonic functions are quasi-continuous with respect to the associated Monge–Ampère capacity, and the Dirichlet problem on a ball can be solved for increasingly singular measures as data. The capacity detects pluripolar sets (chapter 10); extremal functions and locality in the plurifine topology are developed there before the boundedness hypothesis is removed, because all later truncation arguments depend on them.

A second route into singularities passes through holomorphic functions (chapter 11, chapter 12, chapter 13). Solving a weighted $\bar\partial $-equation corrects the error introduced by a cutoff function, and a singular weight forces the correction to satisfy additional conditions. Extension from slices then controls integrability at a point and along a submanifold. These estimates lead to coherent multiplier ideals, to the analyticity of the level sets of Lelong numbers and to strong openness. Weighted Bergman kernels turn the same information into approximations with analytic singularities. In this way the algebraic description of a singularity stays tied to the analytic estimates that justify it.

The passage to compact Kähler manifolds (chapter 14) introduces a global constraint. Local potentials of a closed positive current differ by pluriharmonic functions on overlaps, and a cohomology class need not contain a smooth positive representative. Global potentials, positive cohomology classes and singular Hermitian metrics provide the framework in which the local theory can still be used. Approximation and extension are established with explicit curvature losses and uniform constants before they enter the global constructions; the chapter ends with a global form of Josefson's theorem on compact Hermitian manifolds, where no Kähler assumption is needed.

Non-pluripolar products (chapter 15) are obtained by truncating the local potentials and keeping the product only where all of them are finite. Plurifine locality makes the truncations compatible across truncation levels and on overlaps, and mass bounds on compact manifolds make their limit finite. Discarding the pole sets changes the nature of the mass problem: even a nonzero closed positive current can have zero non-pluripolar mass in degree one. Comparison, monotonicity and convergence in capacity must therefore be proved anew for these products; they do not follow from Stokes' formula for bounded potentials.

Envelopes (chapter 16) select potentials subject to an obstacle and to a constraint on their singularities. Their contact properties lead to mass inequalities for rooftop envelopes and to model potentials. Envelopes of obstacles with bounded metric Laplacian inherit this regularity, and on the contact set the non-pluripolar measure of a potential is then computed from the second derivatives of the obstacle. Relative capacities make integration by parts possible for potentials sharing an unbounded singularity, and the relative energy (chapter 17) links the theory of envelopes with Monge–Ampère equations and Perron segments. The identity between ceilings and singularity envelopes of [Theorem 1.2, PSWZ26], including its zero-mass case, is proved once this theory of equations is in place. The last chapter (chapter 18) develops convex duality, real Monge–Ampère measures and the geometry of ample toric varieties, as needed for the toric part of [XiaSampleBook].

Two related questions recur throughout this part: which information about a singularity is retained by holomorphic integrability, and which by nonlinear mass? The local estimates and geometric constructions prepare both questions, and the global products and envelopes show that their answers differ. The classification of singularities built on these foundations is the subject of [XiaSampleBook].
