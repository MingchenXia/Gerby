Chapter 15 · Multiplier-ideal models and partial equilibrium · Tag 987BD480177F

Complete model topologies

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory · 16.6.18 Complete model topologies

We now compare the complete metrics obtained so far and ask how they might extend to all ordinary models. On a curve, the total variation of polar curvature is a complete metric on ordinary models, ideal projection keeps the atomic part of the polar curvature, and the distance to the closure of the analytic models is exactly the diffuse polar mass, by corollary 30.12.3, proposition 30.12.6. This topology is stronger than $\tau _{\omega ,0}$ on ordinary models: proposition 30.12.9 exhibits a concentration of diffuse polar curvature that is lost in the latter topology.

Adding the smooth curve background as a further coordinate gives the complete space of theorem 30.14.1. Its analytic closure is the locus of ideal models, also over backgrounds of zero area, and metric convergence controls the normalized potentials in $L^1$ and in capacity. This construction keeps the positive-volume topology but imposes a different Cauchy condition at the zero-volume boundary.

For a smooth big background with bounded mass, theorem 30.11.3 gives a complete topology on ordinary models of positive lower volume. Its locus of ideal models is the completion of the analytic models of positive volume, by corollary 30.11.4.

At zero volume, the product constructions of the preceding subsections keep information that top-degree mass cannot detect. A fixed divisor with unit coefficient forces every ordinary representative of the induced models to have zero mass, while polar curvature on the curve factors still distinguishes these models and gives a complete metric whose analytic closure is exactly the locus of ideal models, with ideal projection as the unique nearest-point retraction. These conclusions hold on the product loci of proposition 16.6.26, proposition 16.6.43, including the nonclosed background with unbounded ambient mass in example 16.6.46.

Changing the curve areas rescales the polar distances, and a zero area removes the corresponding coordinate. The comparisons in proposition 16.6.35 quantify this change even when different sets of coordinates survive. If the curve backgrounds converge uniformly to forms of area zero, the diameters tend to zero and the potentials converge in $L^1$, uniformly over the model tuples, and the limit still contains the normalized smooth potentials of the limiting backgrounds, by corollary 16.6.38. These loci thus describe explicit degenerations beyond the positive-volume theory.

A curve quotient can also describe the entire model space. If $p:X\to C$ is a surjective holomorphic map with connected fibers, including critical fibers, and $\beta $ is a smooth real form of nonnegative area on the compact curve $C$, every potential in the background $\theta =p^*\beta $ descends to $C$. For $\dim X\geq 2$ all top-degree masses vanish, but the polar trace, normalized by the constant Gauduchon volume of the fiber divisor with its multiplicities, gives the complete curve metric on all ordinary $\theta $-models by 16.6.49. Its ideal subspace is the analytic closure, and 16.6.50 characterizes equality of the ordinary and ideal envelopes below every smooth obstacle. For an arbitrary surjective map to a compact curve, 1.22.9 supplies the finite factor used in 16.6.51, so disconnected fibers have the same complete model-space description. The polar trace must be kept upstairs: in 16.6.52, two distinct analytic models have the same trace pushforward to the original base curve.

We do not know how to extend the complete topology to general ordinary models while keeping analytic closure and compatibility across backgrounds. Since polar curvature is the coordinate that achieves this on a curve, it is natural to test polar curvature on all modifications in higher dimension. The rest of this subsection shows that this candidate fails in three ways: the first polar current misses isolated poles unless a modification exposes them; normalizing polar traces by Gauduchon forms makes the topology depend on the chosen forms; and diffuse poles of product type are invisible to polar curvature on every modification, although they lower the volume.
