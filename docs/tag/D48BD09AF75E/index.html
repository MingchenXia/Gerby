Riesz measures and pointwise representatives

From Foundations of pluripotential theory · 3.2 Riesz measures and pointwise representatives

We now compare subharmonic functions with distributions. Recall from proposition 2.2.1 that the fundamental solutions $E_ d$ of (2.9) satisfy $\Delta E_ d=\delta _0$, and that $E_ d(0)=-\infty $ for $d\geq 2$, while $E_1(0)=0$. Superpositions of translates of $E_ d$ produce subharmonic functions whose Laplacian is a prescribed positive measure. Conversely, by the Riesz decomposition (theorem 3.2.3), every locally integrable subharmonic function is locally such a superposition plus a harmonic function.

Proposition 3.2.1. Let $\nu $ be a finite positive Borel measure on $\mathbb R^ d$ with compact support. Then its fundamental potential

\[ p(x)=E_ d*\nu (x)=\int E_ d(x-y)\, \mathrm d\nu (y) \]

is subharmonic and locally integrable on $\mathbb R^ d$, and $\Delta p=\nu $ in the sense of distributions.

Proof. By proposition 1.1.2, $\nu $ is a Radon measure; let $L$ be its compact support, so that $\nu (\mathbb R^ d\setminus L)=0$ (section 1.1). For each $x$, the function $E_ d(x-\cdot )$ is bounded above on $L$, so $p(x)$ is well defined in $[-\infty ,\infty )$. Next, $E_ d$ is subharmonic on $\mathbb R^ d$. For $d\geq 2$ it is harmonic on $\mathbb R^ d\setminus \{ 0\} $; it is upper semicontinuous at the origin because $E_ d(x)\to -\infty $ as $x\to 0$, and the submean inequality at the origin holds because $E_ d(0)=-\infty $. Since subharmonicity is local, $E_ d\in \mathrm{SH}(\mathbb R^ d)$. For $d=1$, the triangle inequality gives $|x|\leq \frac12(|x+r|+|x-r|)$, which is the submean inequality for $E_1=|x|/2$.

To show that $p$ is subharmonic, we truncate the kernel. The functions $E_ d^{(m)}=\max \{ E_ d,-m\} $, $m\in \mathbb N$, are continuous and subharmonic, and they decrease to $E_ d$. Put $p_ m=E_ d^{(m)}*\nu $. For $x$ in a compact set $K$, the differences $x-y$ with $y\in L$ lie in the compact set $K-L$, on which $E_ d^{(m)}$ is uniformly continuous; hence $p_ m$ is continuous. By theorem 3.1.3, each translate $E_ d^{(m)}(\cdot -y)$ satisfies the submean inequality on every sphere. Integrating in $y$ and using Fubini's theorem for the bounded continuous integrand gives $p_ m(x)\leq M_{p_ m}(x,r)$ for all $x$ and $r>0$, so $p_ m\in \mathrm{SH}(\mathbb R^ d)$. For fixed $x$, the functions $E_ d^{(m)}(x-y)$ decrease to $E_ d(x-y)$ and are bounded above, uniformly in $m$ and $y\in L$, by the finite constant $\max \{ 0,\sup _{y\in L}E_ d(x-y)\} $. Monotone convergence, applied to the differences from this constant, gives $p_ m(x)\downarrow p(x)$. Hence $p$ is subharmonic by theorem 3.1.6.

To prove local integrability, let $K$ be compact and choose $R>0$ with $K-L\subset B(0,R)$. Since $E_ d$ is locally integrable, Tonelli's theorem gives

\[ \int _ K\int |E_ d(x-y)|\, \mathrm d\nu (y)\, \mathrm d x \leq \nu (L)\int _{B(0,R)}|E_ d(z)|\, \mathrm d z<\infty . \]

Hence $p$ is locally integrable. Finally, let $\chi $ be a test function. The preceding estimate with $K=\operatorname{Supp}\chi $ justifies the use of Fubini's theorem, and the identity $\Delta E_ d=\delta _0$, translated to the point $y$, gives

\[ \int p\Delta \chi \, \mathrm d x =\int \left(\int E_ d(x-y)\Delta \chi (x)\, \mathrm d x\right)\mathrm d\nu (y) =\int \chi (y)\, \mathrm d\nu (y). \]

This is the distributional equation $\Delta p=\nu $. $\square$

Theorem 3.2.2. Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb R^ d$ be open.

  1. Let $u\in \mathrm{SH}(\Omega )$ be not identically $-\infty $ on any connected component of $\Omega $. Then $u$ is locally integrable, and $\Delta u$ is a positive distribution, hence a positive Radon measure.

  2. Let $u\in \mathrm{SH}(\Omega )$ and $x\in \Omega $. For $0<\varepsilon <\operatorname {dist}(x,\mathbb R^ d\setminus \Omega )$ put $u*\rho _\varepsilon (x)=\int u(x-y)\rho _\varepsilon (y)\, \mathrm dy$, with the mollifier $\rho _\varepsilon $ of section 1.4. This integral and the integral of $u$ over a ball $B(x,r)$ with $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset \Omega $ are defined in $[-\infty ,\infty )$, because $u$ is bounded above on compact sets; if $u$ is locally integrable, $u*\rho _\varepsilon $ is the mollification of section 1.4. Then

    3.3
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:canonical-subharmonic} u*\rho _\varepsilon (x)\downarrow u(x)\quad (\varepsilon \downarrow 0),\qquad u(x)=\lim _{r\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B(x,r)|}\int _{B(x,r)}u(y)\, \mathrm d y. \end{equation}

    In particular, two subharmonic functions on $\Omega $ that are equal almost everywhere are equal everywhere.

  3. Let $T$ be a real distribution on $\Omega $ with $\Delta T\geq 0$. Then there is exactly one locally integrable $u\in \mathrm{SH}(\Omega )$ such that $\langle T,\chi \rangle =\int _\Omega u\chi \, \mathrm d x$ for every test function $\chi $.

Proof. (1) By proposition 3.1.5, $u$ is locally integrable. To show that $\Delta u\geq 0$, fix $\varepsilon >0$ and $x\in \Omega _\varepsilon $, with $\Omega _\varepsilon $ as in section 1.4. For sufficiently small $r>0$, all the closed balls $\overline{B(x-z,r)}$ with $|z|\leq \varepsilon $ lie in $\Omega $, so $u(x-z)\leq M_ u(x-z,r)$ for these $z$ by theorem 3.1.3. We multiply by $\rho _\varepsilon (z)$ and integrate over $z$. Since $u$ is bounded above on the compact set $\overline{B(x,\varepsilon +r)}\subset \Omega $, Fubini's theorem applies to the difference between an upper bound and $u$; as $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset \Omega _\varepsilon $, we obtain

\[ u*\rho _\varepsilon (x)\leq M_{u*\rho _\varepsilon }(x,r). \]

The function $v=u*\rho _\varepsilon $ is smooth and real on $\Omega _\varepsilon $ by proposition 1.4.3, and we have just shown that $v(x)\leq M_ v(x,r)$ for every $x\in \Omega _\varepsilon $ and every sufficiently small $r>0$. This forces $\Delta v\geq 0$ on $\Omega _\varepsilon $. Indeed, suppose that $\Delta v(x)<0$. Since $\Delta v$ is continuous, there is $R>0$ such that $\overline{B(x,R)}\subset \Omega _\varepsilon $, $\Delta v<0$ on $B(x,R)$ and $v(x)\leq M_ v(x,r)$ for $0<r<R$. By (2.4), the function $r\mapsto M_ v(x,r)$ has negative derivative on $(0,R)$, so it is strictly decreasing there. Moreover, $M_ v(x,s)\to v(x)$ as $s\downarrow 0$, because $|M_ v(x,s)-v(x)|\leq \max _{|y-x|=s}|v(y)-v(x)|$ and $v$ is continuous at $x$. Hence $M_ v(x,r)<M_ v(x,r/2)\leq \lim _{s\downarrow 0}M_ v(x,s)=v(x)$ for $0<r<R$, a contradiction.

Now let $\chi $ be a nonnegative test function. Then $\operatorname{Supp}\chi \subset \Omega _\varepsilon $ for small $\varepsilon $, and $u*\rho _\varepsilon \to u$ in $L^1$ on $\operatorname{Supp}\chi $ by lemma 1.4.1. Hence

\[ \langle \Delta u,\chi \rangle =\int u\Delta \chi \, \mathrm d x=\lim _{\varepsilon \downarrow 0}\int (u*\rho _\varepsilon )\Delta \chi \, \mathrm d x=\lim _{\varepsilon \downarrow 0}\int \Delta (u*\rho _\varepsilon )\chi \, \mathrm d x\geq 0. \]

By theorem 1.4.5, the positive distribution $\Delta u$ is a positive Radon measure.

(2) Fix $x\in \Omega $ and $0<\varepsilon <\operatorname {dist}(x,\mathbb R^ d\setminus \Omega )$, let $C$ be a real upper bound for $u$ on $\overline{B(x,\varepsilon )}$, and let $e_1$ be the first coordinate unit vector. Since $\rho $ is radial, $\rho _\varepsilon (r\omega )=\varepsilon ^{-d}\rho ((r/\varepsilon )e_1)$ for $\omega \in S^{d-1}$, and the spherical mean of $\omega \mapsto u(x-r\omega )$ equals $M_ u(x,r)$ by the symmetry $\omega \mapsto -\omega $. Integration in polar coordinates (2.1), applied to the nonnegative function $(C-u(x-y))\rho _\varepsilon (y)$ on $B(0,\varepsilon )$, and the substitution $r=\varepsilon t$ therefore give, with $w(t)=s_{d-1}\rho (te_1)t^{d-1}$,

\[ \int _{B(0,\varepsilon )}\bigl (C-u(x-y)\bigr )\rho _\varepsilon (y)\, \mathrm d y=\int _0^1\bigl (C-M_ u(x,\varepsilon t)\bigr )\, w(t)\, \mathrm d t. \]

The same computation, applied to $\rho _\varepsilon $ alone, shows that $\int _0^1w\, \mathrm dt=\int \rho _\varepsilon \, \mathrm dy=1$, so $w(t)\, \mathrm dt$ is a probability measure on $(0,1)$, and we obtain

\[ u*\rho _\varepsilon (x)=\int _0^1M_ u(x,\varepsilon t)\, w(t)\, \mathrm d t\in [-\infty ,\infty ). \]

By theorem 3.1.3, $M_ u(x,\varepsilon t)$ increases with $\varepsilon $ and is at least $u(x)$. Hence $u*\rho _\varepsilon (x)$ decreases as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ and is at least $u(x)$. On the other hand, $u*\rho _\varepsilon (x)\leq \sup _{B(x,\varepsilon )}u$, and the upper limit of the right-hand side as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ is at most $u(x)$ by upper semicontinuity, also when $u(x)=-\infty $. Thus $u*\rho _\varepsilon (x)\downarrow u(x)$. The ball averages are treated in the same way: by (3.2), the average of $u$ over $B(x,r)$ is the average of $M_ u(x,rt)$, $0<t<1$, with respect to the probability measure $d\, t^{d-1}\, \mathrm d t$, so it lies between $u(x)$ and $\sup _{B(x,r)}u$ and tends to $u(x)$. This proves (3.3). The ball averages in (3.3) depend only on the almost-everywhere class of $u$; hence two subharmonic functions that agree almost everywhere agree everywhere.

(3) Since a distribution determines a locally integrable function representing it up to null sets (proposition 1.4.3), (2) shows that $T$ has at most one locally integrable subharmonic representative. It remains to construct one; we do this on balls and then glue.

By theorem 1.4.5, $\mu =\Delta T$ is a positive Radon measure. Let $B$ be a ball with $\overline B\subset \Omega $, and choose a nonnegative cutoff $\eta \in C_ c^\infty (\Omega )$ equal to one on an open set $U\supset \overline B$ (lemma 1.4.1). The measure $\eta \mu $, extended by zero to $\mathbb R^ d$, is finite and has compact support, so by proposition 3.2.1 its fundamental potential $p=E_ d*(\eta \mu )$ is subharmonic and locally integrable, and $\Delta p=\eta \mu =\mu $ on $U$. Thus $\Delta (T-p)=0$ on $U$, and by Weyl's lemma (theorem 2.1.4) the real distribution $T-p$ is represented on $U$ by a smooth harmonic function $h$; since $\operatorname{Re}h$ also represents $T-p$, the uniqueness in theorem 2.1.4 shows that $h=\operatorname{Re}h$ is real. Then $u_ B=p+h$ is a locally integrable subharmonic function on $B$ representing $T$ there.

If $B'$ is another such ball, $u_ B$ and $u_{B'}$ both represent $T$ on $B\cap B'$, so they agree almost everywhere there (proposition 1.4.3), and hence everywhere on $B\cap B'$ by (2). Hence there is a function $u$ on $\Omega $ with $u=u_ B$ on every ball $B$ with $\overline B\subset \Omega $. It is subharmonic, because subharmonicity is local, and it is locally integrable.

To see that $u$ represents $T$ on all of $\Omega $, let $T_\varepsilon (x)=\langle T,\rho _\varepsilon (x-\cdot )\rangle $ be the mollification of proposition 1.4.3. If $x\in \Omega _\varepsilon $, then $\overline{B(x,\varepsilon ')}\subset \Omega $ for some $\varepsilon '>\varepsilon $, and the test function $\rho _\varepsilon (x-\cdot )$ is supported in the ball $B=B(x,\varepsilon ')$, on which $u=u_ B$ represents $T$; hence $T_\varepsilon (x)=u*\rho _\varepsilon (x)$. For every test function $\chi $, proposition 1.4.3, lemma 1.4.1 now give

\[ \langle T,\chi \rangle =\lim _{\varepsilon \downarrow 0}\int T_\varepsilon \chi \, \mathrm d x=\lim _{\varepsilon \downarrow 0}\int (u*\rho _\varepsilon )\chi \, \mathrm d x=\int u\chi \, \mathrm d x. \]
$\square$

The function $u$ of theorem 3.2.2(3) is called the canonical representative of $T$, and every locally integrable subharmonic function is the canonical representative of the distribution it defines. Subharmonicity is a property of a pointwise function, not of its almost-everywhere class. For instance, if we raise the value of a subharmonic function $u$ at a single point $x_0$, the spherical means $M_ u(x_0,r)$ do not change and still tend to $u(x_0)$ as $r\downarrow 0$ (theorem 3.1.3), so the submean inequality fails at $x_0$. On the other hand, by theorem 3.2.2(2), the almost-everywhere class of a subharmonic function determines it at every point, even without integrability assumptions.

Suppose that locally integrable subharmonic functions $u_ j$ on $\Omega $ converge in the sense of distributions to $T\in \mathcal D'(\Omega )$. Then $T$ is real as a limit of real distributions. Since each $u_ j$ is locally integrable, it is not identically $-\infty $ on any connected component of $\Omega $, so $\Delta u_ j\geq 0$ by theorem 3.2.2(1), and hence $\langle \Delta T,\chi \rangle =\lim _ j\langle \Delta u_ j,\chi \rangle \geq 0$ for every nonnegative test function $\chi $. Therefore $T$ has a canonical representative by theorem 3.2.2(3).

For a locally integrable $u\in \mathrm{SH}(\Omega )$, the positive Radon measure $\mu _ u=\Delta u$ is called the Riesz measure of $u$. Some texts on potential theory in the plane define the Riesz measure as $(2\pi )^{-1}\Delta u$; with our normalization, $\mu _{\log |z|}=2\pi \delta _0$ on $\mathbb C=\mathbb R^2$, since $\log |z|=2\pi E_2(z)$.

Theorem 3.2.3 (Riesz decomposition). Let $u\in \mathrm{SH}(\Omega )$ be locally integrable. Let $\eta $ be a bounded nonnegative Borel function on $\Omega $ which vanishes outside a compact subset of $\Omega $ and equals one on an open set $V\subset \Omega $; for instance, $\eta =\mathbf1_ V$ for an open set $V\Subset \Omega $, or $\eta \in C_ c^\infty (\Omega )$ with values in $[0,1]$ and $\eta =1$ on $V$. Let $\eta \mu _ u$ denote the measure $E\mapsto \int _{E\cap \Omega }\eta \, \mathrm d\mu _ u$ on $\mathbb R^ d$. Then there is a smooth real harmonic function $h$ on $V$ such that

\[ u=E_ d*(\eta \mu _ u)+h\qquad \text{on }V. \]

This equality holds at every point of $V$, including the points where $u=-\infty $.

Proof. If $\eta $ vanishes outside the compact set $C\subset \Omega $, then $\eta \mu _ u$ is a positive Borel measure on $\mathbb R^ d$ with support in $C$ and total mass at most $\sup \eta \cdot \mu _ u(C)<\infty $. Let $p=E_ d*(\eta \mu _ u)$. By proposition 3.2.1, $p$ is subharmonic and locally integrable on $\mathbb R^ d$, and $\Delta p=\eta \mu _ u$. If $\chi $ is a test function with $\operatorname{Supp}\chi \subset V$, then $\int \chi \eta \, \mathrm d\mu _ u=\int \chi \, \mathrm d\mu _ u$ because $\eta =1$ on $V$; hence $\Delta p=\Delta u$ on $V$. By Weyl's lemma (theorem 2.1.4), the real distribution $u-p$ is represented on $V$ by a smooth harmonic function $h$; since $\operatorname{Re}h$ also represents $u-p$, the uniqueness in theorem 2.1.4 shows that $h=\operatorname{Re}h$ is real. Then $p+h$ is a locally integrable subharmonic function on $V$ that agrees with $u$ almost everywhere on $V$ (proposition 1.4.3), and theorem 3.2.2(2), applied on $V$, gives $u=p+h$ at every point of $V$. $\square$

Example 3.2.4. Let $I\subset \mathbb R$ be an open interval. Every $u\in \mathrm{SH}(I)$ other than the constant $-\infty $ is a finite, continuous, convex function. Indeed, $u$ is locally integrable by proposition 3.1.5. Given $x\in I$, choose an open interval $V\ni x$ with $\overline V\subset I$ and apply theorem 3.2.3 with $\eta =\mathbf1_ V$. On $V$ we have $u=E_1*(\eta \mu _ u)+h$ with $h$ affine, and the potential $E_1*(\eta \mu _ u)(\xi )=\frac12\int |\xi -y|\, \eta (y)\, \mathrm d\mu _ u(y)$ is finite and Lipschitz in $\xi $, since $\bigl ||\xi -y|-|\xi '-y|\bigr |\leq |\xi -\xi '|$ and $\eta \mu _ u$ is a finite measure. Hence $u$ is finite and continuous.

In dimension one the submean inequality reads $u(x)\leq \frac12(u(x-r)+u(x+r))$, and by theorem 3.1.3 it holds whenever $[x-r,x+r]\subset I$. Thus $u$ satisfies the midpoint inequality $u\bigl ((a+b)/2\bigr )\leq \bigl (u(a)+u(b)\bigr )/2$ for all $a,b\in I$. By induction on $n$, it follows that $u((1-\lambda )a+\lambda b)\leq (1-\lambda )u(a)+\lambda u(b)$ for every dyadic number $\lambda =k2^{-n}\in [0,1]$, because a dyadic number of order $n+1$ is the midpoint of two adjacent dyadic numbers of order $n$. Since $u$ is continuous and the dyadic numbers are dense in $[0,1]$, the same inequality holds for every $\lambda \in [0,1]$.

Conversely, a finite convex function $f$ on $I$ is continuous. For $p<q$ in $I$ let $\sigma (p,q)=(f(q)-f(p))/(q-p)$ be the slope of the chord of $f$ over $[p,q]$. If $p<q<r$ lie in $I$, then $q=(1-\lambda )p+\lambda r$ with $\lambda =(q-p)/(r-p)\in (0,1)$, and convexity gives $f(q)\leq (1-\lambda )f(p)+\lambda f(r)$, that is, $f(q)-f(p)\leq \lambda (f(r)-f(p))$ and $f(r)-f(q)\geq (1-\lambda )(f(r)-f(p))$. Dividing by $q-p=\lambda (r-p)$ and by $r-q=(1-\lambda )(r-p)$, respectively, we obtain

\[ \sigma (p,q)\leq \sigma (p,r)\leq \sigma (q,r). \]

Hence $\sigma (p,q)$ increases with $q$ for fixed $p$, and with $p$ for fixed $q$. Now let $[a,b]$ be a compact subinterval of $I$, choose $a',b'\in I$ with $a'<a$ and $b<b'$, and let $a\leq s<t\leq b$. Applying these monotonicity properties and the displayed inequality to the triples $(a',s,t)$ and $(s,t,b')$, we find

\[ \sigma (a',a)\leq \sigma (a',s)\leq \sigma (s,t)\leq \sigma (t,b')\leq \sigma (b,b'), \]

so $|f(t)-f(s)|\leq \max \{ |\sigma (a',a)|,|\sigma (b,b')|\} \, (t-s)$. Thus $f$ is Lipschitz on every compact subinterval of $I$, and in particular continuous. Since $f$ satisfies the midpoint inequality, which is the submean inequality in dimension one, it is subharmonic. Hence the subharmonic functions on $I$ other than $-\infty $ are exactly the finite convex functions.

Next let $D\subset \mathbb C=\mathbb R^2$ be a domain, and let $f$ be holomorphic on $D$ and not identically zero. Then $\log |f|$ is subharmonic, and

\[ \Delta \log |f|=2\pi \sum _{f(a)=0}\operatorname{ord}_ a(f)\, \delta _ a, \]

where $\operatorname{ord}_ a(f)$ is the order of the zero of $f$ at $a$. Indeed, the zeros of $f$ form a locally finite subset of $D$, and away from them $\log |f|$ is locally the real part of a holomorphic logarithm of $f$, hence harmonic. Near a zero $a$ of order $m$ we can write $f(z)=(z-a)^ mg(z)$ with $g$ holomorphic and nonvanishing, so that $\log |f(z)|=2\pi mE_2(z-a)+\log |g(z)|$, where $\log |g|$ is harmonic and $E_2(\cdot -a)$, the fundamental potential of $\delta _ a$, is subharmonic with $\Delta E_2(\cdot -a)=\delta _ a$ (proposition 3.2.1). Hence $\log |f|$ is subharmonic near every point, and therefore on $D$. Moreover, the right-hand side of the displayed formula is a positive Radon measure, as is $\Delta \log |f|$ (theorem 3.2.2(1)). The two measures agree on a neighborhood of every point of $D$ (proposition 1.4.3), and hence on $D$: cover $D$ by countably many such neighborhoods $V_ k$, and decompose a Borel set $E\subset D$ into the disjoint sets $E\cap V_ k\setminus (V_1\cup \dots \cup V_{k-1})$. For $\log |f|^2=2\log |f|$ the coefficient $2\pi $ becomes $4\pi $.
