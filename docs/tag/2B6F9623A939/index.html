Relative extremal functions and capacity

From Foundations of pluripotential theory · 4.3 Relative extremal functions and capacity

The Newtonian and logarithmic capacities of section 4.1, which we call kernel capacities, are defined by minimizing energy over measures. We now define a second capacity by maximizing over subharmonic functions. Its values depend on a surrounding ball, but it is increasing, continuous along decreasing sequences and strongly subadditive (proposition 4.3.4). These properties allow us to extend it to arbitrary sets (theorem 4.3.5), and we shall show that its null sets are exactly the polar sets.

From now on, $B=B(0,R)$ is an open ball centered at the origin; the results hold for every radius $R>0$, and in some proofs we take $R$ large. For a set $A\Subset B$, we say that a function $v$ is admissible for $A$ if $v\in \mathrm{SH}(B)$, $v\leq 0$ on $B$ and $v\leq -1$ on $A$. The relative extremal function of $A$ and its regularization are

\[ f_ A=\sup \{ v:v\text{ is admissible for }A\} ,\qquad u_ A=f_ A^*. \]

The constant $-1$ is admissible, so $-1\leq f_ A\leq u_ A\leq 0$, and $u_ A$ is subharmonic by theorem 3.4.2. The condition $v\leq -1$ is imposed at every point of $A$, but the regularization $u_ A$ may exceed $-1$ at some points of $A$. For instance, let $A=\{ 0\} $. Since $E_ d$ is a radial function that increases with $|x|$, the function $\varepsilon (E_ d-c_ R)$, where $c_ R$ is the value of $E_ d$ on $\partial B$, is nonpositive on $B$ for every $\varepsilon >0$. It is subharmonic by proposition 3.2.1 and equals $-\infty $ at the origin, so it is admissible for $A$. Letting $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, we obtain $f_ A=0$ on $B\setminus \{ 0\} $, and therefore $u_ A(0)=0$.

Extremal functions will be represented as Green potentials. On a ball, the Green function $G_ B$ of theorem 2.5.2 is given by an explicit formula, from which we read off its symmetry and its smoothness near $\partial B$.

Lemma 4.3.1. The Green function of $B$ is symmetric: $G_ B(x,y)=G_ B(y,x)$ for $x,y\in B$. For $x,y\in B$ with $y\neq 0$, put $y^*=R^2y/|y|^2$. Then for $d\geq 3$,

\[ G_ B(x,y)=\Gamma _ d(x-y)-\left(\frac R{|y|}\right)^{d-2}\Gamma _ d(x-y^*), \]

and for $d=2$,

\[ G_ B(x,y)=\frac1{2\pi }\log \frac{|y|\, |x-y^*|}{R|x-y|}. \]

For $y=0$, $G_ B(x,0)=\Gamma _ d(x)-\Gamma _ d(Re_1)$ if $d\geq 3$ and $G_ B(x,0)=(2\pi )^{-1}\log (R/|x|)$ if $d=2$; here $e_1$ is the first coordinate unit vector. Moreover, for $0<r<R$, these formulas extend the difference $G_ B(x,y)-\Gamma _ d(x-y)$ to a smooth function of $(x,y)$ on the set where $|y|\leq r$ and $|x|<R^2/r$; this range of $x$ contains a neighborhood of $\overline B$.

Proof. Fix $y\in B\setminus \{ 0\} $. Since $|y^*|=R^2/|y|>R$, the function $x\mapsto \Gamma _ d(x-y^*)$ is harmonic on a neighborhood of $\overline B$. Expanding the square gives

4.4
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ball-green-symmetric-quantity} |y|^2|x-y^*|^2=R^4-2R^2x\mathbin {\cdot }y+|x|^2|y|^2, \end{equation}

and therefore $|y|^2|x-y^*|^2-R^2|x-y|^2=(R^2-|x|^2)(R^2-|y|^2)$. Each displayed expression, as a function of $x$, is the sum of $\Gamma _ d(x-y)$ and a function harmonic near $\overline B$. By proposition 2.2.1, it is therefore harmonic on $B\setminus \{ y\} $ and locally integrable on $B$, with distributional Laplacian $-\delta _ y$ on $B$. Moreover, the last identity shows that $|y|\, |x-y^*|>R|x-y|$ on $B$, with equality on $\partial B$. Hence the expression is positive on $B\setminus \{ y\} $ and tends to zero at every point of $\partial B$. The ball is a regular domain by theorem 2.4.6, and the uniqueness assertion of theorem 2.5.2 identifies the displayed expression with $G_ B(y,x)$ for $x\neq y$. The same argument applies to $y=0$, where the stated expressions differ from $\Gamma _ d(x)$ by constants.

Let $Q(x,y)$ denote the right side of (4.4); it is symmetric in $x$ and $y$, and $Q(x,0)=R^4$. The formulas just proved can be written, for all $x,y\in B$ including $y=0$, as

\[ \begin{aligned} G_ B(y,x)& =\Gamma _ d(x-y)-\frac{R^{d-2}Q(x,y)^{(2-d)/2}}{(d-2)s_{d-1}}& & (d\geq 3),\\ G_ B(y,x)& =\Gamma _2(x-y)+\frac1{4\pi }\log \frac{Q(x,y)}{R^2}& & (d=2). \end{aligned} \]

Both right sides are symmetric in $x$ and $y$, so $G_ B(y,x)=G_ B(x,y)$; this also proves the formulas in the order stated. Finally, $Q(x,y)\geq (R^2-|x||y|)^2>0$ when $|y|\leq r$ and $|x|<R^2/r$, so the correction terms $G_ B(x,y)-\Gamma _ d(x-y)$ in the last display are smooth functions of $(x,y)$ there. $\square$

For a finite positive measure $\lambda $ with compact support in $B$, its Green potential is

\[ G_ B\lambda (x)=\int G_ B(x,y)\, \mathrm d\lambda (y),\qquad x\in B. \]

Proposition 4.3.2. Let $K\subset B$ be compact.

  1. The function $f_ K$ is the supremum of the continuous admissible functions for $K$; it is lower semicontinuous, equals $-1$ on $K$, and equals $u_ K$ on $B\setminus K$. The function $u_ K$ is harmonic on $B\setminus K$ and tends to zero at $\partial B$.

  2. Let $\lambda $ be a finite positive measure with compact support in $B$, and put $u=-G_ B\lambda $. Then $u=E_ d*\lambda -H_ B\bigl ((E_ d*\lambda )|_{\partial B}\bigr )$ on $B$, where $H_ B$ is the Poisson integral of theorem 2.3.1; in particular, $u$ is subharmonic on $B$ with Riesz measure $\lambda $. If $\operatorname{Supp}\lambda \subset B(0,r)$ with $0<r<R$, then the restriction of $u$ to the annulus $r<|x|<R$ extends to a harmonic function on the annulus $r<|x|<R^2/r$ that vanishes on $\partial B$. Moreover,

    \[ \lambda (B)=\int _{\partial B}\partial _ nu\, \mathrm d S, \]

    where $\partial _ n$ is the derivative along the outward unit normal of $\partial B$. If $\lambda '$ is another such measure and $-G_ B\lambda \leq -G_ B\lambda '$ on $B$, then $\lambda (B)\geq \lambda '(B)$. Conversely, if $v$ is a locally integrable subharmonic function on $B$ whose Riesz measure $\lambda $ has compact support in $B$, and if $v(x)\to 0$ as $x$ tends to any point of $\partial B$, then $v=-G_ B\lambda $.

  3. The Riesz measure $\lambda _ K=\Delta u_ K$ is a finite positive measure supported on $K$, and $u_ K=-G_ B\lambda _ K$.

The number $C_ B(K)=\lambda _ K(K)$ is called the relative capacity of $K$ with respect to $B$; by (2) and (3), $C_ B(K)=\int _{\partial B}\partial _ nu_ K\, \mathrm dS$.

Proof. (1) Since $K$ is a compact subset of $B$, there is $c>0$ such that $q(x)=c(|x|^2-R^2)\leq -1$ on $K$. The function $q$ is smooth and subharmonic, since $\Delta q=2dc>0$, and it is nonpositive on $B$; hence it is admissible, and $q\leq f_ K\leq u_ K\leq 0$. In particular $f_ K$ and $u_ K$ tend to zero at $\partial B$.

We show that $f_ K$ is the supremum of the continuous admissible functions. Let $v$ be admissible. We first extend $\max \{ v,q\} $ to a subharmonic function on $\mathbb R^ d$: put $w=\max \{ v,q\} $ on $B$ and $w=q$ on $\mathbb R^ d\setminus B$. At $\zeta \in \partial B$ we have $w(\zeta )=0$ and $\varlimsup _{x\to \zeta }w(x)\leq 0$, because $v\leq 0$ on $B$ and $q$ is continuous with $q(\zeta )=0$; so $w$ is upper semicontinuous. We verify the comparison principle of theorem 3.1.3 for $w$ on an arbitrary ball $D$. Let $h$ be harmonic on $D$ and continuous on $\overline D$, with $\varlimsup _{D\ni x\to \zeta }w(x)\leq h(\zeta )$ for $\zeta \in \partial D$. Since $w\geq q$ and $q$ is continuous, $q\leq h$ on $\partial D$, hence on $D$ by the comparison principle for $q$; in particular $w=q\leq h$ on $D\setminus B$. On the bounded open set $D\cap B$, the function $v-h$ has nonpositive boundary upper limits: at points of $\partial D\cap B$ because $v\leq w$, and at points of $\partial B$ because $\varlimsup v\leq 0=q\leq h$ there. By the comparison principle on bounded open sets (the remark after theorem 3.1.3), $v\leq h$ on $D\cap B$. Thus $w\leq h$ on $D$, and the converse part of theorem 3.1.3 shows that $w$ is subharmonic on $\mathbb R^ d$.

Since $w\geq q$, it is locally integrable. Its mollifications $w*\rho _\varepsilon $ are smooth and satisfy $\Delta (w*\rho _\varepsilon )=(\Delta w)*\rho _\varepsilon \geq 0$, so they are subharmonic, and they decrease to $w$ as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ by (3.3). Fix $\delta >0$. On $B$ we have $w*\rho _\varepsilon \leq \sup _{B(0,R+\varepsilon )}w\leq c((R+\varepsilon )^2-R^2)$, because $w\leq 0$ on $B$ and $w=q$ outside $B$; hence $w*\rho _\varepsilon -\delta \leq 0$ on $B$ for small $\varepsilon $. On $K$ we have $w\leq -1$. Since the continuous functions $w*\rho _\varepsilon $ decrease to $w$, the open sets $\{ w*\rho _\varepsilon <-1+\delta \} $ increase and cover the compact set $K$, so $w*\rho _\varepsilon <-1+\delta $ on $K$ for small $\varepsilon $. For such $\varepsilon $ the function $w*\rho _\varepsilon -\delta $ is continuous and admissible, and it is at least $w-\delta \geq v-\delta $. Since $v$ and $\delta $ are arbitrary, $f_ K$ is the supremum of the continuous admissible functions; in particular $f_ K$ is lower semicontinuous. Every admissible function is at most $-1$ on $K$, so $f_ K=-1$ on $K$.

Let $D$ be an open ball with $\overline D\subset B\setminus K$; we show that $f_ K=u_ K$ on $D$ and that $u_ K$ is harmonic there. For a continuous admissible function $v$, let $v^ D$ be its harmonic lifting on $D$, which equals the Poisson integral of $v|_{\partial D}$ on $D$ and $v$ elsewhere. As noted in section 2.4, the continuous subsolutions defined there are exactly the real-valued continuous subharmonic functions. Hence lemma 2.4.1 shows that $v^ D$ is continuous and subharmonic and that $v^ D\geq v$. It is admissible: $v^ D\leq 0$ because the Poisson integral preserves order, and $v^ D=v$ on $K$.

Apply Choquet's lemma (lemma 3.4.1) to the family of continuous admissible functions, whose supremum is $f_ K$: there are continuous admissible functions $v_1,v_2,\ldots $ with $(\sup _ jv_ j)^*=u_ K$. The functions $w_ j=\max \{ -1,v_1,\ldots ,v_ j\} $ are continuous, admissible and increasing, and so are the functions $w_ j^ D$. On $D$, the harmonic functions $w_ j^ D$ take values in $[-1,0]$ and increase, so by corollary 2.3.3 they converge locally uniformly to a harmonic function $H$. On the one hand, each $w_ j^ D$ is admissible, so $H\leq f_ K\leq u_ K$ on $D$. On the other hand, $H\geq \sup _ jw_ j$. The upper semicontinuous regularization of $\sup _ jw_ j=\max \{ -1,\sup _ jv_ j\} $ is $\max \{ -1,u_ K\} =u_ K$, so $\sup _ jw_ j=u_ K$ almost everywhere by theorem 3.4.2. Hence $H=u_ K$ almost everywhere on $D$; since both functions are subharmonic and locally integrable, $H=u_ K$ on $D$ by theorem 3.2.2. Therefore $f_ K=u_ K=H$ on $D$. As $D$ is arbitrary, $f_ K=u_ K$ on $B\setminus K$, and $u_ K$ is harmonic there.

(2) Fix $0<r<R$ with $\operatorname{Supp}\lambda \subset B(0,r)$. By the definition of $G_ B$ in theorem 2.5.2 and the symmetry in lemma 4.3.1,

\[ u(x)=-\int G_ B(y,x)\, \mathrm d\lambda (y)=E_ d*\lambda (x)-\int H_ B\bigl (E_ d(\cdot -y)|_{\partial B}\bigr )(x)\, \mathrm d\lambda (y). \]

By Fubini's theorem the last integral is the Poisson integral of the continuous function $\zeta \mapsto E_ d*\lambda (\zeta )$ on $\partial B$, so it is harmonic on $B$. Hence $u$ is subharmonic with Riesz measure $\lambda $, by proposition 3.2.1. By lemma 4.3.1, for $y\in \operatorname{Supp}\lambda $ the formula given there for $G_ B(x,y)$ defines a harmonic function of $x$ on the annulus $r<|x|<R^2/r$, which vanishes for $|x|=R$. Since this function is jointly smooth in $(x,y)$, its derivatives in $x$ are bounded uniformly in $y\in \operatorname{Supp}\lambda $ on compact subsets of the annulus. Differentiating under the integral sign, we see that $-\int G_ B(x,y)\, \mathrm d\lambda (y)$ defines a harmonic function on this annulus that extends $u$ across $\partial B$ and vanishes on $\partial B$; we denote the extension again by $u$.

Choose $r<r'<R$ and $\chi \in C_ c^\infty (B)$ with $\chi =1$ on a neighborhood of $\overline{B(0,r')}$. Then $\lambda (B)=\int \chi \, \mathrm d\lambda =\int _ Bu\Delta \chi \, \mathrm d x$. The integrand vanishes outside the annulus $r'<|x|<R$, near whose closure $u$ is smooth and harmonic. The divergence identity (2.3) on this annulus, applied to the field $u\nabla \chi -\chi \nabla u$, whose divergence there is $u\Delta \chi $, gives

\[ \int _ Bu\Delta \chi \, \mathrm d x=\int _{|x|=r'}\partial _ ru\, \mathrm d S, \]

because $\chi $ and its derivatives vanish near $|x|=R$, while $\chi =1$ near $|x|=r'$; here $\partial _ r$ is the radial derivative. Applying the divergence identity on the same annulus to $\nabla u$, whose divergence vanishes there, we see that this integral equals $\int _{\partial B}\partial _ nu\, \mathrm dS$. This proves the flux identity.

For the comparison of masses, let $u'=-G_ B\lambda '$ be a second function as above with $u\leq u'$ on $B$. Then $u-u'\leq 0$ on $B$ and $u-u'=0$ on $\partial B$, and since $u-u'$ is smooth near $\partial B$, its derivative at a point of $\partial B$ along the outward normal is the limit of difference quotients $((u-u')(\zeta -sn)-0)/(-s)\geq 0$ as $s\downarrow 0$. Hence $\partial _ nu\geq \partial _ nu'$ on $\partial B$, and integration gives $\lambda (B)\geq \lambda '(B)$.

For the converse, let $v$ and $\lambda $ be as stated, extend $\lambda $ by zero to $\mathbb R^ d$, and put $p=E_ d*\lambda $. By proposition 3.2.1, $\Delta (v-p)=0$ on $B$ in the sense of distributions, so by theorem 2.1.4 the locally integrable function $v-p$ agrees almost everywhere on $B$ with a harmonic function $h$. Then $p+h$ is a locally integrable subharmonic function on $B$ that agrees with $v$ almost everywhere, hence everywhere by theorem 3.2.2. The function $p$ is finite and continuous on the neighborhood $\mathbb R^ d\setminus \operatorname{Supp}\lambda $ of $\partial B$: if $x\notin \operatorname{Supp}\lambda $, then $E_ d(w-y)\to E_ d(x-y)$ uniformly in $y\in \operatorname{Supp}\lambda $ as $w\to x$, because $E_ d$ is uniformly continuous on compact subsets of $\mathbb R^ d\setminus \{ 0\} $. As $v$ tends to zero at $\partial B$, the harmonic function $h=v-p$ extends continuously to $\overline B$ with boundary values $-p|_{\partial B}$, and the uniqueness in theorem 2.3.1 gives $h=-H_ B(p|_{\partial B})$. By the formula for $-G_ B\lambda $ proved at the beginning of (2), $v=p-H_ B(p|_{\partial B})=-G_ B\lambda $.

(3) By (1), $u_ K$ is harmonic on $B\setminus K$, so its Riesz measure $\lambda _ K$, a positive Radon measure on $B$ by theorem 3.2.2, is supported on the compact set $K$; in particular it is finite. Moreover $-1\leq u_ K\leq 0$, so $u_ K$ is locally integrable, and $u_ K$ tends to zero at $\partial B$ by (1). The converse in (2) therefore gives $u_ K=-G_ B\lambda _ K$. The flux formula for $C_ B(K)$ is now (2) with $\lambda =\lambda _ K$. $\square$

Theorem 4.3.3. For a compact set $K\subset \mathbb R^ d$, the following conditions are equivalent:

  1. $K$ is not polar;

  2. $K$ carries a nonzero positive measure with finite energy;

  3. $K$ carries a nonzero positive measure $\mu $ such that $E_ d*\mu $ is finite and continuous on $\mathbb R^ d$.

If $K\subset B$, they are also equivalent to $C_ B(K)>0$. Moreover, every finite positive measure with compact support and finite energy gives zero mass to polar sets: every polar set is contained in a Borel set of measure zero.

Proof. Conditions (1)–(3) do not involve $B$, so for their equivalence we may enlarge $R$ and assume that $K\subset B$. We first show, for every ball $B=B(0,R)$ containing $K$, that $K$ is polar if and only if $C_ B(K)=0$. Since $u_ K=-G_ B\lambda _ K$ and $C_ B(K)=\lambda _ K(K)$ (proposition 4.3.2), we have $C_ B(K)=0$ if and only if $u_ K=0$. Suppose $u_ K=0$. Then $f_ K=0$ almost everywhere by theorem 3.4.2. Choose a point $x\in B$ with $f_ K(x)=0$ and admissible functions $v_ j$ with $v_ j(x)>-2^{-j}$. The partial sums $\sum _{j\leq N}v_ j$ are subharmonic and decrease with $N$, and their values at $x$ stay above $-1$. By theorem 3.1.6, the limit $\sum _ jv_ j$ is subharmonic on $B$ and not identically $-\infty $. It equals $-\infty $ on $K$, since every $v_ j$ is at most $-1$ there. Thus $K$ is polar.

Conversely, if $K$ is polar, proposition 4.2.3 gives a function $E_ d*\nu $, not identically $-\infty $, that equals $-\infty $ on $K$; subtracting a constant, we may assume it is negative on $B$. All its positive multiples are then admissible, so $f_ K=0$ wherever $E_ d*\nu >-\infty $, that is, almost everywhere. Hence $u_ K=0$ almost everywhere, and therefore everywhere by theorem 3.2.2.

Suppose that $K$ is not polar, so that $\lambda _ K\neq 0$. By parts (2) and (3) of proposition 4.3.2, $u_ K=E_ d*\lambda _ K+h$ with the harmonic function $h=-H_ B\bigl ((E_ d*\lambda _ K)|_{\partial B}\bigr )$ on $B$. Since $u_ K\geq -1$, it follows that $E_ d*\lambda _ K\geq -1-h>-\infty $ on $K$. Theorem 4.2.2 writes $\lambda _ K$ as a sum of measures with finite continuous potentials, and one of them, $\mu $, is nonzero; so (3) holds. A measure $\mu $ as in (3) has finite energy, because its potential $U^\mu $ is bounded on the compact support of $\mu $; so (3) implies (2).

Next, let $\mu $ be a finite positive measure with compact support such that $E_ d*\mu $ is finite and continuous, and let $\nu $ be any finite positive measure with compact support. We show that $\mu $ gives measure zero to the Borel set $\{ E_ d*\nu =-\infty \} $; by proposition 4.2.3, every bounded polar set lies in a set of this form. By the reciprocity law (4.1), $\int (E_ d*\nu )\, \mathrm d\mu =\int (E_ d*\mu )\, \mathrm d\nu $. The right side is finite because $E_ d*\mu $ is continuous, hence so is the left side, and therefore $E_ d*\nu >-\infty $ $\mu $-almost everywhere.

Now let $\mu $ be a finite positive measure with compact support and finite energy. Then $U^\mu <\infty $ $\mu $-almost everywhere, that is, $E_ d*\mu $ is finite $\mu $-almost everywhere, and theorem 4.2.2 writes $\mu $ as a sum of measures with finite continuous potentials. If $A$ is a bounded polar set and $\nu $ is chosen for $A$ as in proposition 4.2.3, the previous paragraph shows that each of these measures, and hence $\mu $, gives measure zero to the Borel set $\{ E_ d*\nu =-\infty \} $, which contains $A$. An arbitrary polar set is a countable union of bounded polar sets, so it is contained in a countable union of Borel sets of $\mu $-measure zero; this proves the final assertion. In particular, a nonzero measure with finite energy cannot be carried by a polar compact set, so (2) implies (1). $\square$

Proposition 4.3.4. The relative capacity of compact subsets of $B$ has the following properties. It is increasing: $C_ B(K)\leq C_ B(L)$ for compact sets $K\subset L\subset B$. It is continuous along decreasing sequences: if compact sets $K_ j\subset B$ decrease to $K$, then $C_ B(K_ j)\to C_ B(K)$. It is strongly subadditive: for compact sets $K,L\subset B$,

\[ C_ B(K\cup L)+C_ B(K\cap L)\leq C_ B(K)+C_ B(L). \]

Proof. If $K\subset L$, every function admissible for $L$ is admissible for $K$, so $u_ L\leq u_ K$. By proposition 4.3.2, $u_ L=-G_ B\lambda _ L$ and $u_ K=-G_ B\lambda _ K$, and part (2) of that proposition gives $C_ B(L)\geq C_ B(K)$.

Let $K_ j$ be compact sets decreasing to $K$. Their extremal functions increase with $j$ and are bounded above by $f_ K$. Let $v$ be admissible for $K$ and $\varepsilon >0$. The function $v+\varepsilon (|x|^2-R^2)$ is less than $-1$ on $K$, so by upper semicontinuity it is less than $-1$ on an open neighborhood $U$ of $K$. The compact sets $K_ j\setminus U$ decrease and have empty intersection, so one of them is empty; thus $K_ j\subset U$ for all large $j$. Hence $v+\varepsilon (|x|^2-R^2)$ is admissible for $K_ j$, and therefore at most $f_{K_ j}$, when $j$ is large. Letting $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ and taking the supremum over $v$, we obtain $f_{K_ j}\to f_ K$ pointwise. Since $f_{K_ j}=u_{K_ j}$ and $f_ K=u_ K$ almost everywhere (theorem 3.4.2) and all these functions take values in $[-1,0]$, bounded convergence gives $u_{K_ j}\to u_ K$ in $L^1(B)$. Choose $\chi \in C_ c^\infty (B)$ equal to one near $K_1$. Since all the Riesz measures are supported in $K_1$,

\[ C_ B(K_ j)=\int \chi \, \mathrm d\lambda _{K_ j}=\int u_{K_ j}\Delta \chi \, \mathrm d x\longrightarrow \int u_ K\Delta \chi \, \mathrm d x=C_ B(K). \]

For strong subadditivity we first prove that $F=f_{K\cup L}+f_{K\cap L}$ satisfies $F\geq f_ K+f_ L$. At a point of $K$ we have $f_{K\cup L}=-1=f_ K$, and $f_{K\cap L}\geq f_ L$ because $K\cap L\subset L$; the case of a point of $L$ is symmetric. So the inequality holds on $K\cup L$. By proposition 4.3.2, $F$ is harmonic on the open set $U=B\setminus (K\cup L)$, lower semicontinuous on $B$, and tends to zero at $\partial B$. Let $v$ and $w$ be continuous admissible functions for $K$ and $L$. At a boundary point $\zeta $ of $U$ in $B$, which lies in $K\cup L$, we have

\[ \begin{aligned} \varlimsup _{U\ni x\to \zeta }(v+w)(x)& =v(\zeta )+w(\zeta )\leq f_ K(\zeta )+f_ L(\zeta )\\ & \leq F(\zeta )\leq \varliminf _{U\ni x\to \zeta }F(x), \end{aligned} \]

and at points of $\partial B$ the upper limits of $v+w$ are nonpositive while $F$ tends to zero. By proposition 3.1.4, applied to the subharmonic function $v+w-F$ on the bounded open set $U$, we get $v+w\leq F$ on $U$. Taking the supremum over $v$ and $w$, which by proposition 4.3.2 gives $f_ K$ and $f_ L$, proves the inequality on $U$, hence on $B$.

Since each $f$ equals the corresponding $u$ almost everywhere, we obtain $u_{K\cup L}+u_{K\cap L}\geq u_ K+u_ L$ almost everywhere; taking ball averages and letting the radius tend to zero, (3.3) gives this inequality everywhere. The two sides are the negatives of the Green potentials of $\lambda _{K\cup L}+\lambda _{K\cap L}$ and of $\lambda _ K+\lambda _ L$, so part (2) of proposition 4.3.2 gives the asserted inequality of masses. $\square$

We extend the relative capacity to arbitrary sets with compact closure in $B$. For an open set $O\Subset B$ and a set $A\Subset B$ put

\[ \begin{aligned} C_ B(O)& =\sup \{ C_ B(K):K\subset O\text{ compact}\} ,\\ C_ B^*(A)& =\inf \{ C_ B(O):O\text{ open},\ A\subset O\Subset B\} . \end{aligned} \]

We call $C_ B^*(A)$ the outer relative capacity of $A$. For an open set $O\Subset B$ we have $C_ B^*(O)=C_ B(O)$: the set $O$ itself is allowed in the infimum, and every open $O'\supset O$ satisfies $C_ B(O')\geq C_ B(O)$, because compact subsets of $O$ are compact subsets of $O'$. The outer capacity $C_ B^*$ is increasing by definition, and $C_ B^*(\varnothing )=0$.

Theorem 4.3.5. The outer relative capacity has the following properties.

  1. $C_ B^*(K)=C_ B(K)$ for every compact set $K\subset B$.

  2. It is increasing and countably subadditive: $C_ B^*(\bigcup _ jA_ j)\leq \sum _ jC_ B^*(A_ j)$ whenever $\bigcup _ jA_ j\Subset B$.

  3. If $A_1\subset A_2\subset \cdots $ and $A=\bigcup _ jA_ j\Subset B$, then $C_ B^*(A_ j)\to C_ B^*(A)$.

  4. If $C_ B^*(A)=0$, then $A$ is polar.

Proof. (1) Let $K\subset B$ be compact; we may assume that $K\neq \varnothing $, since $C_ B^*(\varnothing )=0=C_ B(\varnothing )$. For $0<\delta <\operatorname {dist}(K,\mathbb R^ d\setminus B)$, the compact sets $K_ j=\{ x:\operatorname {dist}(x,K)\leq \delta /j\} $ are neighborhoods of $K$ in $B$ that decrease to $K$; let $O_ j$ be their interiors. Every open set containing $K$ has capacity at least $C_ B(K)$, so by proposition 4.3.4

\[ C_ B(K)\leq C_ B^*(K)\leq C_ B(O_ j)\leq C_ B(K_ j)\longrightarrow C_ B(K). \]

Strong subadditivity. Parts (2) and (3) rest on two inequalities for $C_ B^*$, the first of which is $C_ B^*(A\cup A')+C_ B^*(A\cap A')\leq C_ B^*(A)+C_ B^*(A')$ for $A,A'\Subset B$. Consider first open sets $O,O'\Subset B$, and let $K\subset O\cup O'$ and $L\subset O\cap O'$ be compact. Cover $K$ by finitely many closed balls, each contained in $O$ or in $O'$, and let $K'$ and $K''$ be the intersections of $K$ with the union of those contained in $O$ and in $O'$, respectively. Then $K'\cup L\subset O$ and $K''\cup L\subset O'$ are compact, their union contains $K$ and their intersection contains $L$. By monotonicity and proposition 4.3.4,

\[ \begin{aligned} C_ B(K)+C_ B(L)& \leq C_ B\bigl ((K'\cup L)\cup (K''\cup L)\bigr )+C_ B\bigl ((K'\cup L)\cap (K''\cup L)\bigr )\\ & \leq C_ B(K'\cup L)+C_ B(K''\cup L)\leq C_ B(O)+C_ B(O'). \end{aligned} \]

Taking the supremum over $K$ and $L$ proves the inequality for open sets. For arbitrary $A,A'\Subset B$, apply it to open sets $O\supset A$ and $O'\supset A'$ with compact closures in $B$, and take the infimum.

An excess inequality. Let $A_ j\subset E_ j$ be sets with $E_1\cup E_2\Subset B$. All the capacities involved are finite. Strong subadditivity applied to $E_1$ and $A_1\cup E_2$, and to $A_1\cup A_2$ and $E_2$, gives

\[ \begin{aligned} C_ B^*(E_1\cup E_2)+C_ B^*(A_1)& \leq C_ B^*(E_1)+C_ B^*(A_1\cup E_2),\\ C_ B^*(A_1\cup E_2)+C_ B^*(A_2)& \leq C_ B^*(A_1\cup A_2)+C_ B^*(E_2), \end{aligned} \]

where we have used monotonicity to bound the capacities of the intersections from below. Adding these inequalities and cancelling $C_ B^*(A_1\cup E_2)$ gives the case $N=2$ of

4.15
\begin{equation} \label{eq:outer-capacity-excess} C_ B^*\left(\bigcup _{j=1}^ NE_ j\right)-C_ B^*\left(\bigcup _{j=1}^ NA_ j\right) \leq \sum _{j=1}^ N\bigl (C_ B^*(E_ j)-C_ B^*(A_ j)\bigr ), \end{equation}

valid whenever $A_ j\subset E_ j$ and $\bigcup _ jE_ j\Subset B$. Induction on $N$, applied to the pairs $\bigcup _{j<N}A_ j\subset \bigcup _{j<N}E_ j$ and $A_ N\subset E_ N$, proves (4.15) in general.

(2) and (3). If $O_ j$ are open sets increasing to an open set $O\Subset B$, every compact subset of $O$ lies in some $O_ j$, so $C_ B(O_ j)\to C_ B(O)$. Now let $A_ j$ be arbitrary sets with $A=\bigcup _ jA_ j\Subset B$, fix an open set $W$ with $\overline A\subset W\Subset B$, and let $\varepsilon >0$. Intersecting almost optimal open sets in the definition of $C_ B^*(A_ j)$ with $W$, we find open sets $E_ j$ with $A_ j\subset E_ j\subset W$ and $C_ B(E_ j)<C_ B^*(A_ j)+\varepsilon 2^{-j}$. By (4.15) and the result for open sets,

\[ C_ B^*\left(\bigcup _ j A_ j\right)\leq C_ B\left(\bigcup _ j E_ j\right)=\lim _{N\to \infty }C_ B\left(\bigcup _{j=1}^ NE_ j\right) \leq \lim _{N\to \infty }C_ B^*\left(\bigcup _{j=1}^ N A_ j\right)+\varepsilon . \]

Let $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$. If the $A_ j$ increase, the right side is $\lim _ NC_ B^*(A_ N)\leq C_ B^*(A)$, which proves (3). In general, strong subadditivity gives finite subadditivity, and the displayed inequality extends it to countable subadditivity, which proves (2).

(4) Suppose $C_ B^*(A)=0$. We construct subharmonic functions $v_ j$ on $B$ that equal $-1$ on $A$ and whose Riesz measures have mass less than $2^{-j}$. Their sum will be a nontrivial subharmonic function equal to $-\infty $ on $A$. Fix an open set $W$ with $\overline A\subset W\Subset B$, and choose open sets $O_ j$ with $A\subset O_ j\subset W$ and $C_ B(O_ j)<2^{-j}$. Exhaust $O_ j$ by compact sets $L_{j,m}$, increasing in $m$, whose interiors cover $O_ j$. Choose $c>0$ with $q(x)=c(|x|^2-R^2)\leq -1$ on $\overline W$; then $q$ is admissible for every $L_{j,m}$, so $u_{L_{j,m}}\geq q$. The functions $u_{L_{j,m}}$ decrease as $m$ increases, and their limit $v_ j$ is subharmonic by theorem 3.1.6, lies between $q$ and $0$, and hence tends to zero at $\partial B$. Moreover, $v_ j=-1$ on $O_ j$, since $f_{L_{j,m}}$ and hence $u_{L_{j,m}}$ equal $-1$ on the interior of $L_{j,m}$.

The functions $u_{L_{j,m}}$ converge to $v_ j$ in $L^1(B)$ by bounded convergence, so their Riesz measures, which are supported in $\overline W$, converge in the sense of distributions on $B$, and hence vaguely by proposition 1.5.1, to the Riesz measure $\lambda _ j$ of $v_ j$. By proposition 1.5.2, applied to the open set $B\setminus \overline W$, the measure $\lambda _ j$ is supported in $\overline W$. Testing against a function in $C_ c^\infty (B)$ equal to one near $\overline W$ gives $\lambda _ j(B)=\lim _ mC_ B(L_{j,m})=C_ B(O_ j)$, since every compact subset of $O_ j$ lies in some $L_{j,m}$. The function $v_ j$ is bounded, hence locally integrable, tends to zero at $\partial B$, and has a Riesz measure with compact support in $B$; by the converse in part (2) of proposition 4.3.2, $v_ j=-G_ B\lambda _ j$.

The measure $\nu =\sum _ j\lambda _ j$ is finite, since $\lambda _ j(B)=C_ B(O_ j)<2^{-j}$, and it is supported in $\overline W$. By Tonelli's theorem for the nonnegative kernel $G_ B$, we have $\sum _ jv_ j=-G_ B\nu $. By part (2) of proposition 4.3.2, $-G_ B\nu $ is subharmonic on $B$ with Riesz measure $\nu $, so it is locally integrable and not identically $-\infty $. Since every $v_ j$ equals $-1$ on $A$, the sum $\sum _ jv_ j$ equals $-\infty $ on $A$. Hence $A$ is polar. $\square$

Example 4.3.6. Let $0<r<R$ and $K=\overline{B(0,r)}$. The function $x\mapsto G_ B(x,0)$ is radial and decreasing in $|x|$ by lemma 4.3.1. We claim that $u_ K=f_ K=w$, where

\[ w=\max \left\{ -1,-\frac{G_ B(\cdot ,0)}{G_ B(re_1,0)}\right\} . \]

The function $w$ equals $-1$ on $K$ and $-G_ B(x,0)/G_ B(re_1,0)$ on the annulus $r<|x|<R$. It is subharmonic, because $-G_ B(\cdot ,0)/G_ B(re_1,0)$ is a positive multiple of $E_ d$ plus a constant on $B$, and it is nonpositive; hence it is admissible and $w\leq f_ K$. Conversely, let $v$ be admissible. On the annulus, $w$ is harmonic and continuous up to the boundary, with boundary values $-1$ on $|x|=r$ and $0$ on $|x|=R$. Since $v\leq -1$ on the inner sphere and $v\leq 0$ on $B$, upper semicontinuity shows that the boundary upper limits of $v$ are at most these values. The comparison principle (theorem 3.1.3) on the annulus gives $v\leq w$ there, and $v\leq -1=w$ on $K$. Hence $f_ K=w$, and since $w$ is continuous, $u_ K=w$. Near $\partial B$, $u_ K$ is $-1/G_ B(re_1,0)$ times $G_ B(\cdot ,0)$, whose outward normal derivative on $\partial B$ is $-(s_{d-1}R^{d-1})^{-1}$. Therefore the flux formula of proposition 4.3.2 gives

\[ C_ B(\overline{B(0,r)})=\frac{1}{G_ B(re_1,0)}= \begin{cases} 2\pi /\log (R/r),& d=2,\\ (d-2)s_{d-1}/(r^{2-d}-R^{2-d}),& d\geq 3. \end{cases} \]

These are relative capacities. To compute the kernel capacities of the same ball, let $\sigma _ r$ be the normalized surface measure on the sphere $|x|=r$ and $p=E_ d*\sigma _ r$. If $|x|>r$, then $p(x)$ is the spherical mean over $\partial B(0,r)$ of the function $y\mapsto E_ d(x-y)$, which is harmonic on a neighborhood of $\overline{B(0,r)}$; hence $p(x)=E_ d(x)$ by the mean-value property (theorem 2.1.2). Surface measure is invariant under orthogonal transformations (section 2.1), which act transitively on spheres centered at the origin, so $p$ is radial. On the ball $|x|<r$, the function $p$ is harmonic: by proposition 3.2.1 and theorem 2.1.4 it agrees almost everywhere with a harmonic function there, hence everywhere by theorem 3.2.2. Since $p$ is radial, for $0<|x|<r$ the value $p(x)$ is the spherical mean of $p$ over $\partial B(0,|x|)$, which equals $p(0)$ by the mean-value property; moreover, $p(0)=E_ d(re_1)$ because $E_ d$ is radial. Finally, $p$ is constant on the sphere $|x|=r$, which is the support of $\sigma _ r$, so part (1) of theorem 4.2.2 shows that $p$ is continuous at the points of the sphere. Hence $p=E_ d(re_1)$ on $K$.

Consequently the Newtonian potential of $\sigma _ r$ equals $\Gamma _ d(re_1)$ on $K$ and $\Gamma _ d(x)$ for $|x|>r$, and the logarithmic potential with a scale $D>2r$ equals $(2\pi )^{-1}\log (D/r)$ on $K$ and $(2\pi )^{-1}\log (D/|x|)$ for $|x|>r$. In particular $\sigma _ r$ has finite energy, and its potential $U^{\sigma _ r}$ has a constant value $V$ on $K$. Since $\sigma _ r$ is a nonzero measure with finite energy carried by the sphere $|x|=r$, theorem 4.3.3 shows that this sphere is not polar, although it has Lebesgue measure zero: for $0<\varepsilon <r$ it is contained in $B(0,r+\varepsilon )\setminus B(0,r-\varepsilon )$, whose measure $s_{d-1}\bigl ((r+\varepsilon )^ d-(r-\varepsilon )^ d\bigr )/d$ tends to zero as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$.

Let $\nu $ be a probability measure on $K$ with finite energy. Then $\nu -\sigma _ r$ has total mass zero, and its positive and negative parts are dominated by $\nu $ and $\sigma _ r$, so they have finite energy because the kernel is nonnegative on $K\times K$. Moreover, $\int U^{\sigma _ r}\, \mathrm d(\nu -\sigma _ r)=V-V=0$. Hence proposition 4.1.2 gives

\[ I(\nu )=I(\sigma _ r)+2\int U^{\sigma _ r}\, \mathrm d(\nu -\sigma _ r)+I(\nu -\sigma _ r)\geq I(\sigma _ r). \]

Thus $\sigma _ r$ is the equilibrium measure of $K$, the Newtonian capacity of $K$ is $(d-2)s_{d-1}r^{d-2}$ if $d\geq 3$, and its logarithmic capacity is $r$ if $d=2$. A probability measure on a compact set is also one on every larger compact set, so the kernel capacities are increasing; when $d=2$, we compute both logarithmic capacities with a scale exceeding the diameter of the larger set, as theorem 4.1.3 allows. Letting $r\downarrow 0$, we recover the fact that a point has kernel capacity zero.
