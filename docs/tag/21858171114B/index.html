Lemma 4.1.121858171114B

Lemma 4.1.1. Let $\sigma $ and $\tau $ be finite real signed measures on $\mathbb R^ d$, and for $t>0$ put

\[ Q_ t(\sigma ,\tau )=\iint \mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}\, \mathrm d\sigma (x)\, \mathrm d\tau (y),\qquad h_ t^\sigma (z)=\int \mathrm e^{-2t|z-x|^2}\, \mathrm d\sigma (x), \]

and $Q_ t(\sigma )=Q_ t(\sigma ,\sigma )$. Then $h_ t^\sigma $ is a bounded continuous function, and

4.2
\begin{equation} Q_ t(\sigma ,\tau )=\left(\frac{4t}{\pi }\right)^{d/2}\int _{\mathbb R^ d}h_ t^\sigma (z)\, h_ t^\tau (z)\, \mathrm d z. \end{equation}

In particular $Q_ t(\sigma )\geq 0$. If $Q_ t(\sigma )=0$ for arbitrarily large $t$, then $\sigma =0$.

Proof. The function $h_ t^\sigma $ is bounded by $|\sigma |(\mathbb R^ d)$, and it is continuous by dominated convergence. Completing the square gives $|z-x|^2+|z-y|^2=2|z-\tfrac 12(x+y)|^2+\tfrac 12|x-y|^2$. Since $\int _{\mathbb R^ d}\mathrm e^{-4t|w|^2}\, \mathrm dw=(\pi /4t)^{d/2}$, it follows that

\[ \mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}=\left(\frac{4t}{\pi }\right)^{d/2}\int _{\mathbb R^ d}\mathrm e^{-2t|z-x|^2}\, \mathrm e^{-2t|z-y|^2}\, \mathrm d z. \]

We integrate this identity against $\sigma \otimes \tau $. Fubini's theorem applies, because the integral of the absolute value of the integrand with respect to $\mathrm dz\otimes |\sigma |\otimes |\tau |$ is at most $(\pi /4t)^{d/2}|\sigma |(\mathbb R^ d)|\tau |(\mathbb R^ d)$, and integrating first in $x$ and in $y$ gives (4.2). For $\tau =\sigma $ it shows that $Q_ t(\sigma )$ is a positive multiple of $\int (h_ t^\sigma )^2\, \mathrm dz$. Hence $Q_ t(\sigma )\geq 0$, and if $Q_ t(\sigma )=0$, then $h_ t^\sigma =0$ almost everywhere, hence everywhere, because $h_ t^\sigma $ is continuous.

Now suppose that $Q_{t_ j}(\sigma )=0$ for a sequence $t_ j\to \infty $. The functions $g_ t(z)=(2t/\pi )^{d/2}\mathrm e^{-2t|z|^2}$ are nonnegative and have integral one, and $\int _{|z|\geq \delta }g_ t\, \mathrm d z\to 0$ as $t\to \infty $ for every $\delta >0$. Hence $g_ t*f\to f$ uniformly on $\mathbb R^ d$ for every $f\in C_ c(\mathbb R^ d)$: in the integral defining $g_ t*f-f$ we use the uniform continuity of $f$ on the region $|z|<\delta $ and the smallness of the Gaussian tail on the region $|z|\geq \delta $. Since $g_{t_ j}*\sigma =(2t_ j/\pi )^{d/2}h_{t_ j}^\sigma =0$, Fubini's theorem and the symmetry of $g_ t$ give

\[ \int (g_{t_ j}*f)\, \mathrm d\sigma =\int f\, (g_{t_ j}*\sigma )\, \mathrm d z=0. \]

Letting $j\to \infty $, we get $\int f\, \mathrm d\sigma =0$ for every $f\in C_ c(\mathbb R^ d)$. Thus the positive and negative parts of $\sigma $ define the same positive linear functional on $C_ c(\mathbb R^ d)$, and the uniqueness assertion of theorem 1.2.2 shows that they are equal, that is, $\sigma =0$. $\square$
