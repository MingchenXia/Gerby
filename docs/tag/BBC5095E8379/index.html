From Foundations of pluripotential theory · Introduction to Part I

This part develops the potential theory of the Laplacian on open subsets of $\mathbb R^ d$, as far as it is needed for the complex theory of 2. A potential can be described in three ways: by its distributional derivatives, by its mean values, and by the measure that records its singularities. Each description is suited to different operations, namely weak limits, pointwise comparison and the analysis of poles. Our main task is to pass between these descriptions while keeping control of the exceptional sets produced by limits and envelopes.

Measures and distributions come first (chapter 1), because the curvature currents of later chapters have measures as coefficients. We prove that positive linear functionals are given by measures, approximate distributions by smooth functions through convolution, and establish the compactness principles used to extract weak limits. Harmonic functions (chapter 2) are the comparison functions of the theory. The Poisson kernel solves the Dirichlet problem on a ball; on a general bounded domain, Perron's method produces a harmonic function in the interior, and barriers decide whether it attains the prescribed boundary values.

Subharmonic functions (chapter 3) are obtained by replacing the mean-value equality by an inequality. Their distributional Laplacians are positive measures, and a subharmonic function is determined at every point by its almost-everywhere class. This correspondence is indispensable for upper envelopes: the pointwise supremum of a family and its upper semicontinuous regularization may differ, and one must know where. The Riesz decomposition splits a subharmonic function into a harmonic part and the potential of its Laplacian, which links comparison arguments with energy.

Capacity (chapter 4) measures the size of a set by the potentials it can support. A set of Lebesgue measure zero can have positive capacity, whereas a polar set carries no nonzero measure of finite energy. Equilibrium potentials connect this distinction with comparison arguments. The fine topology (chapter 5) is the coarsest topology that makes all subharmonic functions continuous. The reduction of a nonnegative superharmonic function on a set is the infimum of the nonnegative superharmonic functions that dominate it there, and balayage sweeps a measure onto a compact set while preserving its potential quasi-everywhere on that set. These constructions explain why the exceptional sets of potential theory can be discarded only when they have capacity zero, and not merely when they have Lebesgue measure zero.

The last chapter of this part (chapter 6) prepares the passage to several complex variables. Before subharmonicity along complex lines can produce a plurisubharmonic function, upper bounds along individual lines must be made uniform. An energy estimate, the Fourier transform and a projection argument provide this uniformity. The same Fourier methods give $L^ p$ estimates for second derivatives. The two results serve different purposes in 2: the incidence estimate provides the local upper bounds that identify the pointwise representative of a plurisubharmonic function, while the second-derivative estimates control the Monge–Ampère measure of an envelope lying below a nonsmooth obstacle.
