From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory · Introduction

A singular potential can be studied through its growth near its poles, through the holomorphic functions that remain integrable there, or through its Monge–Ampère measure. These descriptions lead to ordinary singularity types, multiplier ideals and curvature measures. Global pluripotential theory relates them and asks which singularities can be approximated by analytic ones. This book develops that theory for Hermitian backgrounds, starting from Singularities in global pluripotential theory [SGPT].

In a fixed Kähler class, all bounded potentials have the same Monge–Ampère mass. The non-pluripolar mass of a singular potential can therefore be compared with a fixed total mass, and this comparison connects analytic approximation, envelopes and energy. A nonclosed Hermitian background changes this relation at its first step: the mass can vary even among smooth potentials. Ordinary singularity type, which identifies potentials with bounded difference, no longer determines a single mass. Envelopes and multiplier ideals remain available, but their connection to mass requires different arguments.

This loss of closedness is distinct from the geometry of the manifold. A non-Kähler manifold can carry closed positive currents, and a Kähler manifold can be equipped with a nonclosed Hermitian form. Let $X$ be a connected compact complex manifold of dimension $n\geq 1$, and let $\theta $ be a smooth real $(1,1)$-form on $X$. A function $u$ is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic if it is locally the sum of a plurisubharmonic function and a smooth function and the current $\theta _ u=\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ is positive. If $\theta $ is closed, this current is locally a complex Hessian. For a general background, a smooth local correction still makes $u$ plurisubharmonic, but leaves a smooth remainder in the current. Compactness, regularization and multiplier ideals thus survive locally; the new difficulties arise when these constructions are related globally.
