Chapter 18 · Toric and convex geometry · Tag 6F5A4A494129

Toric pluripotential theory on ample line bundles

From Singularities in global pluripotential theory · 5 Toric pluripotential theory on ample line bundles

This chapter develops the toric form of pluripotential theory in the case of an ample line bundle. Throughout the chapter, $P\subseteq M_{\mathbb R}$ is a full-dimensional smooth lattice polytope, $\Sigma $ is the corresponding fan, $X=X_\Sigma $ is the associated smooth projective toric variety, and $L=\mathcal O_ X(D_ P)$ is the ample toric line bundle defined by $P$.

The basic principle is that toric plurisubharmonic metrics on $L$ can be described by convex functions on $N_{\mathbb R}$. Under this dictionary, singularity type, rooftop envelopes, Monge–Ampère mass and energy all admit convex-analytic interpretations. The Newton body of a toric potential is the main invariant in this chapter: it records the effective convex domain seen by multiplier ideals and non-pluripolar mass.

The central result is Yao's theorem, stated as theorem 5.2.14. It characterizes the torus characters belonging to multiplier-ideal spaces in terms of the Newton body. As a consequence, the asymptotic dimension of

\[ \mathrm{H}^0\left(X,L^ k\otimes \mathcal I(k\varphi )\right) \]

is governed by the Euclidean volume of the Newton body. This gives a toric model for the general volume theorem proved later in chapter 7 and shows, in the ample toric setting, why multiplier ideals and non-pluripolar mass should be related.

The chapter also proves the real Monge–Ampère formula for toric metrics, the toric energy formula, and the expression of the $d_1$-distance in convex terms. These results will be used again in chapter 12, where the ample assumption is removed and the Newton body is compared with partial Okounkov bodies.

We assume familiarity with basic toric geometry at the level of [CLS11]. Some convex-analytic facts used below are recalled in chapter A. The chapter can be skipped on a first reading if the reader is only interested in the non-toric theory, but it provides useful examples and a guiding model for several later constructions.

Finally, throughout the book convex functions are understood in the usual sense: the function $\mathbb R\ni x\mapsto x^2$ is convex. This convention is opposite to the convention often used in toric geometry, including parts of [CLS11]; care is therefore needed when comparing formulas.
