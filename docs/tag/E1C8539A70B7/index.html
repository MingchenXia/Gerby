Varying the curve coefficients

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory · 16.6.9 Varying the curve coefficients

The coefficient $f$ of the auxiliary curve in corollary 16.6.32 does not affect the metric on the model locus. Scaling the curve forms themselves has a different effect: a positive coefficient rescales the polar distance in its coordinate, whereas a zero coefficient removes the coordinate altogether, so the diameter depends on the remaining curve areas. We describe the resulting family of model spaces and then compare its members.

Keep the product $M=Y\times C_0\times Z$, its projections and the forms of that corollary. For $a=(a_1,\ldots ,a_ m)\in [0,\infty )^ m$ and a smooth function $f\geq 0$ on $Y$, put

\[ J(a)=\{ i:a_ i>0\} ,\qquad \beta _ a=\sum _ i a_ i\pi _ i^*\eta _ i,\qquad \Theta _{a,f}=p^*\beta _ a+(f\circ p)s^*\tau +r^*\kappa . \]

The coordinates $i\in J(a)$ are called active. For normalized ordinary models $q_ i$ in the fixed curve backgrounds $\eta _ i$, define

\[ \Phi _ a(q)=\Psi +\sum _{i\in J(a)}a_ iq_ i\circ \pi _ i\circ p, \qquad \Psi =\psi \circ r. \]

Terms with zero coefficient are omitted, also on their pole sets, so $\Phi _ a(q)$ depends only on the active coordinates. Write $\mathcal S_ a$ for this locus of potentials, and $q_{i,\mathcal I}=P_{\eta _ i}[q_ i]_{\mathcal I}$.

Lemma 16.6.33. For every $a$ and $f$ as above,

\[ P_{\Theta _{a,f}}[\Phi _ a(q)]=\Phi _ a(q),\qquad P_{\Theta _{a,f}}[\Phi _ a(q)]_{\mathcal I}=\Phi _ a(q_{\mathcal I}). \]

Both potentials have supremum zero. In particular, $\mathcal S_0=\{ \Psi \} $.

Proof. For $a_ i>0$, scaling the ordinary competitors shows that $a_ iq_ i$ is an ordinary model for $a_ i\eta _ i$. Its polar curvature is $a_ i\mu _ i$, where $\mu _ i$ is the polar curvature of $q_ i$. Taking atomic parts and applying theorem 30.13.3, we see that its ideal model is $a_ iq_{i,\mathcal I}$.

Put $w_ a=\sum _{i\in J(a)}a_ iq_ i\circ \pi _ i$. If there are at least two active factors, lemma 16.6.29 makes the corresponding sum an ordinary model on the product of the active curves, with its positive product background. For one active factor, this is the identity on the curve. The identity is preserved under pullback to $Y$, by proposition 1.22.3: every competitor in the pulled-back background descends, and so do its obstacle bound and its ordinary order, since the projection is surjective. If there are no active factors, the normalized model is zero by the maximum principle. Hence $P_{\beta _ a}[w_ a]=w_ a$ in all cases.

On $B=Y\times C_0$, use the background $\beta _{a,f}=\pi ^*\beta _ a+(f\circ \pi )s_0^*\tau $. As in corollary 16.6.32, replace an ordinary competitor by its maximum with $w_ a\circ \pi $ and restrict it to each section $Y\times \{ z\} $. This gives $P_{\beta _{a,f}}[w_ a\circ \pi ]=w_ a\circ \pi $.

For the ideal identity, take $g_ a=\sum _{i\in J(a)}a_ i g_ i\circ \pi _ i\circ \pi $, with the atomic Green potentials $g_ i$ of lemma 19.5.2. Scaling, addition and pullback preserve multiplier-ideal equivalence by lemma 1.16.4, lemma 1.16.6. The complex Hessian of $g_ a$ is a closed countable divisor current of finite mass minus a smooth form, and $g_ a$ is $\beta _{a,f}$-plurisubharmonic. It is ordinarily equivalent to $w_{a,\mathcal I}\circ \pi $, where $w_{a,\mathcal I}=\sum _{i\in J(a)}a_ iq_{i,\mathcal I}\circ \pi _ i$. By lemma 1.17.4, every ideal competitor is an ordinary competitor for $g_ a$, so by the ordinary identity applied to the models $a_ iq_{i,\mathcal I}$ it is bounded by $w_{a,\mathcal I}\circ \pi $. The latter is itself an ideal competitor, so the ideal envelope equals it. When $J(a)$ is empty, both envelopes on $B$ are zero directly. Finally, proposition 16.6.13, applied to $M\to B$ and the divisor $B\times D$ with weight one, gives the identities upstairs, and independence of the factors gives normalization. $\square$

For two tuples $q,\widetilde q$, let $\mu _ i,\nu _ i$ be their polar curvature measures in the fixed backgrounds $\eta _ i$, and set

16.31
\begin{equation} \label{eq:weighted-product-polar-metric} \rho _ a(\Phi _ a(q),\Phi _ a(\widetilde q)) =\sum _{i\in J(a)}a_ i\| \mu _ i-\nu _ i\| _{\mathrm{TV}}. \end{equation}

On the singleton $\mathcal S_0$ this distance is zero.

Proposition 16.6.34. For every $a$, the locus $\mathcal S_ a$ is a complete geodesic metric space for $\rho _ a$, independently of $f$. Its ideal subspace is closed, geodesically convex and equal to the metric closure of its models with analytic singularities. Ideal projection is the unique nearest-point retraction onto this subspace and is $1$-Lipschitz, with distance

\[ \rho _ a\bigl (\Phi _ a(q),\Phi _ a(q_{\mathcal I})\bigr ) =\sum _{i\in J(a)}a_ i\mu _ i^{\mathrm{na}}(C_ i), \]

where $\mu _ i^{\mathrm{na}}$ is the nonatomic part of $\mu _ i$. The diameter is $2\sum _ i a_ iA_ i$, and metric convergence implies $L^1(M)$ convergence for every smooth positive volume form. The upper-volume comparison vanishes on this locus.

If $a,b$ have the same nonempty active set $J$, the correspondence $T_{ba}\Phi _ a(q)=\Phi _ b(q)$ is well defined, commutes with ideal projection and satisfies

\[ \min _{i\in J}\frac{b_ i}{a_ i}\, \rho _ a(u,v) \leq \rho _ b(T_{ba}u,T_{ba}v) \leq \max _{i\in J}\frac{b_ i}{a_ i}\, \rho _ a(u,v). \]

For a fixed smooth positive volume form on $M$, there are constants $K_ i$, independent of $a,b$ and of the normalized model tuple $q$, such that

\[ \| \Phi _ a(q)-\Phi _ b(q)\| _{L^1(M)} \leq \sum _ i K_ i|a_ i-b_ i|. \]

Consequently, as all $a_ i$ tend to zero, the diameter tends to zero and every potential in $\mathcal S_ a$ tends to $\Psi $ in $L^1(M)$, uniformly over the normalized model tuples.

Proof. Restriction to coordinate sections on which the other potentials are finite determines every active term $a_ iq_ i$ up to a constant, and normalization makes these constants zero. Thus (16.31) is well defined, and it identifies $\mathcal S_ a$ with the product of the spaces of curve models in the active coordinates, with their distances multiplied by $a_ i$. Coordinates with $a_ i=0$ do not appear, and for $J(a)=\varnothing $ the empty product is a single point.

The completeness, geodesic and atomic-projection results of corollary 30.12.3, proposition 30.12.6 apply coordinate by coordinate, and the weighted distances add; this proves the metric assertions and the distance formula. The diameter bound is attained by choosing polar measures $A_ i\delta _{x_ i}$ and $A_ i\delta _{y_ i}$ with $x_ i\ne y_ i$ on each active curve. Fiber integration and theorem 30.12.4 give $L^1$ convergence upstairs. The preceding lemma shows that ideal projection does not depend on $f$. Since every model and every pairwise maximum is $\Psi $ plus a pullback from $B$, proposition 16.6.13 gives zero upper volumes, so the upper-volume comparison vanishes.

For analytic density, approximate the atomic part $a_ i\mu _ i^{\mathrm{at}}$ of each scaled polar measure, where $\mu _ i^{\mathrm{at}}=\mu _ i-\mu _ i^{\mathrm{na}}$, by finite atomic measures with rational coefficients and mass at most $a_ iA_ i$. These approximations converge in total variation. Their curve models in the background $a_ i\eta _ i$ have finitely many logarithmic poles with rational coefficients, whose denominators, together with the unit coefficient of $D$, can be cleared in local product monomials. They therefore give analytic models in $\mathcal S_ a$ converging to every ideal model. Conversely, suppose that $\Phi _ a(q)$ has analytic singularities. Restrict it to a section along an active curve on which all other potentials are finite and whose $Z$-coordinate lies outside $D$. By proposition 1.13.1, the restriction, which is $a_ iq_ i$ plus a constant and not identically $-\infty $, has analytic singularities. It is an ordinary model in the positive background $a_ i\eta _ i$, so proposition 16.2.7 shows that it equals its ideal projection. The preceding lemma now shows that $\Phi _ a(q)$ is an ideal model, and closedness gives the analytic-closure assertion. The argument uses rational coefficients for the scaled poles $a_ iq_ i$ themselves. With the rational-coefficient convention of section 1.13, an irrational scaling need not preserve an analytic curve model, since it can give a pole an irrational Lelong number.

For a common active set, the normalized tuple is determined by the potential. The two bounds for $T_{ba}$ follow by comparing each positive weight $b_ i$ with $a_ i$, and atomic projection in each coordinate commutes with this correspondence.

Finally, normalized compactness, theorem 1.4.2, bounds the $L^1(C_ i)$ norms of all the $q_ i$ uniformly. Integration along the compact fibers of $\pi _ i\circ p$ gives constants $K_ i$ bounding the $L^1(M)$ norms of their pullbacks. Off the pole sets, which have smooth volume zero, the difference of the two potentials is the sum of the functions $(a_ i-b_ i)q_ i\circ \pi _ i\circ p$. The triangle inequality proves the uniform estimate, also when some coefficients vanish. Taking $b=0$ gives uniform convergence to $\Psi $, and the diameter formula shows that the diameters tend to zero. $\square$

Thus letting the auxiliary coefficient vanish leaves the metric of corollary 16.6.32 unchanged, whereas letting a curve coefficient vanish removes its coordinate and decreases the diameter. These computations concern only the specified product loci, not general ordinary models in higher dimension.
