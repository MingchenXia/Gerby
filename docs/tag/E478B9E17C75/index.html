Non-pluripolar products

From Foundations of pluripotential theory · 15 Non-pluripolar products

In chapter 9 we multiplied the currents $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u_1,\ldots ,\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u_ p$ of locally bounded plurisubharmonic functions by the Bedford–Taylor recursion $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u_1\wedge T=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(u_1T)$. The potentials that arise in global geometry are often unbounded: by corollary 14.1.9, a closed positive $(1,1)$-current on a compact Kähler manifold is $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\varphi $ with $\theta $ a smooth form and $\varphi $ quasi-plurisubharmonic, and $\varphi =-\infty $ wherever the current has a positive Lelong number, for instance along a hypersurface carrying a current of integration. Indeed, at a point where $\varphi $ is finite, the spherical means of a local potential are bounded below by its value at that point, so the Lelong number vanishes there by (12.5). For such potentials the recursion breaks down, since it multiplies the measure coefficients of a current by an unbounded function. In this chapter we construct a product for arbitrary plurisubharmonic potentials which keeps positivity, closedness, multilinearity and locality, at the price of ignoring everything that happens on pluripolar sets.

The construction truncates the potentials and discards their poles. We replace each $u_ j$ by $\max \{ u_ j,-k\} $, take the Bedford–Taylor product, and keep only its restriction to the set where all $u_ j>-k$, on which the truncated potentials coincide with the original ones. Since bounded products are local in the plurifine topology (theorem 10.4.3), the restrictions obtained for different $k$ are compatible and increase with $k$. Their limit is the non-pluripolar product $\langle \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u_1\wedge \cdots \wedge \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u_ p\rangle $, which by construction gives no mass to pluripolar sets. Two phenomena without counterpart in the bounded theory appear. The limit may have infinite mass on compact sets (example 15.1.6). And the mass carried by the poles is lost: in degree one, $\langle \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\rangle $ is the restriction of $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ to $\{ u>-\infty \} $ (proposition 15.3.6), so the current of integration at a point of $\mathbb P^1$ has zero non-pluripolar product, and even a potential with zero Lelong numbers and trivial multiplier ideals can have zero non-pluripolar mass (example 15.3.8). When the local masses are finite, the product is a closed positive current (theorem 15.2.1).

On a compact Kähler manifold $X$ the local masses are always finite: every tuple of closed positive $(1,1)$-currents has a non-pluripolar product, and its total mass is bounded by a cohomological quantity (theorem 15.4.3). The total mass is, however, no longer determined by the cohomology classes of the factors; it depends on the singularities of the potentials. The central result of the second half of the chapter is that it is monotone in the singularities: if $\theta $ is a smooth closed real $(1,1)$-form, $\varphi ,\psi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, and $\psi $ is more singular than $\varphi $ in the sense of definition 14.2.6, then the mass of $\varphi $ is at least that of $\psi $ (theorem 15.7.4). When all potentials are locally bounded outside the closed pole set of a fixed quasi-plurisubharmonic function, this follows from Stokes' formula; arbitrary singularities are reduced to this case by an auxiliary potential on $X\times \mathbb P^ N$, whose total mass is an average of the masses along a segment of potentials. Along the way we prove that non-pluripolar products are lower semicontinuous under convergence in capacity, and continuous when no mass is lost in the limit (theorem 15.6.5).

Monotonicity has several consequences that are used in the rest of the book. Mixed masses do not change under bounded perturbations of the potentials, and in a big class the potentials with minimal singularities have the largest masses; these maximal masses define the positive intersection numbers and the volume of pseudo-effective classes. The full-mass classes and the comparison and domination principles at the end of the chapter are basic tools for the envelopes of chapter 16, and the fiber formula of section 15.5 is used for relative energy in chapter 17.

Historical remarks and references for this chapter are collected in section B.15.
