4.2 Fine topology and balayage

5 Fine topology and balayage

Throughout this chapter $\Omega \subset \mathbb R^ d$ is open and $d\geq 2$. Subharmonic functions are upper semicontinuous but in general not continuous. For instance, let $e_1$ be the first coordinate unit vector, and let $u=E_ d*\nu $ be the fundamental potential of the probability measure $\nu =\sum _{j\geq 1}2^{-j}\delta _{e_1/j}$, which is supported on the compact set $\{ 0\} \cup \{ e_1/j:j\geq 1\} $. By proposition 3.2.1, $u$ is subharmonic on $\mathbb R^ d$. At each atom we have $u(e_1/j)=-\infty $, because the integrand $E_ d(e_1/j-\cdot )$ is bounded above on the support of $\nu $ and equals $-\infty $ at $e_1/j$. On the other hand, $u(0)=\sum _ j2^{-j}E_ d(e_1/j)$ is finite, since $|E_ d(e_1/j)|$ equals $(2\pi )^{-1}\log j$ if $d=2$ and a constant multiple of $j^{d-2}$ if $d\geq 3$. Hence $u$ is not continuous at $0$.

By theorem 4.5.2, the discontinuities of subharmonic functions are nevertheless controlled by capacity: on a compact subset of a ball, a nontrivial subharmonic function becomes continuous once an open set of arbitrarily small capacity has been removed. In this chapter we study two further constructions, the fine topology and balayage, in which the polar sets of chapter 3, chapter 4 appear as exceptional sets. Both have counterparts in the pluripotential theory of 2.

The fine topology is the coarsest topology that contains the Euclidean topology and makes every subharmonic function continuous. In this topology, thinness of a set at a point becomes an ordinary neighborhood condition (proposition 5.1.3). Polar sets are finely closed, and the fine topology induces the discrete topology on them (proposition 5.1.5). Since there are uncountable compact polar sets, the fine topology is neither Lindelöf nor second countable (example 5.1.7). Up to polar sets, however, it has the Lindelöf property: by Choquet's lemma and theorem 4.4.4, every union of finely open sets agrees with a countable subunion outside a polar set (theorem 5.4.1). Consequently finely open sets are measurable, and together with quasi-continuity this shows that they are open up to sets of small capacity (proposition 5.4.2).

Balayage is based on reduction. For a nonnegative superharmonic function $s$ and a set $A$, the reduction $R_ s^ A$ of $s$ on $A$ is the infimum of all nonnegative superharmonic functions that majorize $s$ on $A$: it keeps the values of $s$ on $A$ and removes as much of $s$ elsewhere as superharmonicity permits. Its lower semicontinuous regularization $\widehat R_ s^ A$ is superharmonic and agrees with $R_ s^ A$ outside a polar set. On a ball, when $A$ is compact and $s$ is the Green potential of a measure $\mu $ whose potential is bounded on $A$ or whose Green energy is finite, $\widehat R_ s^ A$ is the Green potential of a measure $\mu ^ A$ carried by $A$, the balayage of $\mu $ onto $A$. We characterize $\mu ^ A$ by an orthogonality relation for the Green energy, which is a positive definite quadratic form on signed measures of finite energy (proposition 5.2.2, theorem 5.3.1, theorem 5.3.2), and express the balayage of a Dirac mass through harmonic measure (example 5.3.3).

Finally, we compare the fine topology of $\mathbb C^ n=\mathbb R^{2n}$ with the topology generated by the functions that are subharmonic on complex lines, which becomes the plurifine topology of chapter 10; for $n\geq 2$ the two topologies differ (proposition 5.5.1).

Historical remarks and references for this chapter are collected in section B.5.

5.1 Fine neighborhoods and thinness

A subharmonic function $u$ is upper semicontinuous, so its sublevel sets $\{ u<a\} $ are open. It is continuous exactly when its superlevel sets $\{ u>a\} $ are open as well. The fine topology is obtained by declaring all these superlevel sets open.

Definition 5.1.1. The classical fine topology on $\Omega $ is the coarsest topology on $\Omega $ that contains the Euclidean topology and makes every subharmonic function $u\in \mathrm{SH}(V)$, on every Euclidean open set $V\subset \Omega $, continuous as a map from $V$ with the induced topology to $[-\infty ,\infty )$. A set is finely open if it is open in this topology, and a fine neighborhood of a point is a neighborhood of it in this topology.

Since the sublevel sets of upper semicontinuous functions are Euclidean open, a subbasis of the fine topology consists of the Euclidean open sets and the sets $\{ x\in V:u(x)>a\} $, where $V\subset \Omega $ is Euclidean open, $u\in \mathrm{SH}(V)$ and $a\in \mathbb R$. Restricting finitely many such functions to the intersection of their domains, we see that the sets
\[ V\cap \{ u_1>a_1\} \cap \dots \cap \{ u_ m>a_ m\} ,\qquad m\geq 0,\quad u_ i\in \mathrm{SH}(V),\quad a_ i\in \mathbb R, \]

with $V\subset \Omega $ Euclidean open, form a basis of the fine topology. Superharmonic functions, being negatives of subharmonic functions, are finely continuous as well.

The fine topology depends on the pointwise values of subharmonic functions. These values are determined by the almost-everywhere class: by theorem 3.2.2, a locally integrable subharmonic function is the canonical representative (3.3) of its distribution class. A function that merely agrees almost everywhere with a subharmonic function need not be finely continuous. For instance, the indicator function $\mathbf1_{\{ x_0\} }$ of a point $x_0\in \Omega $ agrees almost everywhere with the zero function, but $\{ \mathbf1_{\{ x_0\} }>1/2\} =\{ x_0\} $ is not finely open by proposition 5.1.4 below.

In dimension one every subharmonic function is continuous, since on each component interval of its domain it is either identically $-\infty $ or finite, continuous and convex (example 3.2.4). The fine topology is then the Euclidean topology, and this is why we assume $d\geq 2$.

Definition 5.1.2. A set $A\subset \Omega $ is thin at a point $x\in \Omega $ if either $x$ does not belong to the Euclidean closure of $A\setminus \{ x\} $, or there is a subharmonic function $u$ on a Euclidean open neighborhood of $x$ such that

\[ u(x)>\varlimsup _{\substack {y\to x\\ y\in A\setminus \{ x\} }}u(y). \]

Proposition 5.1.3. Let $A\subset \Omega $ and $x\in \Omega $. Then $A$ is thin at $x$ if and only if some fine neighborhood of $x$ does not meet $A\setminus \{ x\} $. Moreover, every fine neighborhood of $x$ contains a set $\{ y\in V:u(y)>a\} $, where $V$ is a Euclidean open neighborhood of $x$, $u\in \mathrm{SH}(V)$, $a\in \mathbb R$ and $u(x)>a$.

Proof. Suppose first that $A$ is thin at $x$. If $x$ does not belong to the Euclidean closure of $A\setminus \{ x\} $, some Euclidean neighborhood of $x$ does not meet $A\setminus \{ x\} $. Otherwise let $u$ be a subharmonic function on a Euclidean open neighborhood $V$ of $x$ as in definition 5.1.2, and choose $a\in \mathbb R$ strictly between the upper limit in that definition and $u(x)$. By the definition of the upper limit, after shrinking $V$ we have $u<a$ on $(A\setminus \{ x\} )\cap V$. Hence $\{ y\in V:u(y)>a\} $ is a fine neighborhood of $x$ that does not meet $A\setminus \{ x\} $.

Conversely, let $N$ be a fine neighborhood of $x$. By the description of the basis above, $N$ contains a set

\[ N_0=V\cap \bigcap _{i=1}^ m\{ u_ i>a_ i\} ,\qquad u_ i\in \mathrm{SH}(V),\quad u_ i(x)>a_ i, \]

where $V$ is a Euclidean open neighborhood of $x$; adding the function $0$ with the constant $-1$ if necessary, we may assume that $m\geq 1$. Each $u_ i(x)$ is finite, since $u_ i(x)>a_ i$. Put $b_ i=u_ i(x)-a_ i>0$ and

\[ v=\sum _{i=1}^ m\frac{u_ i-u_ i(x)}{b_ i}\in \mathrm{SH}(V). \]

Then $v(x)=0$. We claim that

\[ \varlimsup _{\substack {y\to x\\ y\in V\setminus N_0}}v(y)\leq -1. \]

There is nothing to prove if $x$ is not an accumulation point of $V\setminus N_0$. Otherwise let $y_ k\to x$ be a sequence in $V\setminus N_0$ along which $v(y_ k)$ converges to the upper limit. For each $k$ some index $i$ satisfies $u_ i(y_ k)\leq a_ i$, that is, $(u_ i(y_ k)-u_ i(x))/b_ i\leq -1$; passing to a subsequence, we may assume that this index $i$ is the same for all $k$. For every other index $j$, upper semicontinuity of $u_ j$ at $x$ gives $\varlimsup _ k(u_ j(y_ k)-u_ j(x))/b_ j\leq 0$. Hence $\lim _ kv(y_ k)\leq -1$, which proves the claim.

If $N$ does not meet $A\setminus \{ x\} $, then $(A\setminus \{ x\} )\cap V\subset V\setminus N_0$, and the claim shows that either $x$ is not in the Euclidean closure of $A\setminus \{ x\} $ or the upper limit of $v$ along $A\setminus \{ x\} $ is at most $-1<v(x)$. In both cases $A$ is thin at $x$. For the last assertion, the claim provides a Euclidean open neighborhood $V'\subset V$ of $x$ such that $v<-1/2$ on $V'\setminus N_0$. Thus $\{ y\in V':v(y)>-1/2\} \subset N_0\subset N$, and $v(x)=0>-1/2$. $\square$

Proposition 5.1.4. Let $N$ be a fine neighborhood of a point $x\in \Omega $. Then every Euclidean ball centered at $x$ contains a Borel subset of $N$ of positive Lebesgue measure. In particular, the fine topology has no isolated points.

Proof. By proposition 5.1.3, $N$ contains a set $\{ y\in V:u(y)>a\} $ with $u\in \mathrm{SH}(V)$ and $u(x)>a$. It suffices to consider balls $B(x,r)$ with $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset V$. The set $E=\{ y\in B(x,r):u(y)>a\} $ is a Borel subset of $N$, because $u$ is Borel measurable. Let $M$ be a finite upper bound for $u$ on $\overline{B(x,r)}$; then $M\geq u(x)>a$. Since $u\leq M$ on $E$ and $u\leq a$ on $B(x,r)\setminus E$, the ball submean inequality of theorem 3.1.3 gives

\[ u(x)\leq \frac{1}{|B(x,r)|}\int _{B(x,r)}u(y)\, \mathrm d y\leq a+(M-a)\frac{|E|}{|B(x,r)|}. \]

Hence $|E|\geq |B(x,r)|\, (u(x)-a)/(M-a)>0$. As a singleton has Lebesgue measure zero, it is not a fine neighborhood of its point. $\square$

Proposition 5.1.5. Let $A\subset \Omega $ be polar. For every $x\in \Omega $ there is a fine neighborhood $N$ of $x$ such that $N\cap A\subset \{ x\} $. Consequently, $A$ is finely closed, and the topology induced on $A$ by the fine topology is discrete.

Proof. We construct a subharmonic function on $\mathbb R^ d$ that is finite at $x$ and equals $-\infty $ on $A\setminus \{ x\} $ near $x$. Fix $x\in \Omega $ and $\rho >0$ with $\overline{B(x,\rho )}\subset \Omega $. For $k\geq 0$ put $r_ k=2^{-k}\rho $, let $S_ k=\{ y:r_{k+1}\leq |y-x|\leq r_ k\} $ be the closed annulus, and let $A_ k=A\cap S_ k$; these sets cover $A\cap \overline{B(x,\rho )}\setminus \{ x\} $. Each $A_ k$ is polar and has compact closure, so proposition 4.2.3 provides a finite positive measure $\nu _ k'$ of compact support with $E_ d*\nu _ k'=-\infty $ on $A_ k$. We restrict it to the open set $U_ k=\{ y:r_{k+2}<|y-x|<2\rho \} $, which contains $S_ k$, and put $\nu _ k=\nu _ k'|_{U_ k}$. For $y\in A_ k$ and $z\in \operatorname{Supp}\nu _ k'\setminus U_ k$ we have $|y-z|\geq \operatorname {dist}(S_ k,\mathbb R^ d\setminus U_ k)>0$, so $E_ d(y-z)$ is bounded on this set of pairs and

\[ E_ d*\nu _ k(y)=E_ d*\nu _ k'(y)-\int _{\mathbb R^ d\setminus U_ k}E_ d(y-z)\, \mathrm d\nu _ k'(z)=-\infty ,\qquad y\in A_ k. \]

Moreover, $\nu _ k$ is supported in $\overline{U_ k}$, which does not meet $B(x,r_{k+2})$, so $p_ k=E_ d*\nu _ k$ is finite at $x$.

Choose $c_ k>0$ with

\[ \sum _ kc_ k\bigl (\nu _ k(\mathbb R^ d)+|p_ k(x)|\bigr )<\infty , \]

and put $\nu =\sum _ kc_ k\nu _ k$, a finite measure supported in $\overline{B(x,2\rho )}$, and $p=E_ d*\nu $. Let $C$ be an upper bound for $E_ d(y-z)$ when $|y-z|\leq 3\rho $. For $y\in \overline{B(x,\rho )}$, monotone convergence applied to the nonnegative functions $C-E_ d(y-\cdot )$ gives

\[ p(y)-C\nu (\mathbb R^ d)=\sum _ kc_ k\bigl (p_ k(y)-C\nu _ k(\mathbb R^ d)\bigr ), \]

where every term on the right is nonpositive. Hence $p(x)$ is finite, and $p=-\infty $ on $A\cap \overline{B(x,\rho )}\setminus \{ x\} $, because there one term equals $-\infty $. Since $p$ is subharmonic on $\mathbb R^ d$ (proposition 3.2.1), the set

\[ N=\{ y\in B(x,\rho ):p(y)>p(x)-1\} \]

is a fine neighborhood of $x$, and $N\cap A\subset \{ x\} $. If $x\notin A$, this shows that $\Omega \setminus A$ is a fine neighborhood of each of its points, so $A$ is finely closed. If $x\in A$, it shows that $\{ x\} $ is relatively open in $A$ for the fine topology. $\square$

Singletons are polar since $d\geq 2$, and countable unions of polar sets are polar by proposition 4.2.3; hence every countable set is polar. In particular, if $\Omega \neq \varnothing $, the set $\Omega \cap \mathbb Q^ d$ is finely closed but Euclidean dense in $\Omega $, so the fine topology is strictly finer than the Euclidean topology. As we show in example 5.1.7 below, the fine topology is not even Lindelöf; this is why the countability theorem of section 5.4 must allow a polar exceptional set. To produce uncountable compact polar sets in every dimension, we use the following observation: a subharmonic function of some of the variables is subharmonic in all of them.

Lemma 5.1.6. Let $1\leq k<d$, write $x=(z,y)\in \mathbb R^ k\times \mathbb R^{d-k}$, let $V\subset \mathbb R^ k$ be open, and let $g\in \mathrm{SH}(V)$. Then the function $G(z,y)=g(z)$ is subharmonic on $V\times \mathbb R^{d-k}$.

Proof. The function $G$ is upper semicontinuous, because $\{ G<a\} =\{ g<a\} \times \mathbb R^{d-k}$ for every real $a$. Since subharmonicity is a local property, it suffices to show that $G$ is subharmonic on $D\times \mathbb R^{d-k}$ for every open ball $D=B(z_0,s)\subset \mathbb R^ k$ with $\overline{B(z_0,2s)}\subset V$. If $g$ is identically $-\infty $ on $B(z_0,2s)$, then so is $G$ on $B(z_0,2s)\times \mathbb R^{d-k}$, and there is nothing to prove. Otherwise $g$ is locally integrable on the ball $B(z_0,2s)$ by proposition 3.1.5.

For $0<\varepsilon <s$, the $k$-dimensional mollification $g_\varepsilon =g*\rho _\varepsilon $ is smooth on $D$ and satisfies $\Delta _ zg_\varepsilon =(\Delta g)*\rho _\varepsilon \geq 0$ there, by part (1) of theorem 3.2.2 and parts (1) and (3) of proposition 1.4.3; moreover, $g_\varepsilon $ decreases to $g$ on $D$ as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, by (3.3). The function $G_\varepsilon (z,y)=g_\varepsilon (z)$ is smooth on $D\times \mathbb R^{d-k}$, with $\Delta _ xG_\varepsilon (z,y)=\Delta _ zg_\varepsilon (z)\geq 0$. By (2.4), its sphere means in $\mathbb R^ d$ increase with the radius, and they tend to its value at the center as the radius tends to zero, because $G_\varepsilon $ is continuous. Hence $G_\varepsilon $ satisfies the submean inequality and is subharmonic on $D\times \mathbb R^{d-k}$. Along the sequence $\varepsilon _ j=s/(j+1)$, the functions $G_{\varepsilon _ j}$ decrease to $G$, which is therefore subharmonic on $D\times \mathbb R^{d-k}$ by theorem 3.1.6. $\square$

Example 5.1.7. Every Euclidean ball in $\mathbb R^ d$ contains an uncountable compact polar set. Consequently, if $\Omega $ is nonempty, the fine topology on $\Omega $ is neither Lindelöf nor second countable.

Proof. A similarity $x\mapsto a+tx$ with $t>0$ preserves upper semicontinuity and sphere means, and hence maps polar sets to polar sets. It therefore suffices to find one uncountable compact polar subset of $\mathbb R^ d$. For $d=2$ we take the Cantor set $K$ of proposition 4.2.4. It is compact and polar, and it is uncountable because it carries an atom-free probability measure.

For $d>2$, write $x=(z,y)\in \mathbb R^2\times \mathbb R^{d-2}$. By lemma 5.1.6, applied with $k=2$ to the subharmonic function $p$ of proposition 4.2.4, the function $P(x)=p(z)$ is subharmonic on $\mathbb R^ d$. It is finite when $z\notin K$ and equals $-\infty $ on $K\times \mathbb R^{d-2}$. Hence $K\times \{ 0\} $ is an uncountable compact polar subset of $\mathbb R^ d$.

Now let $\Omega \neq \varnothing $, and let $K'\subset \Omega $ be an uncountable compact polar set. By proposition 5.1.5, $K'$ is finely closed, and its induced fine topology is discrete. A closed subspace $F$ of a Lindelöf space $X$ is Lindelöf: extend the members of an open cover of $F$ to open subsets of $X$, add $X\setminus F$, and pass to a countable subcover. On the other hand, the cover of an uncountable discrete space by its singletons has no countable subcover. Hence the fine topology on $\Omega $ is not Lindelöf. Nor is it second countable, because a second countable space is Lindelöf: given an open cover and a countable basis, choose, for each basis element contained in some member of the cover, one such member; these countably many members cover the space. $\square$

5.2 Green energy and compact reduction

This section prepares balayage: the Green energy is a positive definite quadratic form on signed measures of finite energy (proposition 5.2.2), and a regularized reduction on a compact set is the Green potential of a measure carried by that set (theorem 5.2.5).

Fix an open ball $B\subset \mathbb R^ d$ and let $G_ B$ be its Green function (theorem 2.5.2); it is nonnegative and symmetric (lemma 4.3.1). The results of chapter 4 about balls are stated for balls centered at the origin; we apply them to $B$ after a translation. In this section and the next, unless signed measures are explicitly mentioned, a measure means a finite positive Borel measure with compact support in $B$. Recall from section 4.3 that the Green potential of a measure $\mu $ is $G_ B\mu (x)=\int G_ B(x,y)\, \mathrm d\mu (y)$. We denote the mutual Green energy of measures $\mu $ and $\nu $ and the Green energy of $\mu $ (section 4.5) by

\[ I_ B(\mu ,\nu )=\iint G_ B(x,y)\, \mathrm d\mu (x)\, \mathrm d\nu (y),\qquad I_ B(\mu )=I_ B(\mu ,\mu ). \]

By lemma 4.3.1, $G_ B(x,y)-\Gamma _ d(x-y)$ is continuous on $B\times B$, so $G_ B$ is a continuous function on $B\times B$ with values in $[0,\infty ]$, and these integrals are defined in $[0,\infty ]$ by Tonelli's theorem. For finite signed measures we use the same formulas whenever $G_ B$ is integrable for the product of the total variations.

Let $\mu $ be supported in a compact set $K\subset B$. By the definition of $G_ B$ in theorem 2.5.2 and by theorem 2.3.1, the harmonic correction $G_ B(x,y)-\Gamma _ d(x-y)=-H_ B(\Gamma _ d(\cdot -x)|_{\partial B})(y)$ has absolute value at most $\max _{\zeta \in \partial B}|\Gamma _ d(\zeta -x)|$, which is bounded for $x\in K$ because $K$ has positive distance from $\partial B$. Hence, on $K\times K$, the Green function differs by a bounded function from the kernel $k$ of section 4.1 if $d\geq 3$, and from the kernel $k_ D$ with $D>\operatorname {diam}K$ if $d=2$, because $k(x,y)=\Gamma _ d(x-y)$ and $k_ D(x,y)-\Gamma _2(x-y)$ is constant. Consequently $\mu $ has finite Green energy if and only if it has finite energy in the sense of section 4.1. In particular, by theorem 4.3.3, a measure $\mu $ of finite Green energy does not charge polar sets: every polar set is contained in a Borel set of $\mu $-measure zero.

Definition 5.2.1. A property holds quasi-everywhere on a set $E\subset \mathbb R^ d$ if it holds at every point of $E$ outside a polar set.

Thus a property that holds quasi-everywhere on a compact set $K\subset B$ holds $\mu $-almost everywhere for every measure $\mu $ on $K$ with finite Green energy. In 2 the exceptional sets are pluripolar, and quasi-everywhere will mean outside a pluripolar set (definition 10.1.1).

Proposition 5.2.2. Let $\mu $ and $\nu $ be measures with finite Green energy. Then the distributional gradients $\nabla G_ B\mu $ and $\nabla G_ B\nu $ belong to $L^2(B)$, the mutual energy $I_ B(\mu ,\nu )$ is finite, and

\[ I_ B(\mu ,\nu )=\int _ B\nabla G_ B\mu \mathbin {\cdot }\nabla G_ B\nu \, \mathrm d x. \]

The same identity holds for finite signed measures whose positive and negative parts have finite Green energy. On such signed measures, $I_ B$ is a positive definite quadratic form, that is, $I_ B(\sigma )\geq 0$ with equality only for $\sigma =0$, and it satisfies the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $I_ B(\sigma ,\tau )^2\leq I_ B(\sigma )I_ B(\tau )$. Moreover, every measure $\mu $ with finite Green energy is the sum of its restrictions $\mu ^{(1)},\mu ^{(2)},\ldots $ to countably many pairwise disjoint compact sets, and for every $n$ the Green potential of $\mu ^{(1)}+\cdots +\mu ^{(n)}$ is finite, continuous and bounded on $B$.

Proof. We first prove the gradient identity for the partial sums of a decomposition of $\mu $ and $\nu $ into pieces with bounded Green potentials, to which proposition 4.5.1 applies, and then pass to the limit. Translating, we may assume that $B=B(0,R)$ for some $R>0$, and we choose $r<R$ such that the supports of $\mu $ and $\nu $ lie in $B(0,r)$. By lemma 4.3.1, the function $\kappa (x,y)=G_ B(x,y)-\Gamma _ d(x-y)$ extends to a continuous function on the compact set $\overline B\times \overline{B(0,r)}$; let $C$ be a bound for $|\kappa |$ there. Since $\Gamma _ d=-E_ d$, every finite positive measure $\lambda $ supported in $B(0,r)$ satisfies

\[ G_ B\lambda (x)=-E_ d*\lambda (x)+\int \kappa (x,y)\, \mathrm d\lambda (y),\qquad x\in B, \]

and the last integral is a continuous function of $x\in \overline B$ bounded by $C\lambda (B)$.

Step 1: decomposition. Since $\int G_ B\mu \, \mathrm d\mu =I_ B(\mu )<\infty $, the Green potential $G_ B\mu $ is finite $\mu $-almost everywhere, and by the display so is $E_ d*\mu $. By part (2) of theorem 4.2.2, $\mu $ is therefore the sum of its restrictions to countably many disjoint compact sets, each of which has a finite and continuous fundamental potential. Let $\mu _ n$ be the sum of the first $n$ of these restrictions. Since $E_ d*\mu _ n$ is finite and continuous, the display shows that $G_ B\mu _ n$ is finite, continuous and bounded on $B$; this proves the last assertion of the proposition. By part (2) of proposition 4.3.2, $-G_ B\mu _ n$ is a subharmonic function on $B$ with Riesz measure $\mu _ n$, which extends harmonically across $\partial B$ and vanishes on $\partial B$; in particular, it tends to zero at $\partial B$. Thus proposition 4.5.1 applies to the functions $-G_ B\mu _ n$, $n\geq 1$. Hence $\nabla G_ B\mu _ n\in L^2(B)$ and

\[ \int _ B\nabla G_ B\mu _ n\mathbin {\cdot }\nabla G_ B\mu _ m\, \mathrm d x=I_ B(\mu _ n,\mu _ m). \]

Step 2: the gradient of $G_ B\mu $. For $m>n$, expanding the square with the last display gives

\[ \| \nabla G_ B\mu _ m-\nabla G_ B\mu _ n\| _{L^2(B)}^2=I_ B(\mu _ m-\mu _ n)\leq I_ B(\mu -\mu _ n), \]

where the inequality holds because $0\leq \mu _ m-\mu _ n\leq \mu -\mu _ n$ and $G_ B\geq 0$. The measure $\mu -\mu _ n$ is the restriction of $\mu $ to a decreasing sequence of sets whose intersection is $\mu $-null. Since $G_ B$ is nonnegative and integrable for $\mu \otimes \mu $, dominated convergence gives $I_ B(\mu -\mu _ n)\to 0$. Thus $(\nabla G_ B\mu _ n)$ is a Cauchy sequence in $L^2(B)$; let $g$ be its limit.

On the other hand, $G_ B\mu _ n\uparrow G_ B\mu $ pointwise by monotone convergence, and $G_ B\mu \in L^1(B)$. Indeed, for $y\in \operatorname{Supp}\mu $, the bound $|\kappa |\leq C$ and the local integrability of $\Gamma _ d$ (proposition 2.2.1) give

\[ \int _ BG_ B(x,y)\, \mathrm d x\leq \int _ B\bigl (|\Gamma _ d(x-y)|+C\bigr )\, \mathrm d x\leq \int _{B(0,2R)}|\Gamma _ d(z)|\, \mathrm d z+C|B|<\infty , \]

and integrating against $\mu $, by Tonelli's theorem, shows that $\int _ BG_ B\mu \, \mathrm d x$ is at most $\mu (B)$ times the right side. Hence $G_ B\mu _ n\to G_ B\mu $ in $L^1(B)$ by monotone convergence. For a smooth compactly supported vector field $\varphi $ on $B$ we obtain

\[ \int _ Bg\mathbin {\cdot }\varphi \, \mathrm d x=\lim _ n\int _ B\nabla G_ B\mu _ n\mathbin {\cdot }\varphi \, \mathrm d x=-\lim _ n\int _ BG_ B\mu _ n\operatorname {div}\varphi \, \mathrm d x=-\int _ BG_ B\mu \operatorname {div}\varphi \, \mathrm d x, \]

so $g=\nabla G_ B\mu $. Since $\| \nabla G_ B\mu _ n\| _2^2=I_ B(\mu _ n)\uparrow I_ B(\mu )$ by monotone convergence, we get $\| \nabla G_ B\mu \| _2^2=I_ B(\mu )$.

Step 3: the mutual energy. Construct the partial sums $\nu _ n$ of $\nu $ in the same way. By proposition 4.5.1 and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in $L^2(B)$,

\[ I_ B(\mu _ n,\nu _ n)=\int _ B\nabla G_ B\mu _ n\mathbin {\cdot }\nabla G_ B\nu _ n\, \mathrm d x\leq I_ B(\mu _ n)^{1/2}I_ B(\nu _ n)^{1/2}\leq I_ B(\mu )^{1/2}I_ B(\nu )^{1/2}. \]

As $n\to \infty $, the left side increases to $I_ B(\mu ,\nu )$ by monotone convergence, so the mutual energy is finite, while the integral converges to $\int _ B\nabla G_ B\mu \mathbin {\cdot }\nabla G_ B\nu \, \mathrm d x$ by convergence in $L^2(B)$. This proves the identity for positive measures. For signed measures, we expand both sides bilinearly in the positive and negative parts.

Step 4: positive definiteness. Let $\sigma $ be a signed measure as in the statement, and put $G_ B\sigma =G_ B\sigma ^+-G_ B\sigma ^-$, a function in $L^1(B)$ by Step 2. Then $I_ B(\sigma )=\| \nabla G_ B\sigma \| _2^2\geq 0$. Suppose that $I_ B(\sigma )=0$, so that $\nabla G_ B\sigma =0$ as a distribution on $B$. Since $B$ is connected, corollary 1.4.4 shows that the distribution defined by $G_ B\sigma $ is a constant. On the other hand, $-\Delta G_ B\sigma =\sigma $ in the sense of distributions, by part (2) of proposition 4.3.2. Hence $\sigma ^+$ and $\sigma ^-$, which are Radon measures by proposition 1.1.2, define the same distribution, so $\sigma ^+=\sigma ^-$ by part (4) of proposition 1.4.3, that is, $\sigma =0$. Finally, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for $I_ B$ follows from the gradient identity and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in $L^2(B)$. $\square$

Recall from section 4.3 the relative extremal function $u_ K$ of a compact set $K\subset B$, its Riesz measure $\lambda _ K=\Delta u_ K$, the relative capacity $C_ B(K)=\lambda _ K(K)$, and the outer capacity $C_ B^*$.

Proposition 5.2.3. Let $K\subset B$ be compact. Then

\[ C_ B(K)=\left(\inf _{\substack {\tau \geq 0,\ \operatorname{Supp}\tau \subset K\\ \tau (K)=1}} I_ B(\tau )\right)^{-1}, \]

with the conventions that the infimum of an empty family is $+\infty $ and that $1/(+\infty )=0$. If $C_ B(K)>0$, then $\lambda _ K/C_ B(K)$ is the unique probability measure on $K$ minimizing $I_ B$. Moreover, if $D\subset B$ are open balls and $A\Subset D$, then $C_ B^*(A)\leq C_ D^*(A)$.

Proof. By part (3) of proposition 4.3.2, the function $w_ K=-u_ K$ is the Green potential $G_ B\lambda _ K$, and $0\leq w_ K\leq 1$ because $-1\leq u_ K\leq 0$. Since $u_ K$ is the upper semicontinuous regularization of the envelope $f_ K$, which equals $-1$ on $K$, part (2) of theorem 4.4.4 shows that $w_ K=1$ quasi-everywhere on $K$. In particular $I_ B(\lambda _ K)=\int w_ K\, \mathrm d\lambda _ K\leq \lambda _ K(K)$, so $\lambda _ K$ has finite Green energy. As measures of finite Green energy do not charge polar sets, we obtain, with $m=C_ B(K)$,

\[ I_ B(\lambda _ K)=\int _ Kw_ K\, \mathrm d\lambda _ K=m,\qquad I_ B(\lambda _ K,\tau )=\int _ Kw_ K\, \mathrm d\tau =1 \]

for every probability measure $\tau $ on $K$ with finite Green energy; in the second identity we used the symmetry of $G_ B$.

Suppose that $m>0$, and let $\tau $ be a probability measure on $K$. If $I_ B(\tau )<\infty $, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality of proposition 5.2.2 gives $1=I_ B(\lambda _ K,\tau )^2\leq mI_ B(\tau )$; thus $I_ B(\tau )\geq 1/m$ in all cases. Equality holds for $\tau =\lambda _ K/m$, whose energy is $m/m^2=1/m$. Hence the infimum equals $1/m$, which proves the formula. If $\tau _1\neq \tau _2$ were two minimizers, their midpoint would again be a probability measure on $K$, and by positive definiteness

\[ I_ B\Bigl (\frac{\tau _1+\tau _2}{2}\Bigr )=\frac{I_ B(\tau _1)+I_ B(\tau _2)}{2}-\frac{I_ B(\tau _1-\tau _2)}{4}<\frac1m, \]

a contradiction. Suppose next that $m=0$. Then $K$ is polar by theorem 4.3.3, so it carries no probability measure of finite energy, and the infimum is $+\infty $ (if $K=\varnothing $, the family is empty).

For the last assertion, recall that balls are regular domains (theorem 2.4.6). The monotonicity of Green functions in the domain (theorem 2.5.2) gives $G_ D\leq G_ B$ on $D\times D$. Hence $I_ B(\tau )\geq I_ D(\tau )$ for every measure $\tau $ supported on a compact set $K\subset D$, and the variational formula, applied in $B$ and in $D$, gives $C_ B(K)\leq C_ D(K)$. Taking suprema over compact subsets, we obtain $C_ B(O)\leq C_ D(O)$ for every open $O\Subset D$. Every such $O$ containing $A$ is also allowed in the infimum defining $C_ B^*(A)$, so $C_ B^*(A)\leq C_ D^*(A)$. $\square$

We now turn to reduction. Let $U$ be an open set, $s$ a nonnegative superharmonic function on $U$, and $A\subset U$ an arbitrary subset. The reduction of $s$ on $A$, also called the réduite, and the regularized reduction are

\[ R_ s^ A=\inf \{ h:h\geq 0\text{ is superharmonic on }U,\ h\geq s\text{ on }A\} , \qquad \widehat R_ s^ A=(R_ s^ A)_*. \]

Here $F_*=-(-F)^*$, with $(-F)^*$ the upper semicontinuous regularization of section 3.4, is the lower semicontinuous regularization of a function $F$ that is locally bounded below; explicitly, $F_*(x)=\sup _{r>0}\inf _{B(x,r)\cap U}F$. We call the functions $h$ in this infimum admissible for $R_ s^ A$; the inequality $h\geq s$ is required at every point of $A$. If $U=B$, $s=1$ and $A\Subset B$, the admissible functions are exactly the negatives of the functions admissible for $A$ in the sense of section 4.3, so that $R_1^ A=-f_ A$ and $\widehat R_1^ A=-u_ A$.

Since $s$ itself is admissible, $0\leq R_ s^ A\leq s$, and $R_ s^ A=s$ on all of $A$. The negatives of the admissible functions form a family of subharmonic functions bounded above by zero, whose supremum is $-R_ s^ A$. Since $(-R_ s^ A)^*=-\widehat R_ s^ A$, theorem 3.4.2 shows that $\widehat R_ s^ A$ is superharmonic, and part (2) of theorem 4.4.4 shows that $R_ s^ A=\widehat R_ s^ A$ outside a polar set. Thus $\widehat R_ s^ A=s$ quasi-everywhere on $A$, though in general not everywhere on $A$: if $U$ is bounded, $s=1$ and $A\subset U$ is a nonempty polar set, then $\widehat R_ s^ A=0$ by proposition 5.2.4 below.

The ball-average formula (3.3) holds for every subharmonic function, integrable or not. Applied to $-w$, it shows that every superharmonic function $w$ on an open set satisfies

\[ w(x)=\lim _{r\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B(x,r)|}\int _{B(x,r)}w(y)\, \mathrm d y \]

at every point $x$ of that set. Consequently, two superharmonic functions satisfying an inequality almost everywhere satisfy it everywhere.

Proposition 5.2.4. Let $s$ and $t$ be nonnegative superharmonic functions on an open set $U$, let $A\subset A'\subset U$, and let $c\geq 0$. Then

\[ R_ s^ A\leq R_ s^{A'},\qquad R_{cs}^ A=cR_ s^ A,\qquad R_{s+t}^ A\leq R_ s^ A+R_ t^ A, \]

and $R_ s^ A\leq R_ t^ A$ if $s\leq t$. The same relations hold for the regularized reductions; in particular,

\[ \widehat R_{s+t}^ A\leq \widehat R_ s^ A+\widehat R_ t^ A. \]

If $U$ is bounded and $A$ is polar, then there is a locally integrable nonnegative superharmonic function $p$ on $U$ with $p=+\infty $ on $A$, and $\widehat R_ s^ A=0$.

Proof. Enlarging $A$ or $s$ shrinks the class of admissible functions, which proves the two monotonicity assertions. For $c>0$, multiplication by $c$ is a bijection between the admissible functions for $s$ and those for $cs$; for $c=0$, both sides vanish (with $0\cdot \infty =0$), since the zero function is admissible. If $h_1$ and $h_2$ are admissible for $s$ and $t$ respectively, then $h_1+h_2$ is admissible for $s+t$, so $R_{s+t}^ A\leq R_ s^ A+R_ t^ A$. Monotonicity and homogeneity are preserved by lower semicontinuous regularization. For subadditivity, recall that each reduction agrees with its regularization outside a polar set, hence almost everywhere. Therefore $\widehat R_{s+t}^ A\leq \widehat R_ s^ A+\widehat R_ t^ A$ almost everywhere. Both sides are superharmonic, so the inequality holds everywhere by the ball-average formula above.

Now let $U$ be bounded and $A$ polar. Then $A$ has compact closure, so proposition 4.2.3 provides a finite positive measure $\nu $ of compact support with $E_ d*\nu =-\infty $ on $A$. The kernel $E_ d$ is bounded above on the bounded set of differences $x-y$ with $x\in U$ and $y\in \operatorname{Supp}\nu $, so adding a constant to $-E_ d*\nu $ gives a nonnegative superharmonic function $p$ on $U$ with $p=+\infty $ on $A$; by proposition 3.2.1, $p$ is locally integrable. For every $\varepsilon >0$, the function $\varepsilon p$ is admissible, even at points of $A$ where $s=+\infty $. Hence $R_ s^ A\leq \varepsilon p$ for every $\varepsilon >0$, and so $R_ s^ A=0$ on the set $\{ p<\infty \} $. This set has full Lebesgue measure, because $p$ is locally integrable, and is therefore dense in $U$. Consequently $\widehat R_ s^ A=0$. $\square$

Theorem 5.2.5. Let $A\subset B$ be compact, and let $s$ be a nonnegative superharmonic function on $B$ which is bounded on $A$. Then $r=\widehat R_ s^ A$ is bounded, harmonic on $B\setminus A$, and tends to zero at $\partial B$. Moreover, there is a finite positive measure $\nu $ supported on $A$, with finite Green energy, such that

\[ r=G_ B\nu \quad \text{on }B,\qquad r=s\quad \text{quasi-everywhere on }A. \]

Proof. We show that $r$ is harmonic off $A$ by replacing admissible functions with their Poisson integrals on balls in $B\setminus A$ (Steps 1–3), and then identify $r$ with the Green potential of its Riesz measure (Step 4).

Step 1: capped admissible functions. Choose a closed ball $L\subset B$ whose interior contains $A$, and a constant $M\geq 0$ with $s\leq M$ on $A$. The relative extremal function $u_ L$ takes values in $[-1,0]$ and tends to zero at $\partial B$ by part (1) of proposition 4.3.2; it equals $-1$ on $\operatorname{Int}L$, because it is the upper semicontinuous regularization of the envelope $f_ L$, which equals $-1$ on $L$. Hence $h_0=-Mu_ L$ is a nonnegative superharmonic function on $B$, bounded by $M$, which majorizes $s$ on $A$ and tends to zero at $\partial B$. If $h$ is admissible, so is $\min \{ h,h_0\} $. Therefore $R_ s^ A$ is also the infimum of the admissible functions $h$ with $h\leq h_0$; we call these capped. In particular $0\leq r\leq R_ s^ A\leq h_0\leq M$, so $r$ is bounded and tends to zero at $\partial B$.

Step 2: Poisson modification. Let $D$ be an open ball with $\overline D\subset B\setminus A$, and let $h$ be a capped admissible function. Its restriction to $\partial D$ is bounded and lower semicontinuous, so by lemma 3.1.2, applied to $-h$, it is the increasing limit of continuous functions $g_ k$ on $\partial D$. By monotone convergence, the Poisson integrals $H_ Dg_ k$ of theorem 2.3.1 increase to

\[ H(y)=\int _{\partial D}P_ D(y,\zeta )h(\zeta )\, \mathrm d S(\zeta ),\qquad y\in D. \]

Since $0\leq h\leq M$ and the Poisson kernel has integral one, $0\leq H\leq M$, and $H$ is harmonic by part (2) of corollary 2.3.3. For $\zeta \in \partial D$, lower semicontinuity gives $\varliminf _{y\to \zeta }h(y)\geq h(\zeta )\geq g_ k(\zeta )$, so the comparison principle for the superharmonic function $h$ (theorem 3.1.3) gives $H_ Dg_ k\leq h$ on $D$; hence $H\leq h$ on $D$. On the other hand, $H\geq H_ Dg_ k$, and $H_ Dg_ k$ extends continuously to $\overline D$ with boundary values $g_ k$; letting $k\to \infty $, we get

\[ \varliminf _{D\ni y\to \zeta }H(y)\geq h(\zeta ),\qquad \zeta \in \partial D. \]

Define $h^ D=H$ on $D$ and $h^ D=h$ on $B\setminus D$. The last inequality and the lower semicontinuity of $h$ show that $h^ D$ is lower semicontinuous, and $0\leq h^ D\leq h$.

We check that $h^ D$ is superharmonic, using the converse part of theorem 3.1.3. Let $E$ be a ball with $\overline E\subset B$, and let $q$ be harmonic on $E$ and continuous on $\overline E$ with $\varliminf _{E\ni y\to \zeta }h^ D(y)\geq q(\zeta )$ for $\zeta \in \partial E$. Since $h\geq h^ D$, the function $h$ satisfies the same boundary inequality, so $h\geq q$ on $E$. In particular $h^ D=h\geq q$ on $E\setminus D$. On the open set $E\cap D$ consider the harmonic function $H-q$. At boundary points of $E\cap D$ lying on $\partial E$, its lower limits are nonnegative by the hypothesis on $q$. At boundary points $\zeta \in E\cap \partial D$, they are at least $h(\zeta )-q(\zeta )\geq 0$ by the inequality displayed above. The minimum principle (proposition 2.1.3) gives $H\geq q$ on $E\cap D$. Hence $h^ D\geq q$ on $E$, and $h^ D$ is superharmonic. Since $D\cap A=\varnothing $, the function $h^ D$ is again a capped admissible function. Finally, $h^ D$ depends monotonically on $h$, by the integral formula for $H$.

Step 3: harmonicity off $A$. By Choquet's lemma (lemma 3.4.1), applied to the negatives of the capped admissible functions, there is a sequence of capped admissible functions whose infimum has the same lower semicontinuous regularization $r$ as $R_ s^ A$. By theorem 3.4.2, applied to the negatives of this sequence, the infimum equals $r$ almost everywhere. Replacing the functions of the sequence by their successive minima, we obtain a decreasing sequence $(h_ j)$ of capped admissible functions whose limit equals $r$ almost everywhere.

Now fix a ball $D$ as in Step 2. On $D$, the modified functions $h_ j^ D$ are harmonic, decrease, and lie between $0$ and $M$; by part (3) of corollary 2.3.3, they converge on $D$ to a harmonic function $\Phi $. Each $h_ j^ D$ is admissible, so $h_ j^ D\geq R_ s^ A\geq r$; moreover $h_ j^ D\leq h_ j$. Thus $r\leq \Phi \leq \lim _ jh_ j=r$ almost everywhere on $D$. The bounded superharmonic functions $r$ and $\Phi $ therefore agree almost everywhere, hence everywhere on $D$ by theorem 3.2.2. Since such balls cover $B\setminus A$, the function $r$ is harmonic on $B\setminus A$.

Step 4: Green representation. The function $-r$ is bounded and subharmonic on $B$, so $\nu =-\Delta r$ is a positive measure by part (1) of theorem 3.2.2. As $r$ is harmonic on $B\setminus A$, the measure $\nu $ is supported on the compact set $A$ and is therefore finite. The function $-r$ is bounded, hence locally integrable, its Riesz measure $\nu $ has compact support in $B$, and it tends to zero at $\partial B$; so the converse in part (2) of proposition 4.3.2 gives $-r=-G_ B\nu $, that is, $r=G_ B\nu $ on $B$. The reduction $R_ s^ A$ equals $s$ on $A$, and $r=R_ s^ A$ outside a polar set, so $r=s$ quasi-everywhere on $A$. Finally $I_ B(\nu )=\int r\, \mathrm d\nu \leq M\nu (A)<\infty $. $\square$

5.3 Sweeping measures and harmonic measure

We keep the ball $B$ and the conventions of section 5.2. Applied to the Green potential of a measure, theorem 5.2.5 sweeps the measure onto a compact set without changing its potential there, apart from a polar set.

The measure obtained in this way is characterized by an energy identity, and the uniqueness part of this characterization rests on the following consequence of proposition 5.2.2. Let $\nu _1$ and $\nu _2$ be measures with finite Green energy such that $I_ B(\nu _1,\tau )=I_ B(\nu _2,\tau )$ for $\tau =\nu _1$ and for $\tau =\nu _2$. Then $\nu _1=\nu _2$. Indeed, the mutual energies $I_ B(\nu _ i,\nu _ k)$ are finite by proposition 5.2.2, so the hypothesis gives $I_ B(\nu _1-\nu _2)=I_ B(\nu _1-\nu _2,\nu _1)-I_ B(\nu _1-\nu _2,\nu _2)=0$. The positive and negative parts of $\nu _1-\nu _2$, being dominated by $\nu _1$ and $\nu _2$ respectively, have finite Green energy because $G_ B\geq 0$, and $\nu _1=\nu _2$ by positive definiteness.

Theorem 5.3.1. Let $A\subset B$ be compact, and let $\mu $ be a measure such that $G_ B\mu $ is bounded on $A$. Then there is a unique positive measure $\mu ^ A$ supported on $A$ whose Green potential is $\widehat R_{G_ B\mu }^ A$. It has finite Green energy and satisfies

\[ G_ B\mu ^ A\leq G_ B\mu ,\qquad G_ B\mu ^ A=G_ B\mu \text{ quasi-everywhere on }A, \qquad \mu ^ A(A)\leq \mu (B). \]

For every positive measure $\tau $ supported on $A$ with finite Green energy,

5.1
\begin{equation} I_ B(\mu ,\tau )=I_ B(\mu ^ A,\tau ). \end{equation}

This identity characterizes $\mu ^ A$ among the positive measures on $A$ with finite Green energy. Consequently, sweeping is additive and positively homogeneous: if $\mu _1$ and $\mu _2$ satisfy the boundedness hypothesis, then $(\mu _1+\mu _2)^ A=\mu _1^ A+\mu _2^ A$ and $(c\mu _1)^ A=c\mu _1^ A$ for $c\geq 0$.

Proof. The function $G_ B\mu $ is nonnegative, superharmonic by part (2) of proposition 4.3.2, and bounded on $A$. Applying theorem 5.2.5 to $s=G_ B\mu $, we see that $\widehat R_{G_ B\mu }^ A$ is the Green potential of a positive measure $\mu ^ A$ on $A$ with finite Green energy, and that $G_ B\mu ^ A=G_ B\mu $ quasi-everywhere on $A$. Since $G_ B\mu $ is itself admissible, $G_ B\mu ^ A=\widehat R_{G_ B\mu }^ A\leq G_ B\mu $. The measure $\mu ^ A$ is unique because $-\Delta G_ B\nu =\nu $ for every measure $\nu $, by part (2) of proposition 4.3.2, so that a measure is determined by its Green potential. Since $-G_ B\mu \leq -G_ B\mu ^ A$ on $B$, the comparison of boundary fluxes in part (2) of proposition 4.3.2, applied with $\lambda =\mu $ and $\lambda '=\mu ^ A$, gives the mass bound $\mu ^ A(A)\leq \mu (B)$.

Let $\tau $ be a positive measure on $A$ with finite Green energy. It does not charge polar sets, so we may integrate the equality $G_ B\mu =G_ B\mu ^ A$, valid quasi-everywhere on $A$, against $\tau $. Both integrals are finite, since $0\leq G_ B\mu ^ A\leq G_ B\mu $ and $G_ B\mu $ is bounded on $A$. By Tonelli's theorem and the symmetry of $G_ B$, the two integrals are $I_ B(\mu ,\tau )$ and $I_ B(\mu ^ A,\tau )$, which proves (5.1).

Suppose that $\nu _1$ and $\nu _2$ are positive measures on $A$ with finite Green energy which both satisfy (5.1) in place of $\mu ^ A$. Then $I_ B(\nu _1,\tau )=I_ B(\mu ,\tau )=I_ B(\nu _2,\tau )$ for $\tau =\nu _1$ and $\tau =\nu _2$, so $\nu _1=\nu _2$ by the uniqueness criterion at the beginning of this section. If $\mu _1$ and $\mu _2$ satisfy the boundedness hypothesis, so does $\mu _1+\mu _2$, and $\mu _1^ A+\mu _2^ A$ is a positive measure on $A$ with finite Green energy satisfying (5.1) for the source $\mu _1+\mu _2$. By the characterization, $(\mu _1+\mu _2)^ A=\mu _1^ A+\mu _2^ A$. The same argument gives $(c\mu _1)^ A=c\mu _1^ A$. $\square$

The measure $\mu ^ A$ is called the balayage, or swept measure, of $\mu $ onto $A$, relative to $B$. We now replace the boundedness of $G_ B\mu $ on $A$ by the assumption that $\mu $ has finite Green energy.

Theorem 5.3.2. Let $A\subset B$ be compact, and let $\mu $ be a measure with finite Green energy. Then the regularized reduction $\widehat R_{G_ B\mu }^ A$ is the Green potential of a unique positive measure $\mu ^ A$ supported on $A$, and $\mu ^ A$ has finite Green energy. All the conclusions of theorem 5.3.1 remain valid, additivity and homogeneity now holding for sources of finite Green energy. Moreover,

\[ I_ B(\mu ,\mu ^ A)=I_ B(\mu ^ A),\qquad I_ B(\mu -\mu ^ A)=I_ B(\mu )-I_ B(\mu ^ A)\geq 0. \]

If $\mu $ is supported on $A$, then $\mu ^ A=\mu $.

Proof. We sweep the partial sums of a decomposition of $\mu $ into pieces with bounded Green potentials, show that the swept measures increase to a measure $\nu $ satisfying (5.1), and then identify $G_ B\nu $ with the regularized reduction. By the last assertion of proposition 5.2.2, $\mu $ is the sum of its restrictions to countably many pairwise disjoint compact sets, and the partial sums $\mu _ n$ of this series, which satisfy $\mu _ n\uparrow \mu $, have bounded Green potentials. Hence theorem 5.3.1 applies to the measures $\mu _ n$ and to the differences $\mu _ m-\mu _ n$ with $m>n$, whose Green potentials are bounded by $G_ B\mu _ m$.

By additivity, $\mu _ m^ A=\mu _ n^ A+(\mu _ m-\mu _ n)^ A\geq \mu _ n^ A$. Hence the measures $\mu _ n^ A$ increase setwise, and $\nu (E)=\lim _ n\mu _ n^ A(E)$ defines a measure $\nu $ on $A$: if a Borel set $E$ is the disjoint union of Borel sets $E_ k$, the monotone convergence theorem for series gives $\nu (E)=\lim _ n\sum _ k\mu _ n^ A(E_ k)=\sum _ k\nu (E_ k)$. Moreover $\nu (A)=\lim _ n\mu _ n^ A(A)\leq \mu (B)$. For every nonnegative Borel function $f$ we have

5.2
\begin{equation} \int f\, \mathrm d\mu _ n^ A\uparrow \int f\, \mathrm d\nu \qquad (n\to \infty ). \end{equation}

This is clear for simple functions, and in general it follows by approximating $f$ from below by an increasing sequence of simple functions $f_ k$ and interchanging the suprema over $k$ and $n$ of $\int f_ k\, \mathrm d\mu _ n^ A$.

Testing (5.1) for $\mu _ n$ against $\tau =\mu _ n^ A$ and using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we get

\[ I_ B(\mu _ n^ A)=I_ B(\mu _ n,\mu _ n^ A)\leq I_ B(\mu _ n)^{1/2}I_ B(\mu _ n^ A)^{1/2}, \]

so that $I_ B(\mu _ n^ A)\leq I_ B(\mu _ n)\leq I_ B(\mu )$. We claim that $I_ B(\mu _ n^ A)\uparrow I_ B(\nu )$. The sequence increases and is bounded by $I_ B(\nu )$, because $\mu _ n^ A\leq \mu _{n+1}^ A\leq \nu $ and $G_ B\geq 0$. Conversely, applying (5.2) to $f=G_ B(x,\cdot )$, we get $G_ B\mu _ m^ A\uparrow G_ B\nu $ pointwise as $m\to \infty $. For $m\leq n$ we have $I_ B(\mu _ n^ A)\geq \int G_ B\mu _ m^ A\, \mathrm d\mu _ n^ A$, and by (5.2) the right side tends to $\int G_ B\mu _ m^ A\, \mathrm d\nu $ as $n\to \infty $; letting $m\to \infty $ and using monotone convergence for the fixed measure $\nu $, we obtain $\lim _ nI_ B(\mu _ n^ A)\geq \int G_ B\nu \, \mathrm d\nu =I_ B(\nu )$. This proves the claim, and hence $I_ B(\nu )\leq I_ B(\mu )<\infty $.

Now let $\tau $ be a positive measure on $A$ with finite Green energy. We have $I_ B(\mu _ n,\tau )=I_ B(\mu _ n^ A,\tau )$ for every $n$. As $n\to \infty $, the left side $\int G_ B\tau \, \mathrm d\mu _ n$ increases to $\int G_ B\tau \, \mathrm d\mu =I_ B(\mu ,\tau )$ by monotone convergence, since $\mu _ n$ is the restriction of $\mu $ to an increasing sequence of sets whose union carries $\mu $, and the right side $\int G_ B\tau \, \mathrm d\mu _ n^ A$ increases to $I_ B(\nu ,\tau )$ by (5.2). Hence $I_ B(\mu ,\tau )=I_ B(\nu ,\tau )$, which is (5.1) for $\nu $.

By monotone convergence, $G_ B\mu _ n\uparrow G_ B\mu $ pointwise on $B$, and we have seen that $G_ B\mu _ n^ A\uparrow G_ B\nu $. Since $G_ B\mu _ n^ A\leq G_ B\mu _ n$, it follows that $G_ B\nu \leq G_ B\mu $ everywhere. Moreover, $G_ B\mu _ n^ A=G_ B\mu _ n$ on $A$ outside a polar set $E_ n$, so $G_ B\nu =G_ B\mu $ on $A\setminus E$, where $E=\bigcup _ nE_ n$ is polar by proposition 4.2.3.

We now identify $\widehat R_{G_ B\mu }^ A$ with $G_ B\nu $. Since $B$ is bounded and $E$ is polar, proposition 5.2.4, applied with $U=B$ and with $E$ in place of $A$, provides a nonnegative superharmonic function $p$ on $B$, locally integrable and hence finite almost everywhere, with $p=+\infty $ on $E$. For every $\varepsilon >0$, the function $G_ B\nu +\varepsilon p$ is nonnegative and superharmonic, and it is at least $G_ B\mu $ at every point of $A$: on $A\setminus E$ because $G_ B\nu =G_ B\mu $ there, and on $E$ because $p=+\infty $. Hence $R_{G_ B\mu }^ A\leq G_ B\nu +\varepsilon p$. Letting $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, we get $R_{G_ B\mu }^ A\leq G_ B\nu $ wherever $p<\infty $, that is, almost everywhere. Thus $\widehat R_{G_ B\mu }^ A\leq G_ B\nu $ almost everywhere, and hence everywhere by the ball-average formula preceding proposition 5.2.4, since both functions are superharmonic. Conversely, $G_ B\mu _ n\leq G_ B\mu $, so proposition 5.2.4 gives $\widehat R_{G_ B\mu }^ A\geq \widehat R_{G_ B\mu _ n}^ A=G_ B\mu _ n^ A$ for every $n$, and therefore $\widehat R_{G_ B\mu }^ A\geq G_ B\nu $. Consequently $\widehat R_{G_ B\mu }^ A=G_ B\nu $, and we put $\mu ^ A=\nu $; this measure is unique because a measure is determined by its Green potential.

It remains to collect the other conclusions. The inequality $G_ B\mu ^ A\leq G_ B\mu $, the equality quasi-everywhere on $A$, the mass bound and the orthogonality relation have been proved above. If $\nu _1$ and $\nu _2$ are positive measures on $A$ with finite Green energy satisfying (5.1) in place of $\mu ^ A$, then $I_ B(\nu _1,\tau )=I_ B(\mu ,\tau )=I_ B(\nu _2,\tau )$ for $\tau =\nu _1$ and $\tau =\nu _2$, so $\nu _1=\nu _2$ by the uniqueness criterion at the beginning of this section; thus (5.1) characterizes $\mu ^ A$. If $\mu _1$ and $\mu _2$ have finite Green energy, so has $\mu _1+\mu _2$, because the mutual energy $I_ B(\mu _1,\mu _2)$ is finite; and $\mu _1^ A+\mu _2^ A$ is a positive measure on $A$ with finite Green energy which satisfies (5.1) for the source $\mu _1+\mu _2$. By the characterization, $(\mu _1+\mu _2)^ A=\mu _1^ A+\mu _2^ A$, and in the same way $(c\mu _1)^ A=c\mu _1^ A$ for $c\geq 0$. Taking $\tau =\mu ^ A$ in (5.1) gives $I_ B(\mu ,\mu ^ A)=I_ B(\mu ^ A)$, and then

\[ I_ B(\mu -\mu ^ A)=I_ B(\mu )-2I_ B(\mu ,\mu ^ A)+I_ B(\mu ^ A)=I_ B(\mu )-I_ B(\mu ^ A), \]

which is nonnegative by proposition 5.2.2. Finally, if $\mu $ is supported on $A$, then $\mu $ itself satisfies (5.1), so $\mu ^ A=\mu $ by the characterization. $\square$

Example 5.3.3. Let $A\subset B$ be a compact set such that $W=B\setminus A$ is a regular domain, and let $x\in W$. The Dirac measure $\delta _ x$ satisfies the hypothesis of theorem 5.3.1, since $G_ B\delta _ x=G_ B(x,\cdot )$ is continuous on $B\setminus \{ x\} $ and therefore bounded on the compact set $A$. Its swept potential $r_ x=G_ B\delta _ x^ A$ is

\[ r_ x(y)= \begin{cases} G_ B(x,y),& y\in A,\\ G_ B(x,y)-G_ W(x,y),& y\in W, \end{cases} \]

where at $y=x$ the difference $G_ B(x,y)-G_ W(x,y)$ is understood as the value of its harmonic extension across the common pole. Equivalently, let $f_ x$ be the function on $\partial W=\partial A\cup \partial B$ equal to $G_ B(x,\cdot )$ on $\partial A$ and to zero on $\partial B$. In terms of the harmonic measure $\omega _ y^ W$ of $W$ (proposition 2.5.1),

\[ r_ x(y)=\int _{\partial W}f_ x(\zeta )\, \mathrm d\omega _ y^ W(\zeta ),\qquad y\in W. \]

The swept measure $\delta _ x^ A$ is supported on $\partial A$ and has mass at most one. For example, if $A$ is a closed ball concentric with $B$, then $W$ is a regular domain.

Proof. We first check that $\partial W$ is the disjoint union of $\partial A$ and $\partial B$. Since $W=B\cap (\mathbb R^ d\setminus A)$ is the intersection of two open sets, $\partial W\subset \partial B\cup \partial A$, and $\partial A\subset A\subset B$ does not meet $\partial B$. Conversely, no point of $\partial B\cup \partial A$ lies in $W$. A point of $\partial B$ is a limit of points of $B\setminus A=W$, because the compact set $A\subset B$ has positive distance from $\partial B$. Every small ball around a point of $\partial A$ lies in $B$ and contains points outside $A$, that is, points of $W$. Consequently $\partial A$ and $\partial B$ are disjoint compact sets whose union is $\partial W$, and $f_ x$ is continuous on $\partial W$, because $G_ B(x,\cdot )$ is continuous on $B\setminus \{ x\} \supset A$.

Let $H=H_ Wf_ x$ be the solution of the Dirichlet problem on $W$ with boundary values $f_ x$; it is continuous on $\overline W$ since $W$ is regular, and $H\geq 0$ because $f_ x\geq 0$ and $H_ W$ preserves order. The function $G_ B(x,\cdot )=G_ B\delta _ x$ is superharmonic on $B$ by part (2) of proposition 4.3.2, it is continuous on $B\setminus \{ x\} $, and it tends to zero at $\partial B$ (theorem 2.5.2); hence its boundary limits on $\partial W$ are the values of $f_ x$. Therefore the maximum principle (proposition 3.1.4), applied to the subharmonic function $H-G_ B(x,\cdot )$ on the bounded open set $W$, gives $H\leq G_ B(x,\cdot )$ on $W$. Define $r=H$ on $W$ and $r=G_ B(x,\cdot )$ on $A$. Then $r$ is continuous on $B$, because $H$ and $G_ B(x,\cdot )$ have the same values on $\partial A$, and $0\leq r\leq G_ B(x,\cdot )$.

To see that $r$ is superharmonic, we argue as in Step 2 of the proof of theorem 5.2.5. Let $E$ be a ball with $\overline E\subset B$, and let $q$ be harmonic on $E$ and continuous on $\overline E$ with $q\leq r$ on $\partial E$. Then $q\leq G_ B(x,\cdot )$ on $\partial E$, hence on $E$ by superharmonicity of $G_ B(x,\cdot )$; in particular $r\geq q$ on $E\cap A$. On $E\cap W$ the harmonic function $H-q$ has nonnegative boundary limits: at points of $\partial E$ because $H$ extends continuously to $r\geq q$ there, and at points of $E\cap \partial A$ because $H$ tends to $G_ B(x,\cdot )\geq q$ there. The minimum principle gives $H\geq q$ on $E\cap W$. Hence $r\geq q$ on $E$, and $r$ is superharmonic by theorem 3.1.3.

The function $r$ is admissible for $R_{G_ B(x,\cdot )}^ A$: it is nonnegative and superharmonic on $B$ and equals $G_ B(x,\cdot )$ on $A$. Let $h$ be any admissible function. Then $h\geq r$ on $A$. On $W$, the superharmonic function $h$ satisfies $\varliminf _{y\to \zeta }h(y)\geq h(\zeta )\geq f_ x(\zeta )$ at points $\zeta \in \partial A$, by lower semicontinuity, and $\varliminf _{y\to \zeta }h(y)\geq 0=f_ x(\zeta )$ at points $\zeta \in \partial B$, since $h\geq 0$. Applying proposition 3.1.4 to the subharmonic function $H-h$ on $W$, we get $h\geq H$ there. Hence $h\geq r$ on $B$, and $R_{G_ B(x,\cdot )}^ A=r$ pointwise. Since $r$ is continuous, $\widehat R_{G_ B(x,\cdot )}^ A=r$, that is, $r_ x=r$.

On $W$, both $G_ B(x,\cdot )$ and $G_ W(x,\cdot )$ are locally integrable and satisfy $-\Delta =\delta _ x$ there (theorem 2.5.2). Their difference is therefore distributionally harmonic on $W$; by Weyl's lemma (theorem 2.1.4) it has a harmonic representative, which coincides with the difference off $x$. This harmonic function tends to $f_ x$ at $\partial W$, because $G_ W(x,\cdot )$ tends to zero there. By uniqueness of the solution of the Dirichlet problem (proposition 2.1.3), it equals $H$. The harmonic-measure formula is proposition 2.5.1 applied to $H=H_ Wf_ x$.

Finally, the measure $\delta _ x^ A=-\Delta r_ x$ vanishes on $W$, where $r_ x=H$ is harmonic, and on $\operatorname{Int}A$, where $r_ x=G_ B(x,\cdot )$ is harmonic because $x\notin A$. It is therefore supported on $\partial A$, and its mass is at most $\delta _ x(B)=1$ by theorem 5.3.1.

For the last assertion, write $B=B(c,R)$ and let $A=\overline{B(c,\rho )}$ with $0<\rho <R$, so that $W=\{ y:\rho <|y-c|<R\} $ is a bounded open annulus. It is connected: every point of $W$ is joined by a radial segment in $W$ to the sphere $\partial B(c,(\rho +R)/2)$, which is path-connected because $d\geq 2$. By theorem 2.4.6, it suffices to find, for every $\zeta \in \partial W$, a closed ball that meets $\overline W$ only at $\zeta $. If $|\zeta -c|=R$, the closed ball $\overline{B(2\zeta -c,R)}$ meets $\overline{B(c,R)}\supset \overline W$ only at $\zeta $, as noted at the beginning of section 2.5. If $|\zeta -c|=\rho $, let $m=(c+\zeta )/2$. Every $y\in \overline{B(m,\rho /2)}$ satisfies $|y-c|\leq |y-m|+|m-c|\leq \rho $, with equality only if $y-m=(\zeta -c)/2$, that is, $y=\zeta $. Since $|y-c|\geq \rho $ on $\overline W$, the closed ball $\overline{B(m,\rho /2)}$ meets $\overline W$ only at $\zeta $. Hence every boundary point of $W$ is regular, and $W$ is a regular domain. $\square$

5.4 Countable covers and quasi-open sets

The failure of countability in example 5.1.7 takes place on a polar set. We now show that, outside polar sets, Choquet's lemma recovers the countable operations needed for measures and capacity.

Theorem 5.4.1. Let $(U_ i)_{i\in I}$ be a family of finely open subsets of $\Omega $. Then there is an at most countable subset $J\subset I$ such that

\[ \left(\bigcup _{i\in I}U_ i\right)\setminus \left(\bigcup _{j\in J}U_ j\right) \]

is polar. Moreover, every finely open set $U$ contains a set $V$ such that $U\setminus V$ is polar and $V$ is the union of countably many sets $Q_ k\cap \{ u_ k>a_ k\} $, where $Q_ k$ is an open ball with $\overline{Q_ k}\subset \Omega $, $u_ k$ is subharmonic on an open neighborhood of $\overline{Q_ k}$ in $\Omega $, and $a_ k\in \mathbb R$; in particular, $V$ is a Borel set. Consequently, $U$ is measurable for the completion of Lebesgue measure, and for the completion of every finite positive measure of compact support with finite energy.

Proof. We first reduce to sets of a special form attached to balls with rational centers and radii; for each such ball, Choquet's lemma then selects countably many of these sets. Let $U=\bigcup _{i\in I}U_ i$. Call a set basic if it has the form $Q\cap \{ u>a\} $, where $Q$ is a ball with rational center and rational radius such that $\overline Q\subset \Omega $, $u$ is subharmonic on an open neighborhood of $\overline Q$ in $\Omega $, and $a\in \mathbb R$. Basic sets are finely open. Every point $x\in U$ lies in a basic set contained in some $U_ i$. Indeed, if $x\in U_ i$, then proposition 5.1.3 provides a Euclidean open set $V$, a function $u\in \mathrm{SH}(V)$ and $a<u(x)$ with $\{ y\in V:u(y)>a\} \subset U_ i$, and we choose a rational ball $Q$ with $x\in Q$ and $\overline Q\subset V$. Let $\mathcal B$ be the family of basic sets contained in some $U_ i$; thus $U=\bigcup \mathcal B$.

Fix a rational ball $Q$, and let $\mathcal B_ Q$ consist of the members of $\mathcal B$ of the form $Q\cap \{ u>a\} $; we may assume that $\mathcal B_ Q$ is nonempty. For every pair $(u,a)$ as in the definition of basic sets with $Q\cap \{ u>a\} \in \mathcal B_ Q$, put

\[ v=\frac{u-a}{1+\max \{ 0,\sup _ Q(u-a)\} }\quad \text{on }Q. \]

The supremum is less than $+\infty $, because $u$ is upper semicontinuous on a neighborhood of the compact set $\overline Q$ and hence bounded above on $\overline Q$. Thus $v$ is subharmonic on $Q$, $v\leq 1$, and $\{ v>0\} =Q\cap \{ u>a\} $.

Let $F$ be the supremum of all the functions $v$ so obtained. By Choquet's lemma (lemma 3.4.1), there is a sequence $(v_ k)$ among them such that $F'=\sup _ kv_ k$ satisfies $(F')^*=F^*$. By part (2) of theorem 4.4.4, the sets $\{ F\neq F^*\} $ and $\{ F'\neq (F')^*\} $ are polar; let $P_ Q$ be their union. If $y\in Q\setminus P_ Q$ and $v(y)>0$ for some $v$, then $F'(y)=(F')^*(y)=F^*(y)=F(y)>0$, so $v_ k(y)>0$ for some $k$. Hence the union of $\mathcal B_ Q$ is contained in the union of $P_ Q$ and the countably many basic sets $\{ v_ k>0\} $.

Doing this for each of the countably many rational balls $Q$, we obtain countably many members of $\mathcal B$ whose union covers $U$ outside the set $P=\bigcup _ QP_ Q$, which is polar by proposition 4.2.3. For each selected member choose one index $i$ such that the member is contained in $U_ i$. The set $J$ of chosen indices is at most countable, and $U\setminus \bigcup _{j\in J}U_ j\subset P$. This proves the first assertion.

For the second, let $U$ be finely open, and apply the first assertion to the family of all basic sets contained in $U$, whose union is $U$ by proposition 5.1.3. We obtain countably many basic sets whose union $V\subset U$ is such that $U\setminus V$ is polar. Each basic set is Borel, since subharmonic functions are Borel measurable, so $V$ is Borel.

For the measurability assertions, it suffices to show that the polar set $U\setminus V$ is contained in a Borel set $N$ that is null for Lebesgue measure and for every finite positive measure of compact support with finite energy. Since $\mathbb R^ d$ has a countable base, $U\setminus V$ is covered by countably many connected open sets $V_ k$ carrying nontrivial subharmonic functions $\psi _ k$ with $(U\setminus V)\cap V_ k\subset \{ \psi _ k=-\infty \} $. The Borel set $N=\bigcup _ k\{ \psi _ k=-\infty \} $ is polar by proposition 4.2.3; it is Lebesgue null by proposition 3.1.5, and null for measures of finite energy by theorem 4.3.3. $\square$

Proposition 5.4.2. Let $U\subset \Omega $ be finely open, let $B\subset \mathbb R^ d$ be an open ball, let $L\subset B$ be compact, and let $\eta >0$. Then there is a Euclidean open set $O\Subset B$ with $C_ B(O)<\eta $ such that $(U\cup O)\cap L$ is relatively open in $L$. Equivalently, $U\cap (L\setminus O)$ is relatively open in $L\setminus O$. In this sense, every finely open set is locally quasi-open.

Proof. We construct $O$ as the union of an open set covering the polar part of $U$ near $L$ and of open sets outside which the functions defining the rest of $U$ are continuous on $L$. By the second assertion of theorem 5.4.1, there are at most countably many sets $V_ k=Q_ k\cap \{ u_ k>a_ k\} \subset U$, with $Q_ k$, $u_ k$ and $a_ k$ as there, whose union $V$ is such that $U\setminus V$ is contained in a polar set $P$. Discarding the empty ones, we may assume that every $V_ k$ is nonempty and that $k$ ranges over a set $\mathcal K\subset \{ 1,2,\ldots \} $. Since $V_ k\neq \varnothing $, we may assume that $u_ k$ is defined on an open ball $Q_ k'\supset \overline{Q_ k}$ and is nontrivial there; by proposition 3.1.5, it is then locally integrable, hence not identically $-\infty $ on any ball in $Q_ k'$. Fix an open set $W_0$ with $L\subset W_0\Subset B$.

First we cover the polar part. The set $P\cap W_0$ is polar and its closure is a compact subset of $B$, so $C_ B^*(P\cap W_0)=0$ by corollary 4.4.2. Hence there is an open set $O_0$ with $P\cap W_0\subset O_0\subset W_0$ and $C_ B(O_0)<\eta /2$.

Next we deal with the discontinuities of the functions $u_ k$. For each $k\in \mathcal K$, the compact set $\overline{Q_ k}\cap L$ lies in the open set $Q_ k'\cap B$. Each of its points therefore lies in a ball $D'$ such that $D'\Subset D\Subset Q_ k'\cap B$ for another ball $D$, and finitely many such balls $D'$ cover $\overline{Q_ k}\cap L$. Enumerate all the pairs of balls obtained in this way, for all $k\in \mathcal K$, as $(D'_ j,D_ j)$ with $j$ in a set $\mathcal J$ of positive integers, and let $k(j)$ be the index $k$ for which the pair $(D'_ j,D_ j)$ was chosen. For each $j\in \mathcal J$, the quasi-continuity theorem (theorem 4.5.2), applied to $u_{k(j)}$ on $Q_{k(j)}'$ with the ball $D_ j\Subset Q_{k(j)}'$ and the compact set $\overline{D'_ j}\cap L\subset D_ j$, provides an open set $O_ j\Subset D_ j$ with $C_{D_ j}(O_ j)<\eta 2^{-j-1}$ such that $u_{k(j)}$ is finite and continuous on $(\overline{D'_ j}\cap L)\setminus O_ j$. By proposition 5.2.3, $C_ B(O_ j)=C_ B^*(O_ j)\leq C_{D_ j}^*(O_ j)=C_{D_ j}(O_ j)<\eta 2^{-j-1}$.

Put $O=O_0\cup \bigcup _{j\in \mathcal J}(O_ j\cap W_0)$. This is an open subset of $W_0$, so $O\Subset B$, and countable subadditivity and monotonicity of the outer capacity (theorem 4.3.5) give

\[ C_ B(O)\leq C_ B(O_0)+\sum _{j\in \mathcal J}C_ B(O_ j)<\frac\eta 2+\sum _{j\geq 1}\eta 2^{-j-1}=\eta . \]

It remains to show that $U\cap F$ is relatively open in $F=L\setminus O$. Since $L\subset W_0$, the set $(U\setminus V)\cap F$ is contained in $(P\cap W_0)\setminus O_0=\varnothing $, so $U\cap F=V\cap F$. Let $y\in V\cap F$. Then $y\in V_ k$ for some $k\in \mathcal K$; since $y\in \overline{Q_ k}\cap L$, we have $y\in D'_ j$ for some $j$ with $k(j)=k$. Since $L\subset W_0$, we have $O_ j\cap L\subset O$, so $D'_ j\cap F\subset (\overline{D'_ j}\cap L)\setminus O_ j$, and the function $u_ k$ is finite and continuous on $D'_ j\cap F$. Hence

\[ \{ z\in D'_ j\cap Q_ k\cap F:u_ k(z)>a_ k\} \]

is relatively open in $F$, contains $y$, and is contained in $V_ k\cap F$. Therefore $U\cap F=V\cap F$ is relatively open in $F$.

Write $U\cap F=T\cap F$ with $T$ open in $\mathbb R^ d$. Then $(U\cup O)\cap L=(U\cap F)\cup (O\cap L)=(T\cup O)\cap L$ is relatively open in $L$. Conversely, if $(U\cup O)\cap L$ is relatively open in $L$, then its intersection with $F$, which is $U\cap F$, is relatively open in $F$. This proves the equivalence of the two formulations. $\square$

Fine continuity should not be confused with quasi-continuity: a subharmonic function is continuous on all of $\Omega $ for the fine topology, whereas theorem 4.5.2 gives Euclidean continuity only after an open set of small capacity has been removed.

5.5 Complex lines and the choice of topology

On $\mathbb C^ n$, identified with $\mathbb R^{2n}$, the classical fine topology is generated by all subharmonic functions of $2n$ real variables. In 2 we shall instead use the upper semicontinuous functions whose restriction to each connected component of the intersection of their domain with an affine complex line is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $. On domains these are, apart from the constant $-\infty $, the plurisubharmonic functions of definition 8.1.1, and we shall see in example 10.3.4 that they generate the plurifine topology of definition 10.3.1 in the same way. In complex dimension one the line condition is planar subharmonicity, so the two topologies coincide; in higher dimensions they differ.

Proposition 5.5.1. Let $n\geq 2$ and $H=\{ z\in \mathbb C^ n:z_1=0\} $. Then the classical fine topology of $\mathbb C^ n=\mathbb R^{2n}$ induces the discrete topology on $H$. On the other hand, let $\tau $ be the coarsest topology on $\mathbb C^ n$ that contains the Euclidean topology and makes continuous every upper semicontinuous function $u\colon V\to [-\infty ,\infty )$, where $V\subset \mathbb C^ n$ is open, such that for every affine complex line $L$ the restriction of $u$ to each connected component of $L\cap V$ is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $. Then no point of $H$ is isolated in the topology induced on $H$ by $\tau $.

Proof. By example 3.2.4, the function $w\mapsto \log |w|$ is subharmonic on $\mathbb C$. Hence $z\mapsto \log |z_1|$, a function of the first two real coordinates, is subharmonic on $\mathbb R^{2n}$ by lemma 5.1.6; it is finite off $H$ and equals $-\infty $ on $H$. Thus $H$ is polar, and proposition 5.1.5 shows that the classical fine topology induces the discrete topology on $H$.

Now let $x\in H$, and let $N'$ be a neighborhood of $x$ for $\tau $. As in section 5.1, upper semicontinuity turns every condition of the form $u<b$ into a Euclidean open condition, and restrictions to smaller open sets preserve the complex-line condition; hence $N'$ contains a set

\[ N=V\cap \{ u_1>a_1\} \cap \dots \cap \{ u_ m>a_ m\} , \]

where $V$ is a Euclidean open neighborhood of $x$, the $u_ i$ are upper semicontinuous functions on $V$ satisfying the complex-line condition in the statement, and $u_ i(x)>a_ i$. Choose an affine complex line $\ell \subset H$ through $x$, which is possible since $n\geq 2$, and identify $\ell $ with $\mathbb C$. On the connected component of $\ell \cap V$ containing $x$, each restriction $u_ i|_\ell $ is subharmonic and is not identically $-\infty $, because $u_ i(x)$ is finite. Hence $\{ u_ i|_\ell >a_ i\} $ is open in the planar fine topology, and the intersection of these sets with that component is a planar fine neighborhood of $x$ contained in $N\cap \ell $. By proposition 5.1.4, applied in the plane, it contains points other than $x$. These points lie in $H\cap N'$, so $x$ is not isolated in $H$. $\square$
