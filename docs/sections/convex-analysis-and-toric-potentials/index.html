18.1 Convex analysis and toric potentials

Definitions, results, and proofs on this subject.

18 Convex analysis and toric potentials

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

Let the compact torus $(S^1)^ n$ act on $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ by separate rotations of the coordinates, $(z_1,\ldots ,z_ n)\mapsto (\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta _1}z_1,\ldots ,\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta _ n}z_ n)$. A plurisubharmonic function on $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ that is invariant under this action depends only on the logarithmic radii $x_ j=\log |z_ j|^2$, and we show in corollary 18.1.6 that, as a function of $x\in \mathbb R^ n$, it is precisely a finite convex function. The complex Hessian accounts for the convexity; conversely, the supporting affine functions of a convex function give the submean inequality on complex lines. Questions about the growth and the Monge–Ampère mass of invariant potentials thus become questions about convex functions on a real vector space, and we answer them in terms of the slopes of supporting affine functions.

The first part of the chapter develops the convex analysis that this requires. Separation of convex sets produces supporting affine functions, and Legendre duality identifies the closure of the set of slopes of a convex function $F$ with the closure of the set where its conjugate $F^*$ is finite. An upper bound of $F$, up to a constant, by the support function of a convex body $P$ holds exactly when all slopes of $F$ lie in $P$ (proposition 18.3.2); this leads to convex analogues of the classes of potentials with bounded growth, full mass and finite energy. The real Monge–Ampère measure of $F$ assigns to a set $E$ the volume of the set of slopes of $F$ at points of $E$, multiplied by $n!$. Since the subgradient map is multivalued, we construct this measure by pushing Lebesgue measure forward under the gradient of $F^*$, which is defined almost everywhere (theorem 18.4.1). Its total mass is $n!$ times the volume of the closed set of slopes, and it depends continuously on $F$ for local uniform convergence (theorem 18.4.2); when no mass escapes to infinity, the convergence also holds against bounded continuous functions. Finally, counting lattice points in the dilates $kP$ relates these volumes to dimensions of spaces of sections.

The second part constructs, from a smooth lattice polytope $P$, a projective toric manifold $X_ P$ with a very ample line bundle $L$. Monomial charts at the vertices of $P$ make its geometry explicit, and we read off from them the correspondence between torus orbits and faces of $P$, the orders of characters along the boundary divisors, and a basis of $H^0(X_ P,L^ k)$ indexed by the lattice points of $kP$ (theorem 18.8.5). The potential of Guillemin's reference metric on $L$ is the Legendre transform of an explicit entropy function on $P$, whose gradient has logarithmic singularities along the facets. In the same charts we show that these singularities cancel after the transformation, so that the metric is smooth, with positive definite curvature, also along the boundary orbits (theorem 18.9.1). These are the foundations of convex analysis and of ample toric geometry used in [Part I, XiaSampleBook].

Throughout the chapter, $N_{\mathbb R}$ is a real vector space of finite dimension $n\geq 0$ and $M_{\mathbb R}$ is its dual space, with pairing $\langle p,x\rangle $ for $p\in M_{\mathbb R}$ and $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$; we identify the dual of $M_{\mathbb R}$ with $N_{\mathbb R}$. Whenever an estimate requires a norm, we fix a Euclidean norm on $N_{\mathbb R}$ and give $M_{\mathbb R}$ the dual norm $|p|=\max _{|x|\leq 1}\langle p,x\rangle $, so that $|\langle p,x\rangle |\leq |p||x|$; the open and closed balls $B(x,r)$ and $\overline{B(x,r)}$ of section 1.4 are taken with respect to these norms. When volumes are involved, we also fix a lattice $N\subset N_{\mathbb R}$, that is, the set of integral linear combinations of a basis of $N_{\mathbb R}$, and the dual lattice $M=\{ p\in M_{\mathbb R}:\langle p,x\rangle \in \mathbb Z\text{ for all }x\in N\} $. Lebesgue measure on $M_{\mathbb R}$, written $\mathrm dp$ or $\operatorname {vol}$, and Lebesgue measure $\mathrm dx$ on $N_{\mathbb R}$ are normalized so that a fundamental parallelepiped of $M$, respectively of $N$, has volume one; equivalently, they are Lebesgue measure in the coordinates of a lattice basis. (This volume of subsets of $M_{\mathbb R}$ is not to be confused with the volume of a cohomology class in definition 15.8.1.) In dimension zero both spaces are a point and both measures are the unit point mass. A convex body is a nonempty compact convex set, possibly with empty interior.

18.1 Closed convex functions and supporting slopes

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

The conjugate of a finite convex function is in general finite only on a convex subset of the dual space. We therefore allow convex functions to take the value $+\infty $, and we impose lower semicontinuity as the regularity condition that controls their behavior at the boundary of the set where they are finite. The definitions and results of this section and the next are stated on $N_{\mathbb R}$; they apply equally to functions on $M_{\mathbb R}$, with the roles of the two spaces interchanged.

Definition 18.1.1. A function $F\colon N_{\mathbb R}\to [-\infty ,+\infty ]$ is convex if its epigraph

\[ \operatorname {epi}F=\{ (x,r)\in N_{\mathbb R}\times \mathbb R:r\geq F(x)\} \]

is convex. A convex function is proper if it does not take the value $-\infty $ and its effective domain $\operatorname {dom}F=\{ x:F(x)<+\infty \} $ is nonempty; a proper convex function is closed if it is lower semicontinuous. The relative interior $\operatorname {ri}C$ of a convex set $C$ is its interior in its affine span, and $\overline C\setminus \operatorname {ri}C$ is its relative boundary. For a proper convex function $F$ and $x\in \operatorname {dom}F$, the subgradient set of $F$ at $x$ is

\[ \partial F(x)=\{ p\in M_{\mathbb R}:F(y)\geq F(x)+\langle p,y-x\rangle \text{ for every }y\in N_{\mathbb R}\} . \]

Its elements are the subgradients, or supporting slopes, of $F$ at $x$: they are the slopes of the affine functions that lie below $F$ and agree with $F$ at $x$. We set $\partial F(x)=\varnothing $ for $x\notin \operatorname {dom}F$ and $\partial F(E)=\bigcup _{x\in E}\partial F(x)$ for $E\subset N_{\mathbb R}$.

For a function $F$ with values in $(-\infty ,+\infty ]$, the epigraph is convex if and only if $F((1-\lambda )x+\lambda y)\leq (1-\lambda )F(x)+\lambda F(y)$ for all $x,y\in N_{\mathbb R}$ and $0<\lambda <1$. Indeed, suppose that the epigraph is convex. If $F(x)$ and $F(y)$ are finite, the inequality expresses that the corresponding combination of $(x,F(x))$ and $(y,F(y))$ lies in $\operatorname {epi}F$; otherwise its right side is $+\infty $. Conversely, for $(x,r),(y,s)\in \operatorname {epi}F$ the inequality gives $F((1-\lambda )x+\lambda y)\leq (1-\lambda )r+\lambda s$. Thus definition 18.1.1 extends the usual notion of a convex function, with the sign convention fixed in the Conventions, to functions with values in $[-\infty ,+\infty ]$. The effective domain of a convex function is convex, being the image of its epigraph under the projection to $N_{\mathbb R}$.

A convex set contains every convex combination $\sum _{i=1}^ q\lambda _ ix_ i$ of its points, where $\lambda _ i\geq 0$ and $\sum _ i\lambda _ i=1$: for $q\geq 2$ and $\lambda _ q<1$ this follows by induction on $q$ from $\sum _ i\lambda _ ix_ i=(1-\lambda _ q)\sum _{i<q}\frac{\lambda _ i}{1-\lambda _ q}x_ i+\lambda _ qx_ q$. Consequently, the convex hull of a set $S$, that is, the smallest convex set containing $S$, is the set of all convex combinations of points of $S$, since this set is convex and contains $S$. Applied to the epigraph, the same fact gives the finite form of Jensen's inequality

\[ F\Bigl (\sum _{i=1}^ q\lambda _ ix_ i\Bigr )\leq \sum _{i=1}^ q\lambda _ iF(x_ i) \]

for every convex function $F$ with values in $(-\infty ,+\infty ]$ and every convex combination.

A function with values in $(-\infty ,+\infty ]$ is lower semicontinuous if and only if its epigraph is closed in $N_{\mathbb R}\times \mathbb R$. Indeed, if $F$ is lower semicontinuous and $(x_ k,r_ k)\in \operatorname {epi}F$ converge to $(x,r)$, then $F(x)\leq \varliminf _ kF(x_ k)\leq r$. Conversely, if the epigraph is closed and $x_ k\to x$ with $\varliminf _ kF(x_ k)<r<F(x)$ for some real $r$, then $(x_ k,r)\in \operatorname {epi}F$ for infinitely many $k$, and in the limit $(x,r)\in \operatorname {epi}F$, which is impossible.

A finite convex function is a real-valued one. A finite convex function $F$ on an open convex set $\Omega \subset N_{\mathbb R}$ is identified with the proper convex function on $N_{\mathbb R}$ that equals $F$ on $\Omega $ and $+\infty $ elsewhere; this extension is convex, since its convexity inequality is trivial when $x$ or $y$ lies outside $\Omega $. In particular, $\partial F(x)$ is defined for $x\in \Omega $. The subgradient notation $\partial F$ is unrelated to the boundary $\partial E$ of a set.

We shall need a Lipschitz bound on cubes. Let $Q'\subset Q$ be closed cubes with a common center and half-sides $\delta $ and $2\delta $ in affine coordinates on an affine subspace of $N_{\mathbb R}$, and let $|\cdot |$ be the Euclidean norm of these coordinates. If a convex function $F$ is finite on $Q$, with oscillation $\omega =\sup _ QF-\inf _ QF<\infty $, then

18.1
\begin{equation} |F(z)-F(y)|\leq \frac{\omega }{\delta }|z-y|\qquad (y,z\in Q'). \end{equation}

Indeed, for $y\neq z$ in $Q'$ the point $w=z+\delta (z-y)/|z-y|$ lies in $Q$, and $z=(1-\lambda )y+\lambda w$ with $\lambda =|z-y|/(|z-y|+\delta )\leq |z-y|/\delta $. Convexity gives $F(z)-F(y)\leq \lambda (F(w)-F(y))\leq (\omega /\delta )|z-y|$, and we may exchange $y$ and $z$.

Lemma 18.1.2. Let $W$ be a real vector space of finite dimension $n$, and let $C\subset W$ be convex.

  1. If $C$ is closed and nonempty and $z\in W\setminus C$, there is a linear functional $\ell $ on $W$ with $\sup _ C\ell <\ell (z)$.

  2. If $C$ is closed and $z$ is a boundary point of $C$ in $W$, there is a nonzero linear functional $\ell $ on $W$ with $\sup _ C\ell =\ell (z)$; that is, $C$ has a supporting hyperplane at $z$.

  3. Every point of the convex hull of a set $S\subset W$ is a convex combination of affinely independent points of $S$, hence of at most $n+1$ points of $S$.

  4. If $C\neq \varnothing $, then $\operatorname {ri}C\neq \varnothing $, and $(1-t)x_0+ty\in \operatorname {ri}C$ whenever $x_0\in \operatorname {ri}C$, $y\in \overline C$ and $0\leq t<1$. Moreover,

    \[ \overline{\operatorname {ri}C}=\overline C,\qquad \operatorname {ri}C=\operatorname {ri}\overline C, \]

    and the relative boundary of $C$ has Lebesgue measure zero in the affine span of $C$.

Proof. Fix a Euclidean inner product $y\cdot w$ on $W$, with norm $|y|$.

(1) Choose $y_ k\in C$ with $|z-y_ k|\to \inf _{w\in C}|z-w|$. The sequence $(y_ k)$ is bounded, so a subsequence converges to a point $y$, which lies in $C$ because $C$ is closed. Thus $y$ minimizes the distance to $z$ on $C$, and $y\neq z$. For $w\in C$ and $0\leq t\leq 1$ the point $y+t(w-y)$ lies in $C$, so the function $t\mapsto |z-y-t(w-y)|^2$ on $[0,1]$ is minimal at $t=0$, and its derivative there, $-2(z-y)\cdot (w-y)$, is nonnegative. Hence $\ell (w)=(z-y)\cdot w$ satisfies $\ell (w)\leq \ell (y)=\ell (z)-|z-y|^2<\ell (z)$ for every $w\in C$.

(2) Since $z$ is a boundary point of $C$, there are points $z_ k\notin C$ with $z_ k\to z$. By (1) there are unit vectors $a_ k$ with $a_ k\cdot w<a_ k\cdot z_ k$ for all $w\in C$. A subsequence of $(a_ k)$ converges to a unit vector $a$, and in the limit $a\cdot w\leq a\cdot z$ for all $w\in C$. As $z\in C$, the functional $\ell (w)=a\cdot w$ has the required property.

(3) Let $x=\sum _{i=1}^ q\lambda _ ix_ i$ with $x_ i\in S$, $\lambda _ i>0$ and $\sum _ i\lambda _ i=1$. If the points $x_ i$ are affinely dependent, the vectors $(x_ i,1)\in W\times \mathbb R$ are linearly dependent, say $\sum _ i\mu _ i(x_ i,1)=0$ with not all $\mu _ i$ zero. Since $\sum _ i\mu _ i=0$, some $\mu _ i$ is positive. Replacing each $\lambda _ i$ by $\lambda _ i-s\mu _ i$, where $s=\min \{ \lambda _ i/\mu _ i:\mu _ i>0\} $, preserves $\sum _ i\lambda _ i$ and $\sum _ i\lambda _ ix_ i$, keeps all coefficients nonnegative and makes at least one of them zero. We discard the points with zero coefficient and repeat. Eventually the remaining points are affinely independent, and an affinely independent subset of $W$ has at most $n+1$ elements.

(4) Let $A$ be the affine span of $C$, of dimension $k$, and write $B_ A(x,r)$ for the open ball of $A$ with center $x$ and radius $r$. If $k=0$, then $C$ is a point, equal to its relative interior and to its closure, and its relative boundary is empty; so assume $k\geq 1$. A maximal affinely independent subset of $C$ spans an affine subspace containing $C$, hence equal to $A$, so it consists of $k+1$ points $x_0',\ldots ,x_ k'$. Every point $y\in A$ has unique barycentric coordinates $\lambda _ i(y)$, which are affine functions of $y$ with $\sum _ i\lambda _ i(y)=1$ and $y=\sum _ i\lambda _ i(y)x_ i'$. At the barycenter $c$ of the simplex with vertices $x_ i'$ all of them equal $1/(k+1)$, so they are positive on a ball $B_ A(c,r)$; every point of this ball is then a convex combination of the $x_ i'$ and lies in $C$. Hence $c\in \operatorname {ri}C$.

Now let $x_0\in \operatorname {ri}C$, choose $r>0$ with $B_ A(x_0,r)\subset C$, and let $y\in \overline C$ and $0\leq t<1$. If $y\in C$, convexity gives $B_ A((1-t)x_0+ty,(1-t)r)=(1-t)B_ A(x_0,r)+ty\subset C$. In general choose $y_ k\in C$ with $y_ k\to y$; then $B_ A((1-t)x_0+ty_ k,(1-t)r)\subset C$, and for large $k$ this ball contains $B_ A((1-t)x_0+ty,(1-t)r/2)$. Thus $(1-t)x_0+ty\in \operatorname {ri}C$. Letting $t\uparrow 1$, we see that every point of $\overline C$ is a limit of points of $\operatorname {ri}C$, so $\overline{\operatorname {ri}C}=\overline C$. Next, $\operatorname {ri}C\subset \operatorname {ri}\overline C$, since both are interiors in the same affine space $A$. Conversely, if $x\in \operatorname {ri}\overline C$, then $y=x+\varepsilon (x-c)$ lies in $\overline C$ for small $\varepsilon >0$, and $x=(1-t)c+ty$ with $t=1/(1+\varepsilon )<1$; hence $x\in \operatorname {ri}C$.

For the measure assertion, translate so that $c=0$ and identify $A$ with $\mathbb R^ k$ by a linear isomorphism, which changes Lebesgue measure on $A$ only by a constant factor. Let $Z=\overline C\setminus \operatorname {ri}C$ be the relative boundary; it is closed in $A$, because $\operatorname {ri}C$ is open in $A$, hence Borel, and $0\notin Z$. We claim that the dilates $sZ$, $s>0$, are pairwise disjoint. Indeed, suppose that $sz=s'z'$ with $z,z'\in Z$ and $0<s<s'$. Then $z'=(s/s')z$ is of the form $(1-t)\cdot 0+tz$ with $z\in \overline C$ and $0<t<1$, so $z'\in \operatorname {ri}C$ by what we have just shown, which is impossible. Now fix $R>0$ and put $Z_ R=Z\cap B(0,R)$. The sets $sZ_ R$ with $s$ rational in $[\frac12,1]$ are pairwise disjoint subsets of $B(0,R)$, and each has measure $s^ k|Z_ R|\geq 2^{-k}|Z_ R|$. Since infinitely many of them fit into a set of finite measure, $|Z_ R|=0$. Letting $R\to \infty $, we obtain $|Z|=0$. $\square$

Proposition 18.1.3. Let $F$ be a proper convex function on $N_{\mathbb R}$. Then $F$ is locally Lipschitz on $\operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$, and $\partial F(x)\neq \varnothing $ for every $x\in \operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$. Now let $F$ be a finite convex function on an open convex set $\Omega \subset N_{\mathbb R}$. Then the following hold.

  1. The sets $\partial F(x)$, $x\in \Omega $, are compact and bounded uniformly for $x$ in each compact subset of $\Omega $, and the graph $\{ (x,p):x\in \Omega ,\ p\in \partial F(x)\} $ is closed in $\Omega \times M_{\mathbb R}$.

  2. For $x\in \Omega $ and $v\in N_{\mathbb R}$,

    \[ F'(x;v)=\lim _{t\downarrow 0}\frac{F(x+tv)-F(x)}t =\max _{p\in \partial F(x)}\langle p,v\rangle . \]

  3. $F$ is differentiable at $x\in \Omega $ if and only if $\partial F(x)$ is a singleton; in that case $\partial F(x)=\{ \nabla F(x)\} $, where $\nabla F(x)\in M_{\mathbb R}$ denotes the differential of $F$ at $x$.

  4. $F$ is differentiable almost everywhere on $\Omega $. More precisely, the set $\Omega _ F$ of points of $\Omega $ at which $F$ is differentiable is a Borel set whose complement in $\Omega $ is a null set, and $\nabla F\colon \Omega _ F\to M_{\mathbb R}$ is a Borel map.

Proof. We first show that $F$ is bounded, hence Lipschitz, near each point of $\operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$, and find a subgradient there by separation; then we prove (1)–(4). Let $A$ be the affine span of $\operatorname {dom}F$, of dimension $k$, and let $x\in \operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$. Choose affine coordinates on $A$ centered at $x$, with the associated Euclidean norm $|\cdot |$; since all norms on $A$ are equivalent, local Lipschitz continuity does not depend on this choice. For small $\delta >0$ the cube $Q$ of points of $A$ whose coordinates are at most $2\delta $ in absolute value lies in $\operatorname {dom}F$. Every point of $Q$ is a convex combination of the $2^ k$ vertices of $Q$; this follows by induction on $k$, since writing the last coordinate of a point as a convex combination of $\pm 2\delta $ exhibits the point as a convex combination of two points of the opposite faces of $Q$, which are cubes of dimension $k-1$. By the finite form of Jensen's inequality, $F\leq C$ on $Q$, where $C$ is the largest value of $F$ at a vertex. If $y\in Q$, then also $2x-y\in Q$, and $F(x)\leq \frac12(F(y)+F(2x-y))$ gives $F(y)\geq 2F(x)-C$. Thus $F$ is bounded on $Q$, and by (18.1) it is Lipschitz on the cube of half-side $\delta $ centered at $x$.

Next we construct a subgradient at $x$. Since $\operatorname {dom}F\subset A$, the epigraph of $F$ lies in $A\times \mathbb R$; let $E$ be its closure, a closed convex subset of $A\times \mathbb R$. Because $F$ is continuous at $x$ as a function on $A$, the points $(x,F(x)-\varepsilon )$, $\varepsilon >0$, do not belong to $E$, so $(x,F(x))$ is a boundary point of $E$ in $A\times \mathbb R$. By lemma 18.1.2(2), applied in $A\times \mathbb R$ with origin $(x,F(x))$, there are a linear functional $a$ on the linear subspace $A_0=\{ y-y':y,y'\in A\} $ parallel to $A$ and a real number $c$, not both zero, such that

\[ \langle a,y-x\rangle +c\, (s-F(x))\geq 0\qquad \text{for all }(y,s)\in E. \]

Letting $s\to +\infty $ shows that $c\geq 0$. If $c=0$, then $a\neq 0$ and $\langle a,y-x\rangle \geq 0$ for all $y\in \operatorname {dom}F$; but $\operatorname {dom}F$ contains a ball of $A$ about $x$, and this ball contains points $y$ with $\langle a,y-x\rangle <0$. Hence $c>0$, and taking $s=F(y)$ we obtain $F(y)\geq F(x)+\langle p_ A,y-x\rangle $ for $y\in \operatorname {dom}F$, where $p_ A=-a/c$. Extend $p_ A$ to a linear functional $p\in M_{\mathbb R}$. The inequality $F(y)\geq F(x)+\langle p,y-x\rangle $ holds for $y\in \operatorname {dom}F$, because then $y-x\in A_0$, and also for $y\notin \operatorname {dom}F$, where $F(y)=+\infty $. Thus $p\in \partial F(x)$.

From now on let $F$ be a finite convex function on the open convex set $\Omega $, so that $A=N_{\mathbb R}$ and $\operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)=\Omega $. Fix a basis $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$ of $N_{\mathbb R}$, let $K\subset \Omega $ be compact, and choose $r>0$ such that the set $K_ r=\{ y+\sum _ is_ ie_ i:y\in K,\ |s_ i|\leq r\} $ lies in $\Omega $. If $x\in K$ and $p\in \partial F(x)$, the subgradient inequality at $x$, evaluated at the points $x\pm re_ i$, gives $\pm r\langle p,e_ i\rangle \leq F(x\pm re_ i)-F(x)$, hence $|\langle p,e_ i\rangle |\leq 2r^{-1}\max _{K_ r}|F|$, where $F$ is bounded on the compact set $K_ r$ because it is continuous. This is the uniform bound in (1). If $x_ k\to x$ in $\Omega $, $p_ k\in \partial F(x_ k)$ and $p_ k\to p$, then letting $k\to \infty $ in $F(y)\geq F(x_ k)+\langle p_ k,y-x_ k\rangle $ and using that $F$ is continuous at $x$, we get $p\in \partial F(x)$. Thus the graph is closed, and each $\partial F(x)$ is closed and bounded, hence compact.

For (2) and (4) we use the following slope inequalities for a convex function $\phi $ on an interval: if $s_1<s_2<s_3$, then

18.2
\begin{equation} \frac{\phi (s_2)-\phi (s_1)}{s_2-s_1}\leq \frac{\phi (s_3)-\phi (s_1)}{s_3-s_1}\leq \frac{\phi (s_3)-\phi (s_2)}{s_3-s_2}. \end{equation}

Indeed, $s_2=\lambda s_1+(1-\lambda )s_3$ with $\lambda =(s_3-s_2)/(s_3-s_1)\in (0,1)$, and the convexity inequality $\phi (s_2)\leq \lambda \phi (s_1)+(1-\lambda )\phi (s_3)$ is equivalent to each of the two inequalities. Now fix $x$ and $v$, and apply (18.2) to $\phi (t)=F(x+tv)$, which is convex on the open interval of those $t$ with $x+tv\in \Omega $: with $s_1=0$ it shows that the difference quotient in (2) is nondecreasing in $t>0$, so its limit as $t\downarrow 0$ exists in $[-\infty ,+\infty )$. Each $p\in \partial F(x)$ gives $F(x+tv)-F(x)\geq t\langle p,v\rangle $; hence $F'(x;v)\geq \max _{p\in \partial F(x)}\langle p,v\rangle $, the maximum being attained by compactness. Conversely, choose $p_ t\in \partial F(x+tv)$. The subgradient inequality at $x+tv$, evaluated at $x$, gives $F(x+tv)-F(x)\leq t\langle p_ t,v\rangle $. By (1) the vectors $p_ t$ stay bounded as $t\downarrow 0$, and each of their cluster points lies in $\partial F(x)$. Therefore $F'(x;v)\leq \varlimsup _{t\downarrow 0}\langle p_ t,v\rangle \leq \max _{p\in \partial F(x)}\langle p,v\rangle $.

For (3), suppose that $\partial F(x)=\{ p\} $, and choose $p_ h\in \partial F(x+h)$. By (1), every cluster point of $p_ h$ as $h\to 0$ lies in $\partial F(x)$, so $p_ h\to p$. The subgradient inequalities at $x$ and at $x+h$ give

\[ 0\leq F(x+h)-F(x)-\langle p,h\rangle \leq \langle p_ h-p,h\rangle =o(|h|), \]

so $F$ is differentiable at $x$, with $\nabla F(x)=p$. Conversely, if $F$ is differentiable at $x$ and $p\in \partial F(x)$, dividing $F(x+tv)-F(x)\geq t\langle p,v\rangle $ by $t$ and letting $t\downarrow 0$ gives $\langle \nabla F(x),v\rangle \geq \langle p,v\rangle $ for every $v$, hence $p=\nabla F(x)$. As $\partial F(x)\neq \varnothing $, it equals $\{ \nabla F(x)\} $.

For (4), fix $i$, and write $D_ i^+F(x)=F'(x;e_ i)$ and $D_ i^-F(x)=-F'(x;-e_ i)$ for the one-sided partial derivatives; they are finite by (2). Let $\phi $ be the restriction of $F$ to the intersection of $\Omega $ with a line parallel to $e_ i$, parametrized as $\phi (s)=F(y+se_ i)$ on an open interval. By (18.2), $\phi $ has one-sided derivatives $\phi '_\pm $ at every point, $\phi '_-(s)\leq \phi '_+(s)$, and $\phi '_+(s)\leq \phi '_-(s')$ whenever $s<s'$, because both are bounded by the slope of the chord from $s$ to $s'$. Hence the open intervals $(\phi '_-(s),\phi '_+(s))$ are pairwise disjoint; each nonempty one contains a rational number, so only countably many of them are nonempty, and $\phi '_-=\phi '_+$ outside a countable set. Next, $D_ i^+F$ is the pointwise limit on $\Omega $ of the Borel functions equal to $k(F(x+e_ i/k)-F(x))$ where $x+e_ i/k\in \Omega $ and to $0$ elsewhere, so it is a Borel function, and similarly for $D_ i^-F$. Thus the set $Z_ i\subset \Omega $ where $D_ i^+F\neq D_ i^-F$ is a Borel set that meets each line parallel to $e_ i$ in a countable set, and Fubini's theorem, in the coordinates of the basis $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$, shows that $Z_ i$ is a null set. At a point $x\notin Z_1\cup \cdots \cup Z_ n$, (2) gives

\[ \max _{p\in \partial F(x)}\langle p,e_ i\rangle =D_ i^+F(x)=D_ i^-F(x)=\min _{p\in \partial F(x)}\langle p,e_ i\rangle \]

for every $i$, so all subgradients at $x$ coincide, and $F$ is differentiable at $x$ by (3). Conversely, if $F$ is differentiable at $x$, then $D_ i^+F(x)=D_ i^-F(x)=\langle \nabla F(x),e_ i\rangle $ for every $i$. Therefore $\Omega _ F=\Omega \setminus (Z_1\cup \cdots \cup Z_ n)$ is a Borel set with null complement, and the coordinates $\langle \nabla F,e_ i\rangle =D_ i^+F$ of $\nabla F$ are Borel functions on $\Omega _ F$. $\square$

Proposition 18.1.4. Let $F$ be a proper convex function on $N_{\mathbb R}$, and let $\overline F$ be its lower semicontinuous regularization, that is, the largest lower semicontinuous function on $N_{\mathbb R}$ that is at most $F$. Then $\overline F$ is a closed proper convex function, and $\overline F=F$ on $\operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$. A closed proper convex function is determined by its values on the relative interior of its effective domain. Finally, if a sequence of finite convex functions on an open convex set $\Omega \subset N_{\mathbb R}$ converges to a finite limit at each point of a dense subset of $\Omega $, then it converges locally uniformly on $\Omega $ to a finite convex function.

Proof. By proposition 18.1.3, $F$ has a subgradient $p$ at some point $x_1\in \operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$, so $F$ lies above the affine function $\ell (y)=F(x_1)+\langle p,y-x_1\rangle $. The closure $E$ of $\operatorname {epi}F$ is convex, and it lies above the graph of $\ell $ because $\ell $ is continuous. Hence, for each $x$, the set $\{ r:(x,r)\in E\} $ is closed and bounded below by $\ell (x)$, and with each of its points $r$ it contains $[r,+\infty )$, because $\operatorname {epi}F$ has this property. So this set is either empty or a closed half-line $[\widetilde F(x),+\infty )$ with $\widetilde F(x)\geq \ell (x)>-\infty $. Setting $\widetilde F(x)=+\infty $ in the first case, we see that $E$ is the epigraph of a convex function $\widetilde F$. It is lower semicontinuous because its epigraph is closed, and $\widetilde F\leq F$, so $\operatorname {dom}\widetilde F\supset \operatorname {dom}F\neq \varnothing $. If $G\leq F$ is lower semicontinuous, then $\operatorname {epi}G$ is closed and contains $\operatorname {epi}F$, hence also $E$, and so $G\leq \widetilde F$. Thus $\widetilde F=\overline F$, which is therefore closed, proper and convex.

Let $x\in \operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$. If $(y_ k,r_ k)\in \operatorname {epi}F$ converge to $(x,r)$, then $y_ k$ lies in the affine span of $\operatorname {dom}F$, on which $F$ is continuous at $x$; hence $r\geq \lim _ kF(y_ k)=F(x)$. This shows $\overline F(x)\geq F(x)$, and so $\overline F(x)=F(x)$.

Now let $F$ be closed, fix $x_0\in \operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$, and let $x\in \overline{\operatorname {dom}F}$. By lemma 18.1.2(4), the points $(1-t)x_0+tx$ with $0\leq t<1$ lie in $\operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$, so the convex function $\phi (t)=F((1-t)x_0+tx)$ is finite on $[0,1)$. Convexity gives $\phi (t)\leq (1-t)\phi (0)+t\phi (1)$, hence $\varlimsup _{t\uparrow 1}\phi (t)\leq \phi (1)$, while lower semicontinuity gives $\varliminf _{t\uparrow 1}\phi (t)\geq \phi (1)$. Therefore

\[ F(x)=\lim _{t\uparrow 1}F((1-t)x_0+tx) \qquad (x\in \overline{\operatorname {dom}F}), \]

also when $F(x)=+\infty $. Since $F=+\infty $ outside $\overline{\operatorname {dom}F}$, which is the closure of $\operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$, the function $F$ is determined by its restriction to $\operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$.

Finally, let $(F_ j)$ be a sequence of finite convex functions on $\Omega $ converging to a finite limit at every point of a dense set $S\subset \Omega $. We show that near each point of $\Omega $ the functions $F_ j$ are uniformly bounded, hence uniformly Lipschitz, so that convergence on $S$ propagates to uniform convergence. Fix a basis of $N_{\mathbb R}$, write $Q(y,\rho )$ for the closed cube of half-side $\rho $ centered at $y$ in the corresponding coordinates, and let $x\in \Omega $ and $\rho >0$ with $Q(x,2\rho )\subset \Omega $. For each vertex $y$ of $Q(x,\rho )$ choose a point of $S$ whose coordinates differ from those of $y$ by less than $\rho /2$, and let $S_0\subset S\cap Q(x,2\rho )$ be the finite set of these points. Its convex hull $H$ is compact as the image of a simplex under a linear map, and it contains $Q(x,\rho /2)$. Indeed, let $\ell $ be a nonzero linear functional, $\ell (z)=\sum _ ia_ iz_ i$ in the chosen coordinates, and put $\| a\| =\sum _ i|a_ i|$. At a vertex $y^*$ of $Q(x,\rho )$ where $\ell $ is maximal, $\ell (y^*)=\ell (x)+\rho \| a\| $, and the point $s\in S_0$ chosen for $y^*$ satisfies $\ell (s)>\ell (y^*)-\frac\rho 2\| a\| =\max _{Q(x,\rho /2)}\ell $. Hence no point of $Q(x,\rho /2)$ can be separated from $H$ as in lemma 18.1.2(1).

The sequences $(F_ j(s))_ j$, $s\in S_0$, converge, so they are bounded above by some $C$, and the finite form of Jensen's inequality gives $F_ j\leq C$ on $H$ for all $j$. Choose $s_0\in S\cap Q(x,\rho /8)$. Then $Q(s_0,3\rho /8)\subset Q(x,\rho /2)$, and for $y\in Q(s_0,3\rho /8)$ the reflected point $2s_0-y$ lies in the same cube, so that $F_ j(y)\geq 2F_ j(s_0)-C\geq 2\inf _ kF_ k(s_0)-C$. Hence $|F_ j|\leq B$ on $Q(x,\rho /4)\subset Q(s_0,3\rho /8)$ for all $j$, with a constant $B$. By (18.1), applied with $\delta =\rho /8$ and $\omega \leq 2B$, the $F_ j$ are Lipschitz on $Q(x,\rho /8)$ with the common constant $L=16B/\rho $, for the Euclidean norm $|\cdot |$ of the chosen coordinates.

Given $\varepsilon >0$, choose a finite set $S_\varepsilon \subset S\cap Q(x,\rho /8)$ such that every point of $Q(x,\rho /8)$ lies within distance $\varepsilon /L$ of $S_\varepsilon $. To this end, cover the compact cube $Q(x,\rho /8)$ by finitely many balls of radius $\varepsilon /(2L)$ centered in the cube, and pick a point of the dense set $S$ in the intersection of each ball with the interior of the cube, a nonempty open set. For all sufficiently large $j,k$ we have $|F_ j-F_ k|<\varepsilon $ on $S_\varepsilon $, hence $|F_ j-F_ k|<3\varepsilon $ on $Q(x,\rho /8)$. Thus $(F_ j)$ is uniformly Cauchy on $Q(x,\rho /8)$. Finitely many such cubes cover any given compact subset of $\Omega $, so $(F_ j)$ converges locally uniformly to a finite continuous function, which is convex because the convexity inequalities pass to pointwise limits. $\square$

18.1.1 Invariant functions on tubes and complex tori

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

We now prove the correspondence between invariant plurisubharmonic functions and convex functions described at the beginning of the chapter. For a nonempty open convex set $V\subset \mathbb R^ n$, $n\geq 1$, consider the tube

\[ V+\mathrm i\mathbb R^ n=\{ t+\mathrm is:t\in V,\ s\in \mathbb R^ n\} \subset \mathbb C^ n, \]

with coordinates $w=t+\mathrm is$. Recall from section 8.5, here without the variables $z$, that a function $U$ on the tube is invariant under imaginary translations if $U(w+\mathrm is')=U(w)$ for all $s'\in \mathbb R^ n$, that is, if $U(t+\mathrm is)$ depends only on $t$. As observed there, the complex Hessian of a smooth function of this kind is a quarter of its real Hessian in $t$, so plurisubharmonicity becomes convexity; the general case follows by smoothing. In addition, local integrability of $U$ excludes the value $-\infty $.

Proposition 18.1.5. Let $n\geq 1$, let $V\subset \mathbb R^ n$ be a nonempty open convex set, and let $U\in \mathrm{PSH}(V+\mathrm i\mathbb R^ n)$ be invariant under imaginary translations. Then there is a finite convex function $f\colon V\to \mathbb R$ such that $U(t+\mathrm is)=f(t)$ for all $t\in V$ and $s\in \mathbb R^ n$. Conversely, for every finite convex function $f$ on $V$, the function $f(\operatorname{Re}w)$ is plurisubharmonic on $V+\mathrm i\mathbb R^ n$ and invariant under imaginary translations.

Proof. Define $f(t)=U(t)$, identifying $t\in V$ with $t+\mathrm i0$; by invariance, $U(t+\mathrm is)=f(t)$. By theorem 8.2.1, $U$ is locally integrable, so the set $\{ U=-\infty \} =\{ f=-\infty \} +\mathrm i\mathbb R^ n$ is a Lebesgue null set in $\mathbb C^ n$. Applying Fubini's theorem on products of compact boxes in $t$ and in $s$, we see that $\{ f=-\infty \} $ is a null set in $V$.

For $\varepsilon >0$ let $V_\varepsilon =\{ t\in V:\operatorname {dist}(t,\mathbb R^ n\setminus V)>\varepsilon \} $. A point $t$ lies in $V_\varepsilon $ if and only if the closed ball $\overline{B(t,\varepsilon )}$ lies in $V$, so $V_\varepsilon $ is the intersection of the convex sets $V-y$, $|y|\leq \varepsilon $, and is convex. The points of the tube at distance more than $\varepsilon $ from its complement form the tube $V_\varepsilon +\mathrm i\mathbb R^ n$. On this set let $U_\varepsilon =U*\rho _\varepsilon $, where $\rho _\varepsilon $ is the radial mollifier of section 1.4 on $\mathbb C^ n=\mathbb R^{2n}$. The function $U_\varepsilon $ is smooth, and its complex Hessian matrix $(\partial ^2U_\varepsilon /\partial w_ j\partial \bar w_ k)$ is positive semidefinite. Indeed, for $\xi \in \mathbb C^ n$, the function $\sum _{j,k}(\partial ^2U_\varepsilon /\partial w_ j\partial \bar w_ k)\xi _ j\bar\xi _ k$ is, by proposition 1.4.3(1), the mollification of the distribution $\sum _{j,k}(\partial ^2U/\partial w_ j\partial \bar w_ k)\xi _ j\bar\xi _ k$, which is positive by theorem 8.2.1 and the matrix criterion of section 7.3, so it is nonnegative by proposition 1.4.3(3).

Moreover, $U_\varepsilon \downarrow U$ as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ by (3.3), because $U$ is subharmonic and locally integrable (theorem 8.2.1). Since imaginary translations commute with convolution, $U_\varepsilon $ is invariant under them; write $U_\varepsilon (t+\mathrm is)=f_\varepsilon (t)$. For a function of $t=\operatorname{Re}w$ alone we have $\partial /\partial w_ j=\frac12\partial /\partial t_ j=\partial /\partial \bar w_ j$, hence

\[ \frac{\partial ^2U_\varepsilon }{\partial w_ j\partial \bar w_ k} =\frac14\frac{\partial ^2f_\varepsilon }{\partial t_ j\partial t_ k}. \]

The left side is a positive semidefinite matrix, so $f_\varepsilon $ has positive semidefinite real Hessian. Hence the restriction of $f_\varepsilon $ to every segment in the convex set $V_\varepsilon $ has nonnegative second derivative, and $f_\varepsilon $ is convex on $V_\varepsilon $.

Suppose that $f(t_0)=-\infty $ for some $t_0\in V$, and choose $r\in (0,1)$ with $\overline{B(t_0,3r)}\subset V$. The upper semicontinuous function $U$ is bounded above, say by $C$, on the compact set $\overline{B(t_0,3r)}+\mathrm i[-1,1]^ n$. Since $U_\varepsilon (w)$ is an average of $U$ over the ball $\overline{B(w,\varepsilon )}$, we get $U_\varepsilon \leq C$ on $B(t_0,2r)$ for $\varepsilon <r$; moreover $B(t_0,2r)\subset V_\varepsilon $ for such $\varepsilon $. For $t\in B(t_0,r)$, the point $2t-t_0$ lies in $B(t_0,2r)$, and $t$ is the midpoint of $t_0$ and $2t-t_0$. Convexity of $f_\varepsilon $ therefore gives

\[ f_\varepsilon (t) \leq \tfrac 12f_\varepsilon (t_0) +\tfrac 12f_\varepsilon (2t-t_0) \leq \tfrac 12f_\varepsilon (t_0)+\tfrac 12C. \]

As $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, $f_\varepsilon (t_0)\downarrow f(t_0)=-\infty $ and $f_\varepsilon (t)\downarrow f(t)$, so $f=-\infty $ on $B(t_0,r)$. This contradicts the fact that $\{ f=-\infty \} $ is a null set. Hence $f$ is finite. Finally, every segment $[a,b]\subset V$ is compact and therefore lies in $V_\varepsilon $ for small $\varepsilon $; the convexity inequalities for $f_\varepsilon $ on $[a,b]$ pass to the pointwise limit, so $f$ is convex.

Conversely, let $f$ be a finite convex function on $V$. By proposition 18.1.3, $f$ is continuous and has a subgradient $p\in \mathbb R^ n$ at every point. Let $w_0=t_0+\mathrm is_0$ be a point of the tube, $a\in \mathbb C^ n\setminus \{ 0\} $, and $r>0$ such that the circle $\{ w_0+r\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }a:\theta \in [0,2\pi ]\} $ lies in the tube. If $p$ is a subgradient of $f$ at $t_0$, then

\[ f\bigl (\operatorname{Re}(w_0+r\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }a)\bigr ) \geq f(t_0)+r\left\langle p, \operatorname{Re}(\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }a)\right\rangle =f(t_0)+r\operatorname{Re}\Bigl (\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }\sum _ jp_ ja_ j\Bigr ). \]

The last term has mean zero over $\theta \in [0,2\pi ]$, so the mean of the left side over the circle is at least $f(t_0)=f(\operatorname{Re}w_0)$. Since $w_0$, $a$ and $r$ are arbitrary, the restriction of the continuous function $f(\operatorname{Re}w)$ to every affine complex line satisfies the submean inequality of definition 3.1.1 at every point, and it is therefore subharmonic. As $f(\operatorname{Re}w)$ is finite and continuous and the tube is a domain, definition 8.1.1 shows that $f(\operatorname{Re}w)$ is plurisubharmonic. It is invariant under imaginary translations by construction. $\square$

Corollary 18.1.6. Let $n\geq 1$, let $V\subset \mathbb R^ n$ be a nonempty open convex set, and put

\[ \Omega _ V=\{ z\in (\mathbb C^*)^ n: (\log |z_1|^2,\ldots ,\log |z_ n|^2)\in V\} . \]

Let $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega _ V)$ be invariant under the action of the compact torus $(S^1)^ n$ by separate rotations of the coordinates, $(z_1,\ldots ,z_ n)\mapsto (\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta _1}z_1,\ldots ,\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta _ n}z_ n)$. Then there is a finite convex function $f$ on $V$ such that

\[ \varphi (z)=f(\log |z_1|^2,\ldots ,\log |z_ n|^2)\qquad (z\in \Omega _ V). \]

Conversely, for every finite convex function $f$ on $V$ this formula defines an $(S^1)^ n$-invariant plurisubharmonic function on $\Omega _ V$. In particular, an $(S^1)^ n$-invariant plurisubharmonic function on $(\Delta ^*)^ n$ or on $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ is finite everywhere, where $\Delta ^*=\{ \zeta \in \mathbb C:0<|\zeta |<1\} $.

Proof. Consider the holomorphic map

\[ E\colon \mathbb C^ n\to (\mathbb C^*)^ n,\qquad E(w)=(\exp (w_1/2),\ldots ,\exp (w_ n/2)). \]

Since $\log |E_ j(w)|^2=\operatorname{Re}w_ j$, it maps the tube $V+\mathrm i\mathbb R^ n$ onto $\Omega _ V$; in particular, $\Omega _ V$ is connected. By proposition 8.2.3, $\varphi \circ E$ is either plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on the tube, and the second alternative is impossible because $E$ is surjective and $\varphi $ is nontrivial. Since $E(w+\mathrm is')=(\mathrm e^{\mathrm is_1'/2}E_1(w),\ldots ,\mathrm e^{\mathrm is_ n'/2}E_ n(w))$, the rotation invariance of $\varphi $ makes $\varphi \circ E$ invariant under imaginary translations. Proposition 18.1.5 now gives a finite convex function $f$ with $\varphi (E(w))=f(\operatorname{Re}w)$, which is the asserted formula.

Conversely, let $f$ be finite and convex. Then $f(\operatorname{Re}w)$ is plurisubharmonic on the tube by proposition 18.1.5. Near each point of $\Omega _ V$, the map $E$ has the holomorphic local inverse $z\mapsto (2\log z_1,\ldots ,2\log z_ n)$, defined with local branches of the logarithm, and $f(\log |z_1|^2,\ldots ,\log |z_ n|^2)$ is the composition of $f(\operatorname{Re}w)$ with this inverse. By proposition 8.2.3 it is plurisubharmonic near that point, since it is finite. Plurisubharmonicity is a local property, so the function is plurisubharmonic on $\Omega _ V$, and it is $(S^1)^ n$-invariant because it depends only on $|z_1|,\ldots ,|z_ n|$. The domains $(\Delta ^*)^ n$ and $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ are $\Omega _ V$ for $V=(-\infty ,0)^ n$ and $V=\mathbb R^ n$. $\square$

18.2 Legendre duality and convex envelopes

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

The Legendre transform exchanges convex functions on $N_{\mathbb R}$ and on $M_{\mathbb R}$, and points with slopes: up to closure, the slopes of $F$ are the points where $F^*$ is finite. We prove the duality theorem and compute the conjugates of envelopes and sums.

Definition 18.2.1. The Legendre transform, or convex conjugate, of a function $F\colon N_{\mathbb R}\to [-\infty ,+\infty ]$ is the function on $M_{\mathbb R}$ given by

\[ F^*(p)=\sup _{x\in N_{\mathbb R}}(\langle p,x\rangle -F(x)). \]

The conjugate of a function on $M_{\mathbb R}$ is defined in the same way and is a function on $N_{\mathbb R}$; we write $F^{**}=(F^*)^*$. The convex indicator function $\chi _ C$ of a nonempty set $C$, in either space, is $0$ on $C$ and $+\infty $ elsewhere. The support function of a nonempty set $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ is

\[ h_ P(x)=\sup _{p\in P}\langle p,x\rangle \qquad (x\in N_{\mathbb R}); \]

it is convex with values in $(-\infty ,+\infty ]$, and it is finite when $P$ is bounded.

Theorem 18.2.2. Let $F$ be a proper convex function on $N_{\mathbb R}$. Then $F^*$ is a closed proper convex function on $M_{\mathbb R}$, and

\[ F^{**}=\overline F,\qquad (\overline F)^*=F^*. \]

If $F$ is closed, then for $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ and $p\in M_{\mathbb R}$ the following conditions are equivalent:

\[ p\in \partial F(x),\qquad x\in \partial F^*(p),\qquad F(x)+F^*(p)=\langle p,x\rangle ; \]

moreover,

\[ \operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F^*)\subset \partial F(N_{\mathbb R}) \subset \operatorname {dom}F^*,\qquad \overline{\partial F(N_{\mathbb R})} =\overline{\operatorname {dom}F^*}. \]

Finally, for every nonempty closed convex set $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$, the support function and the convex indicator function are conjugate: $h_ P=\chi _ P^*$ and $\chi _ P=h_ P^*$.

Proof. The conjugate is the supremum of the continuous affine functions $p\mapsto \langle p,x\rangle -F(x)$, $x\in \operatorname {dom}F$, so it is convex and lower semicontinuous. Since $\operatorname {dom}F\neq \varnothing $, we have $F^*(p)\geq \langle p,x_0\rangle -F(x_0)>-\infty $ for any $x_0\in \operatorname {dom}F$. By proposition 18.1.3, $F$ has a subgradient $p_0$ at some point $x_1\in \operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F)$, and then $F^*(p_0)=\langle p_0,x_1\rangle -F(x_1)<+\infty $. Thus $F^*$ is closed and proper. The definition of $F^*$ gives $F(x)\geq \langle p,x\rangle -F^*(p)$ for all $p$, that is, $F^{**}\leq F$.

We next show that $F^{**}=F$ when $F$ is closed. An affine function $\langle p,\cdot \rangle -c$ lies below $F$ if and only if $c\geq F^*(p)$. Hence $F^{**}$ is the supremum of all affine minorants of $F$, and it suffices to find, for every $x$ and every real $r<F(x)$, an affine minorant of $F$ whose value at $x$ exceeds $r$. The point $(x,r)$ does not belong to the closed convex set $\operatorname {epi}F$, so by lemma 18.1.2(1), applied in $N_{\mathbb R}\times \mathbb R$, there are $a\in M_{\mathbb R}$, $c\in \mathbb R$ and $\delta >0$ with

\[ \langle a,y\rangle +cs\geq \langle a,x\rangle +cr+\delta \qquad \text{for all }(y,s)\in \operatorname {epi}F. \]

Letting $s\to +\infty $ shows that $c\geq 0$. If $c>0$, taking $s=F(y)$ gives the affine minorant $y\mapsto r+(\delta -\langle a,y-x\rangle )/c$, whose value at $x$ is $r+\delta /c>r$. If $c=0$, then $\langle a,y\rangle \geq \langle a,x\rangle +\delta $ for all $y\in \operatorname {dom}F$. Let $L_0$ be an affine minorant of $F$, which exists by the first paragraph. For $k>0$ the affine functions

\[ L_0(y)+k\bigl (\langle a,x\rangle +\delta /2-\langle a,y\rangle \bigr ) \]

are again minorants of $F$, since the added term is at most $-k\delta /2$ on $\operatorname {dom}F$, and their values at $x$ tend to $+\infty $ as $k\to \infty $. In both cases the required minorant exists, so $F^{**}=F$. For a general proper convex $F$, a continuous affine function lies below $F$ if and only if it lies below the largest lower semicontinuous minorant $\overline F$. Therefore $F^*=(\overline F)^*$, and since $\overline F$ is closed and proper by proposition 18.1.4, $F^{**}=(\overline F)^{**}=\overline F$.

Let $F$ be closed. By definition, $p\in \partial F(x)$ means that $x\in \operatorname {dom}F$ and $\langle p,y\rangle -F(y)\leq \langle p,x\rangle -F(x)$ for all $y$, that is, the supremum defining $F^*(p)$ is attained at $x$; equivalently, $F(x)+F^*(p)=\langle p,x\rangle $. (If $x\notin \operatorname {dom}F$, both conditions fail, since then $F(x)+F^*(p)=+\infty $.) Applying this to the closed proper function $F^*$ and using $F^{**}=F$, we find that $x\in \partial F^*(p)$ if and only if $F^*(p)+F(x)=\langle p,x\rangle $. This proves the equivalences. If $p\in \operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F^*)$, then $F^*$ has a subgradient $x$ at $p$ by proposition 18.1.3, and $p\in \partial F(x)$ by the equivalences; this is the first inclusion. If $p\in \partial F(x)$, then $F^*(p)=\langle p,x\rangle -F(x)<+\infty $, which gives the second. The closures coincide because $\operatorname {ri}(\operatorname {dom}F^*)$ is dense in $\operatorname {dom}F^*$ by lemma 18.1.2(4). Finally, $\chi _ P^*=h_ P$ by the definitions, and $\chi _ P$ is a closed proper convex function, so $h_ P^*=\chi _ P^{**}=\chi _ P$. $\square$

\[ G\Bigl (\sum _ j\lambda _ jx_ j\Bigr )\leq \sum _ j\lambda _ jF_{i_ j}(x_ j). \]

Hence $G\leq H$. For the conjugate, the supremum defining $H^*(p)$ is the supremum over all admissible combinations of

\[ \Bigl \langle p,\sum _ j\lambda _ jx_ j\Bigr \rangle -\sum _ j\lambda _ jF_{i_ j}(x_ j)=\sum _ j\lambda _ j\bigl (\langle p,x_ j\rangle -F_{i_ j}(x_ j)\bigr )\leq \sup _ iF_ i^*(p), \]

and combinations with $q=1$ give equality. Since affine functions are convex, an affine function lies below $H$ if and only if it lies below $\inf _ iF_ i$. Suppose that $H$ has an affine minorant. Then $H$ is proper, so $\overline H$ is a closed proper convex function by proposition 18.1.4, and $\overline H=H^{**}=(\sup _ iF_ i^*)^*$ by theorem 18.2.2. A lower semicontinuous convex function below $\inf _ iF_ i$ lies below $H$, and therefore below $\overline H$.

(3) Convexity of $F\mathbin \square G$ follows as in (2), by mixing decompositions $x=y+z$ and $x'=y'+z'$ whose values $F(y)+G(z)$ are close to the infima. The two suprema over $y$ and $z$ in

\[ (F\mathbin \square G)^*(p)=\sup _{y,z}\bigl (\langle p,y\rangle -F(y)+\langle p,z\rangle -G(z)\bigr )=F^*(p)+G^*(p) \]

are independent, which gives the conjugate formula.

(4) If $n=0$, then $F$ and $G$ are constants and both sides equal $-F(0)-G(0)$; so let $n\geq 1$. Let $J(p)=\inf _{q+r=p}(F^*(q)+G^*(r))$. Each conjugate is bounded below by minus its value at the origin: $F^*\geq -F(0)$ and $G^*\geq -G(0)$. Moreover, for every $R>0$, choosing $x$ with $|x|=R$ and $\langle q,x\rangle =R|q|$ gives

\[ F^*(q)\geq R|q|-\max _{|x|\leq R}F(x), \]

and the analogous bound holds for $G^*$. Consequently, if $p$ stays bounded and $F^*(q)+G^*(r)$ stays bounded above, then $q$ and $r=p-q$ stay bounded: for instance $F^*(q)\leq F^*(q)+G^*(r)+G(0)$, and the last bound with $R=1$ controls $|q|$. Lower semicontinuity of $F^*$ and $G^*$ therefore implies that the infimum defining $J(p)$ is attained whenever it is finite: a minimizing sequence of decompositions has a convergent subsequence. The same argument, applied to $p_ k\to p$ and decompositions attaining $J(p_ k)$, shows that $J$ is lower semicontinuous. By (3), $J=F^*\mathbin \square G^*$ is convex; it is bounded below by $-F(0)-G(0)$, and it is proper, since $J(q_0+r_0)\leq F^*(q_0)+G^*(r_0)<+\infty $ for subgradients $q_0\in \partial F(0)$ and $r_0\in \partial G(0)$. Finite convex functions are continuous, so $F$ and $G$ are closed and $F^{**}=F$, $G^{**}=G$. By (3), $J^*=F^{**}+G^{**}=F+G$, and theorem 18.2.2 gives $J=J^{**}=(F+G)^*$. $\square$

18.3 Slope bodies and bounded growth

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

We now study the finite convex functions on $N_{\mathbb R}$ whose growth at infinity is controlled by the support function $h_ P$ of a convex body $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$. By duality, such control is a condition on the set where the conjugate is finite, that is, on the slopes of the function.

Definition 18.3.1. Let $F,G$ be finite convex functions on $N_{\mathbb R}$. As for functions on a compact manifold (section 13.5), we write $F\preceq G$ if $F\leq G+C$ on $N_{\mathbb R}$ for some constant $C$, and $F\sim G$ if $F\preceq G$ and $G\preceq F$. The closed convex set

\[ K_ F=\overline{\operatorname {dom}F^*}\subset M_{\mathbb R} \]

is nonempty, and by theorem 18.2.2 it is the closure of the set $\partial F(N_{\mathbb R})$ of all supporting slopes of $F$; when it is compact we call it the slope body of $F$. For a convex body $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$, let $\mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ be the set of finite convex functions $F$ on $N_{\mathbb R}$ with $F\preceq h_ P$, and let the bounded class $\mathcal E^\infty (N_{\mathbb R},P)$ be the set of those with $F\sim h_ P$. If $P$ has nonempty interior, the full-mass class $\mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ is the set of $F\in \mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ with $K_ F=P$; if $P$ has empty interior, we set $\mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P)=\mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P)$. Finally, the finite-energy class $\mathcal E^1(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ is the set of $F\in \mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ with $\int _ PF^*\, \mathrm dp<+\infty $. Since $F^*\geq -F(0)$ by the definition of $F^*$, this integral is well defined in $(-\infty ,+\infty ]$.

The notation is chosen by analogy with the classes $\mathcal E^\infty (X,\theta ,\phi )\subset \mathcal E^1(X,\theta ,\phi )\subset \mathcal E(X,\theta ,\phi )$ of definition 15.8.3, definition 17.2.1, with the support function $h_ P$ in the role of the model potential $\phi $.

Proposition 18.3.2. Let $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ be a convex body and $F$ a finite convex function on $N_{\mathbb R}$. The following conditions are equivalent:

\[ F\preceq h_ P,\qquad \operatorname {dom}F^*\subset P, \qquad \partial F(N_{\mathbb R})\subset P. \]

If they hold, then $\max _ x(F(x)-h_ P(x))=F(0)$, and $F$ is Lipschitz on $N_{\mathbb R}$ with constant $\max _{p\in P}|p|$. Moreover, $F\sim h_ P$ if and only if $F^*$ is bounded on $P$ and equal to $+\infty $ outside $P$, and

\[ \mathcal E^\infty (N_{\mathbb R},P)\subset \mathcal E^1(N_{\mathbb R},P)\subset \mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P)\subset \mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P). \]

If $F,G$ are finite convex functions on $N_{\mathbb R}$ and $K_ F$ is compact, then

\[ K_{F+G}=K_ F+K_ G. \]

In particular, if $P'\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ is another convex body, $K_ F=P$ and $K_ G=P'$, then $F+G\in \mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P+P')$.

Proof. If $F\leq h_ P+C$, conjugation reverses the inequality and gives $F^*\geq h_ P^*-C=\chi _ P-C$ by theorem 18.2.2, so $\operatorname {dom}F^*\subset P$. Every subgradient of $F$ lies in $\operatorname {dom}F^*$ by the same theorem, so the second condition implies the third. If all slopes of $F$ lie in $P$ and $p\in \partial F(x)$, the subgradient inequality at $x$, evaluated at $0$, gives

\[ F(x)-F(0)\leq \langle p,x\rangle \leq h_ P(x). \]

Thus $F\leq h_ P+F(0)$, which proves the first condition and, since $h_ P(0)=0$, the identity $\max _ x(F(x)-h_ P(x))=F(0)$. If $p_ x\in \partial F(x)$ and $p_ y\in \partial F(y)$, the subgradient inequalities give $\langle p_ y,y-x\rangle \geq F(y)-F(x)\geq \langle p_ x,y-x\rangle $, so $|F(y)-F(x)|\leq \max _{p\in P}|p|\, |y-x|$.

Conjugation reverses inequalities, and $F^{**}=F$ because the finite convex function $F$ is continuous. Hence $h_ P-C\leq F\leq h_ P+C$ holds if and only if $\chi _ P-C\leq F^*\leq \chi _ P+C$, that is, if and only if $F^*=+\infty $ outside $P$ and $|F^*|\leq C$ on $P$. In that case $K_ F=P$, so $F\in \mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ (by the convention if $P$ has empty interior), and $\int _ PF^*\, \mathrm dp\leq C\operatorname {vol}(P)<\infty $; thus $\mathcal E^\infty \subset \mathcal E^1$. The inclusions $\mathcal E^1\subset \mathcal E\subset \mathcal P$ hold by definition. If $P$ has empty interior and $n>0$, then $P$ lies in a proper affine subspace, because its relative interior is nonempty and open in its affine span (lemma 18.1.2(4)); hence $\operatorname {vol}(P)=0$, every integral over $P$ vanishes, and $\mathcal E^1=\mathcal E=\mathcal P$.

Now let $F,G$ be finite convex functions with $K_ F$ compact. By proposition 18.2.3(4), $(F+G)^*(p)$ is finite exactly when $p=q+r$ with $q\in \operatorname {dom}F^*$ and $r\in \operatorname {dom}G^*$, so

\[ \operatorname {dom}(F+G)^*=\operatorname {dom}F^*+\operatorname {dom}G^*. \]

It remains to pass to closures. Since addition $M_{\mathbb R}\times M_{\mathbb R}\to M_{\mathbb R}$ is continuous, $K_ F+K_ G$ lies in the closure of $\operatorname {dom}F^*+\operatorname {dom}G^*$. Conversely, if $q_ k+r_ k\to s$ with $q_ k\in \operatorname {dom}F^*$ and $r_ k\in \operatorname {dom}G^*$, then a subsequence of $(q_ k)$ converges to some $q\in K_ F$, because $K_ F$ is compact, and along it $r_ k\to s-q\in K_ G$; hence $s\in K_ F+K_ G$. This proves $K_{F+G}=K_ F+K_ G$. Finally, if $K_ F=P$ and $K_ G=P'$, then $F\preceq h_ P$ and $G\preceq h_{P'}$ by the first part, and $h_ P+h_{P'}=h_{P+P'}$; hence $F+G\in \mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P+P')$ and $K_{F+G}=P+P'$, so that $F+G\in \mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P+P')$. $\square$

For smooth approximation we use mollification, which preserves convexity and, on convex functions, is monotone in the radius.

Lemma 18.3.3. Let $A$ be a finite convex function on $N_{\mathbb R}$, and let $\rho _\varepsilon (y)=\varepsilon ^{-n}\rho (y/\varepsilon )$ be the mollifiers of section 1.4, in the coordinates of a basis of $N$. Then the functions $A*\rho _\varepsilon $, $\varepsilon >0$, are smooth and convex, $A*\rho _\varepsilon (x)$ is a nondecreasing function of $\varepsilon >0$ with $A*\rho _\varepsilon \geq A$ for every $x$, and $A*\rho _\varepsilon \to A$ locally uniformly as $\varepsilon \to 0$.

Proof. If $n=0$, then $A*\rho _\varepsilon =A$ and there is nothing to prove; let $n\geq 1$. Recall that $\rho $ is smooth, nonnegative, even, supported in the unit ball, and has integral one. Since $A$ is continuous, being locally Lipschitz by proposition 18.1.3, the functions $A*\rho _\varepsilon $ are smooth by section 1.4, and they converge to $A$ locally uniformly by lemma 1.4.1(1). They are convex, because $A*\rho _\varepsilon (x)=\int A(x-y)\rho _\varepsilon (y)\, \mathrm dy$ and the convexity inequalities of the functions $x\mapsto A(x-y)$ can be integrated against $\rho _\varepsilon (y)\, \mathrm dy\geq 0$. For fixed $x$, consider the function

\[ g(\varepsilon )=\int A(x-\varepsilon y)\rho (y)\, \mathrm dy\qquad (\varepsilon \in \mathbb R), \]

which is finite because $A$ is continuous and $\rho $ has compact support. The substitution $y\mapsto \varepsilon y$ shows that $g(\varepsilon )=A*\rho _\varepsilon (x)$ for $\varepsilon >0$, and $g(0)=A(x)$. For each $y$, the function $\varepsilon \mapsto A(x-\varepsilon y)$ is convex, so $g$ is convex; and $g$ is even, because $\rho $ is even. A convex even function on $\mathbb R$ is nondecreasing on $[0,+\infty )$: if $0\leq a<b$, then $a=\lambda b+(1-\lambda )(-b)$ with $\lambda =(a+b)/(2b)\in (0,1]$, so that $g(a)\leq \lambda g(b)+(1-\lambda )g(-b)=g(b)$. Hence $A*\rho _\varepsilon (x)$ is nondecreasing in $\varepsilon >0$ and bounded below by $g(0)=A(x)$. $\square$

Proposition 18.3.4. Let $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ be a convex body. Every $F\in \mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ is the pointwise limit of a decreasing sequence of smooth functions $F_ j\in \mathcal E^\infty (N_{\mathbb R},P)$, and the convergence is locally uniform. If functions $F_ j\colon N_{\mathbb R}\to (-\infty ,+\infty ]$ decrease pointwise to a function $F$, then the conjugates $F_ j^*$ increase pointwise to $F^*$. A sequence of finite convex functions on $N_{\mathbb R}$ converges pointwise to a finite function if and only if it converges locally uniformly.

Proof. Let $A_ j=\max \{ F,h_ P-j\} $. These are convex functions, decreasing in $j$, with $A_ j\sim h_ P$, and $A_ j=F$ on every compact set on which $h_ P-F\leq j$; hence $A_ j\to F$ locally uniformly. With the mollifiers $\rho _\varepsilon $ of lemma 18.3.3, choose $\varepsilon _ j\downarrow 0$ and set $F_ j=A_ j*\rho _{\varepsilon _ j}$. By that lemma, these functions are smooth and convex, and the monotonicity in $\varepsilon $ together with $A_{j+1}\leq A_ j$ gives $F_{j+1}=A_{j+1}*\rho _{\varepsilon _{j+1}}\leq A_ j*\rho _{\varepsilon _{j+1}}\leq A_ j*\rho _{\varepsilon _ j}=F_ j$, and $F_ j\geq A_ j\geq F$. By proposition 18.3.2, $F$ and $h_ P$ are Lipschitz with constant $\max _{p\in P}|p|$, hence so is $A_ j$, and therefore $|F_ j-A_ j|\leq C\varepsilon _ j$ with $C=\max _{p\in P}|p|\int |y|\rho (y)\, \mathrm dy$. Consequently $F_ j\sim A_ j\sim h_ P$, so $F_ j\in \mathcal E^\infty (N_{\mathbb R},P)$, and $F_ j\to F$ locally uniformly.

If $F_ j\downarrow F$ pointwise, then

\[ \sup _ jF_ j^*(p)=\sup _ j\sup _ x\bigl (\langle p,x\rangle -F_ j(x)\bigr )=\sup _ x\sup _ j\bigl (\langle p,x\rangle -F_ j(x)\bigr )=F^*(p), \]

and the conjugates $F_ j^*$ increase, because conjugation reverses inequalities. Finally, if a sequence of finite convex functions on $N_{\mathbb R}$ converges pointwise to a finite function, it converges locally uniformly by proposition 18.1.4, applied with $\Omega $ and the dense subset both equal to $N_{\mathbb R}$, and conversely local uniform convergence implies pointwise convergence. $\square$

Example 18.3.5. The hypotheses $K_ F=P$ and $K_ G=P'$ in the last assertion of proposition 18.3.2 cannot be replaced by $F\in \mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ and $G\in \mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P')$: when $P$ has empty interior, membership in $\mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ does not force a function to use every slope in $P$. Let $N_{\mathbb R}=M_{\mathbb R}=\mathbb R^2$ with the standard pairing, $P=[0,1]\times \{ 0\} $ and $P'=\{ 0\} \times [0,1]$. Both bodies have empty interior, and the zero function belongs to $\mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P)=\mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ and to $\mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P')$, since $0\leq h_ P$ and $0\leq h_{P'}$. The sum of the zero function with itself is zero, whose slope body is $\{ 0\} $, whereas $P+P'=[0,1]^2$ has nonempty interior. Hence the zero function does not belong to $\mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P+P')$.

Finally, we compare logarithms of sums of exponentials with linear functions.

Lemma 18.3.6. Let $m\geq 1$ and $\beta _1,\ldots ,\beta _ m,\alpha \in M_{\mathbb R}$. The function

\[ x\longmapsto \log \sum _{i=1}^ m\mathrm e^{\langle \beta _ i,x\rangle } -\langle \alpha ,x\rangle \]

is bounded below on $N_{\mathbb R}$ if and only if $\alpha $ belongs to the convex hull of $\beta _1,\ldots ,\beta _ m$. Suppose that all $\beta _ i$ and $\alpha $ lie in $M$, identify $M$ with $\mathbb Z^ n$ by a basis and $N_{\mathbb R}$ with $\mathbb R^ n$ by the dual basis, and write $z^\alpha =z_1^{\alpha _1}\cdots z_ n^{\alpha _ n}$. Then this condition is also equivalent to the boundedness on $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ of

\[ \frac{|z^\alpha |^2}{\sum _ i|z^{\beta _ i}|^2}. \]

Proof. If $\alpha =\sum _ i\lambda _ i\beta _ i$ is a convex combination, then $\langle \alpha ,x\rangle \leq \max _ i\langle \beta _ i,x\rangle \leq \log \sum _ i\mathrm e^{\langle \beta _ i,x\rangle }$, so the function is nonnegative. Conversely, suppose that $\alpha $ is not in the convex hull. The hull is compact, being the image of the standard simplex under the linear map $(\lambda _ i)\mapsto \sum _ i\lambda _ i\beta _ i$, and convex, so lemma 18.1.2(1), applied in $M_{\mathbb R}$, gives $v\in N_{\mathbb R}$ with $\langle \alpha ,v\rangle >\max _ i\langle \beta _ i,v\rangle $. Since the logarithmic sum differs from $\max _ i\langle \beta _ i,x\rangle $ by a number in $[0,\log m]$, the value of the function at $x=tv$ is at most $t(\max _ i\langle \beta _ i,v\rangle -\langle \alpha ,v\rangle )+\log m$, which tends to $-\infty $ as $t\to +\infty $. For the last assertion, put $x_ j=\log |z_ j|^2$. Then $|z^\gamma |^2=\mathrm e^{\langle \gamma ,x\rangle }$ for every $\gamma \in \mathbb Z^ n$, including exponents with negative entries, so the displayed quotient equals the exponential of minus the function above; and $z\mapsto x$ maps $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ onto $\mathbb R^ n$. $\square$

18.4 The real Monge–Ampère measure

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

Let $F$ be a finite convex function on $N_{\mathbb R}$. Following Alexandrov, we measure a set $E\subset N_{\mathbb R}$ by the volume of the set $\partial F(E)$ of supporting slopes of $F$ at points of $E$. For a smooth $F$ with positive definite Hessian, $\partial F(E)=\nabla F(E)$, and the change of variables formula gives $\operatorname {vol}(\nabla F(E))=\int _ E\det (D^2F)\, \mathrm dx$, so this is a weak form of the real Monge–Ampère operator (see proposition 18.4.4). Two difficulties arise in general: the subgradient map is multivalued, and it is not clear a priori that $\partial F(E)$ is measurable. Both are resolved by looking at the inverse map. By duality, a slope $p$ belongs to $\partial F(E)$ if and only if $E$ meets $\partial F^*(p)$, and $\partial F^*(p)$ is a single point for almost every $p$ in the domain of $F^*$.

Theorem 18.4.1. Let $F$ be a finite convex function on $N_{\mathbb R}$, let $G=F^*$, and let $D$ be the set of points of the interior of $\operatorname {dom}G$ at which $G$ is differentiable. For every Borel set $E\subset N_{\mathbb R}$, the set $\partial F(E)$ is measurable for the completed Lebesgue measure, and

\[ \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)(E)=n!\operatorname {vol}(\partial F(E)) \]

defines a positive Radon measure on $N_{\mathbb R}$, the real Monge–Ampère measure of $F$. It is given by

\[ \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F) =(\nabla G)_*(n!\mathbf1_ D\, \mathrm dp). \]

If $\operatorname {dom}G$ has empty interior and $n>0$, then $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)=0$. In every case

\[ \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)(N_{\mathbb R}) =n!\operatorname {vol}(K_ F). \]

Proof. The set $\operatorname {dom}G$ is convex, and its difference from its interior is a null set: if the interior is nonempty, this difference lies in the relative boundary, which is null by lemma 18.1.2(4); otherwise $\operatorname {dom}G$ lies in a proper affine subspace. By proposition 18.1.3(4), applied to the finite convex function $G$ on the interior of its domain, $D$ is a Borel set, the gradient $\nabla G\colon D\to N_{\mathbb R}$ is a Borel map, and the complement of $D$ in the interior of $\operatorname {dom}G$ is a null set. Therefore the complement of $D$ in $\operatorname {dom}G$ is a null set; it is a Borel set, because $\operatorname {dom}G=\bigcup _ k\{ G\leq k\} $ is a countable union of closed sets, $G$ being lower semicontinuous.

Let $p\in D$. Then $\partial G(p)=\{ \nabla G(p)\} $. Indeed, $\partial G(p)$ is nonempty by proposition 18.1.3, and every subgradient of $G$ at $p$ is also a subgradient at $p$ of the restriction of $G$ to the interior of $\operatorname {dom}G$, whose only subgradient at $p$ is $\nabla G(p)$ by proposition 18.1.3(3). Hence theorem 18.2.2 gives, for every $E\subset N_{\mathbb R}$,

\[ p\in \partial F(E)\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \nabla G(p)\in E. \]

Every subgradient of $F$ lies in $\operatorname {dom}G$, so $\partial F(E)$ differs from the Borel set $(\nabla G)^{-1}(E)\cap D$ by a subset of the fixed null set $\operatorname {dom}G\setminus D$. Hence $\partial F(E)$ is measurable for the completed measure, and $\operatorname {vol}(\partial F(E))=\operatorname {vol}((\nabla G)^{-1}(E)\cap D)$. This proves the pushforward formula, which in turn shows that $E\mapsto n!\operatorname {vol}(\partial F(E))$ is countably additive on Borel sets.

If $K$ is compact, $\partial F(K)$ is bounded by proposition 18.1.3(1), and it is closed, since the graph of $\partial F$ is closed and $K$ is compact; so $\partial F(K)$ is compact and has finite volume. Thus the measure is finite on compact sets, and it is a Radon measure by proposition 1.1.2. If $\operatorname {dom}G$ has empty interior and $n>0$, then $D=\varnothing $ and the measure vanishes. In general, the total mass is $n!\operatorname {vol}(D)=n!\operatorname {vol}(\operatorname {dom}G)$, and $\operatorname {vol}(\operatorname {dom}G)=\operatorname {vol}(K_ F)$ because $K_ F\setminus \operatorname {dom}G$ lies in the relative boundary of $\operatorname {dom}G$, or in a proper affine subspace. In dimension zero, $F$ is a constant, $D$ is the single point of $M_{\mathbb R}$, and the formula gives unit mass, with $0!=1$. $\square$

Theorem 18.4.2. Let $F_ j$ and $F$ be finite convex functions on $N_{\mathbb R}$ such that $F_ j\to F$ locally uniformly. Then $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_ j)\to \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)$ vaguely. Moreover, let $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ be a convex body. A function $F\in \mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ belongs to $\mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ if and only if

\[ \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)(N_{\mathbb R})=n!\operatorname {vol}(P). \]

If $F$ and $H$ are finite convex functions with $F\preceq H$, then $K_ F\subset K_ H$, and the total mass of $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)$ is at most that of $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(H)$.

Proof. Write $\mu _ j=\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_ j)$ and $\mu =\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)$. Steps 1 and 2 compare the masses of compact and of open sets, and Step 3 deduces vague convergence.

Step 1: compact sets. Let $K$ be compact. If $x\in K$ and $0\neq p\in \partial F_ j(x)$ (in particular $n\geq 1$), choose $e\in N_{\mathbb R}$ with $|e|=1$ and $\langle p,e\rangle =|p|$; the subgradient inequality at $x$, evaluated at $x+e$, gives $|p|\leq F_ j(x+e)-F_ j(x)\leq 2\sup \{ |F_ j(y)|:\operatorname {dist}(y,K)\leq 1\} $. By local uniform convergence, the sets $\partial F_ j(K)$ are therefore contained in a fixed ball for all large $j$. If $x_ j\in K$, $p_ j\in \partial F_ j(x_ j)$, $x_ j\to x$ and $p_ j\to p$, then letting $j\to \infty $ in the subgradient inequalities gives $p\in \partial F(x)$. The set $\partial F(K)$ is compact, being bounded and closed by proposition 18.1.3(1), since $K$ is compact. Consequently, for every $\varepsilon >0$, the set $\partial F_ j(K)$ lies in the closed $\varepsilon $-neighborhood $(\partial F(K))^\varepsilon $ of $\partial F(K)$ for all large $j$. Otherwise, for infinitely many $j$ there would be points $x_ j\in K$ and $p_ j\in \partial F_ j(x_ j)$ at distance at least $\varepsilon $ from $\partial F(K)$; as $K$ is compact and the $p_ j$ are bounded, a subsequence of $(x_ j,p_ j)$ would converge to some $(x,p)$ with $p\in \partial F(x)$, which is impossible. As $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, these neighborhoods decrease to $\partial F(K)$, and continuity from above of Lebesgue measure on sets of finite measure gives

\[ \varlimsup _ j\mu _ j(K)\leq \mu (K). \]

Step 2: open sets. Let $U$ be open and let $G=F^*$, with $D$ as in theorem 18.4.1. For $p\in \partial F(U)\cap D$, the point $x=\nabla G(p)$ lies in $U$ and is the only point with $p\in \partial F(x)$, so $x$ is the unique minimum point of $F-\langle p,\cdot \rangle $. Choose $r>0$ with $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset U$. Since the sphere $\{ y:|y-x|=r\} $ is compact, $F-\langle p,\cdot \rangle $ exceeds its minimum by some $\delta >0$ on it. For large $j$ we have $|F_ j-F|<\delta /3$ on $\overline{B(x,r)}$, so the minimum of $F_ j-\langle p,\cdot \rangle $ on $\overline{B(x,r)}$ is attained at a point $x_ j\in B(x,r)$. A local minimum of a convex function is a global minimum, because the function is convex along each segment starting at that point; hence $p\in \partial F_ j(x_ j)\subset \partial F_ j(U)$ for all large $j$. Since $\partial F(U)\setminus D$ is a null set, Fatou's lemma, applied to the indicator functions of the sets $\partial F_ j(U)$, gives

\[ \mu (U)\leq \varliminf _ j\mu _ j(U). \]

Step 3: vague convergence. Let $f\geq 0$ be continuous with compact support $S$. By Step 1 there is $C$ with $\mu _ j(S)\leq C$ for all large $j$. For $t>0$, the set $\{ f\geq t\} $ is compact and $\{ f>t\} $ is open. By Tonelli's theorem, $\int f\, \mathrm d\nu =\int _0^\infty \nu (\{ f>t\} )\, \mathrm dt=\int _0^\infty \nu (\{ f\geq t\} )\, \mathrm dt$ for every positive measure $\nu $. Hence Step 2 and Fatou's lemma give

\[ \begin{split} \int f\, \mathrm d\mu & =\int _0^\infty \mu (\{ f>t\} )\, \mathrm dt\\ & \leq \varliminf _ j\int _0^\infty \mu _ j(\{ f>t\} )\, \mathrm dt=\varliminf _ j\int f\, \mathrm d\mu _ j, \end{split} \]

and Step 1 and Fatou's lemma, applied to the nonnegative functions $t\mapsto C-\mu _ j(\{ f\geq t\} )$ on $(0,\max f]$, give

\[ \begin{split} \varlimsup _ j\int f\, \mathrm d\mu _ j& =\varlimsup _ j\int _0^{\max f}\mu _ j(\{ f\geq t\} )\, \mathrm dt\\ & \leq \int _0^{\max f}\mu (\{ f\geq t\} )\, \mathrm dt=\int f\, \mathrm d\mu . \end{split} \]

Hence $\int f\, \mathrm d\mu _ j\to \int f\, \mathrm d\mu $. A general continuous function with compact support is the difference of its positive and negative parts, which proves vague convergence.

Full mass. Let $F\in \mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P)$. Then $K_ F\subset P$ by proposition 18.3.2, and the total mass of $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)$ is $n!\operatorname {vol}(K_ F)$ by theorem 18.4.1. Suppose that $P$ has nonempty interior. If $K_ F=P$, the two masses agree. If $K_ F\neq P$, choose $p\in P\setminus K_ F$ and a ball $B(p,r)$ disjoint from the closed set $K_ F$. Since $P$ is the closure of its interior by lemma 18.1.2(4), there is an interior point $q$ of $P$ in $B(p,r/2)$, and a small ball about $q$ lies in $P\setminus K_ F$. Hence $\operatorname {vol}(K_ F)<\operatorname {vol}(P)$. This proves the equivalence when $P$ has nonempty interior. If $P$ has empty interior, then $n>0$, both $\operatorname {vol}(K_ F)$ and $\operatorname {vol}(P)$ vanish, and $\mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P)=\mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ by definition, so the equivalence holds as well.

Finally, if $F\leq H+C$, then $F^*\geq H^*-C$, so $\operatorname {dom}F^*\subset \operatorname {dom}H^*$ and $K_ F\subset K_ H$. The inequality between the total masses follows from theorem 18.4.1. $\square$

Example 18.4.3. In theorem 18.4.2, vague convergence cannot be replaced by narrow convergence without a condition that prevents mass from escaping to infinity. Let $n=1$, identify $N_{\mathbb R}$ and $M_{\mathbb R}$ with $\mathbb R$ so that $N=M=\mathbb Z$, and put

\[ F_ j(x)=\max \{ 0,x-j\} \longrightarrow 0, \qquad \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_ j)=\delta _ j. \]

Indeed, $\partial F_ j(x)$ is $\{ 0\} $ for $x<j$, $\{ 1\} $ for $x>j$, and $[0,1]$ for $x=j$. All slopes lie in $[0,1]$, and $F_ j\to 0$ locally uniformly. As in example 1.5.4, the measures $\delta _ j$ converge vaguely to $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(0)=0$, while their total masses remain equal to one. Here each $F_ j$ has full mass for $P=[0,1]$, but the limit does not. If the functions and their limit all have full mass for one fixed body, the measures converge narrowly by lemma 18.5.2 below.

Proposition 18.4.4. Let $F$ be a convex function on $N_{\mathbb R}$ of class $C^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}$, that is, differentiable with locally Lipschitz gradient. Then the distributional Hessian $D^2F$ of $F$, computed in the coordinates of a basis of $N$, is a locally bounded measurable matrix-valued function, and

\[ \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F) =n!\det (D^2F)\, \mathrm dx. \]

Moreover, there is a unique way to assign to every $n$-tuple $(F_1,\ldots ,F_ n)$ of finite convex functions on $N_{\mathbb R}$ a positive Radon measure $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_1,\ldots ,F_ n)$, the mixed real Monge–Ampère measure, which is symmetric in its arguments, additive and positively homogeneous in each argument, and satisfies $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F,\ldots ,F)=\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)$. It is continuous for the vague topology under local uniform convergence of each argument.

Proof. Smooth functions. Let $F$ be smooth with positive definite Hessian. For $x\neq y$,

\[ \langle \nabla F(y)-\nabla F(x),y-x\rangle =\int _0^1D^2F(x+s(y-x))(y-x,y-x)\, \mathrm ds>0, \]

so $\nabla F$ is injective, and it is a local diffeomorphism by the inverse function theorem. Since $\partial F(E)=\nabla F(E)$ by proposition 18.1.3(3), the change of variables formula gives the asserted identity. Now let $F$ be smooth and convex; its Hessian is positive semidefinite, because the second derivatives of $F$ along lines are nonnegative. Apply the identity just proved to $F+\varepsilon |x|^2/2$, where $|x|$ is the Euclidean norm in the lattice coordinates. As $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, these functions converge to $F$ locally uniformly and their densities $n!\det (D^2F+\varepsilon I)$ converge to $n!\det (D^2F)$ locally uniformly; comparing the two vague limits, by theorem 18.4.2, proves the identity for $F$.

Functions of class $C^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}$. We work in the coordinates of a basis of $N$. Fix $k$ and $l$, and let $g=\partial _ kF$, which is locally Lipschitz. Let $B=B(x_0,r)$ be a ball, and let $L_ B$ be a Lipschitz constant of $g$ on $\overline{B(x_0,2r)}$. For a nonnegative test function $\chi $ supported in $B$ and $0<|h|<r$, the substitution $x\mapsto x-he_ l$ in one of the two terms gives

\[ \int g(x)\frac{\chi (x+he_ l)-\chi (x)}h\, \mathrm dx=\int \frac{g(x-he_ l)-g(x)}h\, \chi (x)\, \mathrm dx, \]

and the right side is at most $L_ B\int \chi \, \mathrm dx$ in absolute value. As $h\to 0$, the difference quotients of $\chi $ converge uniformly to $\partial _ l\chi $, with supports in a fixed compact set, so the left side tends to $\int g\, \partial _ l\chi \, \mathrm dx=-\langle \partial _ lg,\chi \rangle $. Hence $|\langle \partial _ l\partial _ kF,\chi \rangle |\leq L_ B\int \chi \, \mathrm dx$, and corollary 1.7.2(2), applied to Lebesgue measure, gives a Borel function $H_{kl}$ that represents the distribution $\partial _ l\partial _ kF$ and satisfies $|H_{kl}|\leq L_ B$ almost everywhere on each such ball $B$. Covering $N_{\mathbb R}$ by countably many such balls and setting $H_{kl}=0$ on the null set of points of some ball of the cover at which the bound fails, we may assume that $H_{kl}$ is bounded on every compact set. This proves that $D^2F=(H_{kl})$ is a locally bounded Borel matrix-valued function. Since distributional derivatives commute, $H_{kl}=H_{lk}$ almost everywhere, by the uniqueness in corollary 1.7.2.

Next, $D^2F(x)$ is positive semidefinite for almost every $x$. Indeed, let $v\in \mathbb R^ n$ and let $\chi $ be a nonnegative test function. Taylor's formula shows that the second difference quotients $h^{-2}(\chi (x+hv)+\chi (x-hv)-2\chi (x))$ converge uniformly to $\sum _{k,l}v_ kv_ l\partial _ k\partial _ l\chi $ as $h\to 0$, so the substitutions $x\mapsto x\mp hv$ give

\[ \Bigl \langle \sum _{k,l}v_ kv_ l\partial _ k\partial _ lF,\chi \Bigr \rangle =\lim _{h\to 0}\int \frac{F(x-hv)+F(x+hv)-2F(x)}{h^2}\, \chi (x)\, \mathrm dx\geq 0, \]

because $F$ is convex. Thus the function $\sum _{k,l}v_ kv_ lH_{kl}$ defines a positive distribution, so its mollifications are nonnegative by proposition 1.4.3(3); since they converge to it at its Lebesgue points (theorem 1.7.3), it is nonnegative almost everywhere. Applying this to a countable dense set of vectors $v$, and using the continuity of the quadratic form in $v$, we see that $D^2F(x)$ is positive semidefinite for almost every $x$.

We now obtain the formula by mollification. With the mollifiers $\rho _\varepsilon $ of lemma 18.3.3, in the same coordinates, the functions $F_\varepsilon =F*\rho _\varepsilon $ are smooth and convex, and they converge to $F$ locally uniformly, by that lemma. By proposition 1.4.3(1), their second derivatives are $\partial _ k\partial _ lF_\varepsilon =H_{kl}*\rho _\varepsilon $. Hence, for $\varepsilon \leq 1$, the Hessians $D^2F_\varepsilon $ are bounded on each compact set $K$ by the bound of $D^2F$ on the set of points at distance at most $1$ from $K$, and by theorem 1.7.3 they converge to $D^2F(x)$ at every point $x$ that is a Lebesgue point of all the entries $H_{kl}$, that is, almost everywhere. By the smooth case, $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_\varepsilon )=n!\det (D^2F_\varepsilon )\, \mathrm dx$. Let $\varepsilon $ run through a sequence tending to zero. For $f\in C_ c(N_{\mathbb R})$, dominated convergence gives $n!\int f\det (D^2F_\varepsilon )\, \mathrm dx\to n!\int f\det (D^2F)\, \mathrm dx$, while $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_\varepsilon )\to \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)$ vaguely by theorem 18.4.2. This proves the formula.

Mixed measures. For smooth convex $F_1,\ldots ,F_ n$ define

\[ \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_1,\ldots ,F_ n)=n!\, D(D^2F_1,\ldots ,D^2F_ n)\, \mathrm dx, \]

where $D$ is the mixed discriminant of section 7.2, applied to the real symmetric, hence Hermitian, Hessian matrices. This measure is positive, because the mixed discriminant of positive semidefinite matrices is nonnegative (lemma 7.2.2(3)), and it is symmetric and multilinear, with $D(A,\ldots ,A)=\det A$ (lemma 7.2.2(1)). Expanding $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(\sum _{j\in J}F_ j,\ldots ,\sum _{j\in J}F_ j)$ by multilinearity and using symmetry, we obtain the polarization identity

18.7
\begin{equation} \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_1,\ldots ,F_ n)=\frac1{n!}\sum _{J\subset \{ 1,\ldots ,n\} } (-1)^{n-|J|}\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R} \left(\sum _{j\in J}F_ j\right), \end{equation}

where the empty sum is the zero function, whose measure is zero for $n>0$ (its only slope is $0$). Indeed, after the expansion, a term $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_{\sigma (1)},\ldots ,F_{\sigma (n)})$ with $\sigma \colon \{ 1,\ldots ,n\} \to \{ 1,\ldots ,n\} $ appears with total coefficient $\sum _{J\supset \sigma (\{ 1,\ldots ,n\} )}(-1)^{n-|J|}$, which vanishes unless $\sigma $ is a bijection, and the $n!$ bijections all give $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_1,\ldots ,F_ n)$ by symmetry. Any symmetric, multilinear assignment with the given diagonal values satisfies (18.7), by the same expansion; this proves uniqueness.

Now let $F_1,\ldots ,F_ n$ be arbitrary finite convex functions, and let $F_{j,\varepsilon }=F_ j*\rho _\varepsilon $ be their mollifications, which are smooth and convex by lemma 18.3.3. For every $J$, $\sum _{j\in J}F_{j,\varepsilon }\to \sum _{j\in J}F_ j$ locally uniformly as $\varepsilon \to 0$ (lemma 1.4.1(1)). Let $\varepsilon $ run through a sequence tending to zero. By (18.7) for the smooth functions $F_{j,\varepsilon }$ and by theorem 18.4.2, for every $f\in C_ c(N_{\mathbb R},\mathbb R)$ the integrals $\int f\, \mathrm d\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_{1,\varepsilon },\ldots ,F_{n,\varepsilon })$ converge to

\[ \Lambda (f)=\frac1{n!}\sum _{J\subset \{ 1,\ldots ,n\} }(-1)^{n-|J|}\int f\, \mathrm d\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}\Bigl (\sum _{j\in J}F_ j\Bigr ). \]

The functional $\Lambda $ is linear, and it is positive as a limit of integrals against positive measures. By theorem 1.2.2, there is a unique Radon measure $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_1,\ldots ,F_ n)$ with $\int f\, \mathrm d\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_1,\ldots ,F_ n)=\Lambda (f)$ for every $f$; for smooth $F_ j$ it agrees with the previous definition, by (18.7). It is symmetric, because $\Lambda $ is a symmetric function of $F_1,\ldots ,F_ n$. Additivity and positive homogeneity in each argument hold for the smooth mixed measures; since mollification is linear, they pass to the vague limit. For $F_1=\cdots =F_ n=F$, the smooth mixed measures are $n!\det (D^2F_\varepsilon )\, \mathrm dx=\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_\varepsilon )$, which converge vaguely to $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)$; hence $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F,\ldots ,F)=\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)$. Finally, if $F_ j^{(k)}\to F_ j$ locally uniformly as $k\to \infty $ for each $j$, then $\sum _{j\in J}F_ j^{(k)}\to \sum _{j\in J}F_ j$ locally uniformly for every $J$, and the formula for $\Lambda $ together with theorem 18.4.2 shows that $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_1^{(k)},\ldots ,F_ n^{(k)})\to \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_1,\ldots ,F_ n)$ vaguely. In dimension zero the mixed measure has no arguments, and it is the unit mass at the point. $\square$

18.5 Compact convex envelopes and full-mass limits

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

We add two complements. First, the convex envelope of finitely many closed convex functions whose effective domains lie in a common compact set, such as the conjugates of finitely many functions in $\mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P)$, is closed, and the infimum defining it is attained. Second, vague convergence of real Monge–Ampère measures improves to narrow convergence when the total masses converge, as they do for functions of full mass.

Lemma 18.5.1. Let $G_1,\ldots ,G_ r$, with $r\geq 1$, be closed proper convex functions on a finite-dimensional real vector space, whose effective domains lie in a common compact set $K$, and let $H$ be the largest convex function below $\min _ iG_ i$, given by the formula of proposition 18.2.3(2). Then $H$ is closed and proper, and at every point $x$ with $H(x)<+\infty $ the infimum defining $H(x)$ is attained. Consequently $H=(\max _ iG_ i^*)^*$. In particular, if $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ is a convex body and $F_1,\ldots ,F_ r\in \mathcal P(N_{\mathbb R},P)$, then the convex envelope of $F_1^*,\ldots ,F_ r^*$ is closed and equals $(\max _ iF_ i)^*$.

Proof. Each $G_ i$ is bounded below: the increasing open sets $\{ G_ i>-k\} $, $k\in \mathbb N$, cover the compact set $K$, because $G_ i$ is lower semicontinuous and does not take the value $-\infty $, so one of them contains $K$; and $G_ i=+\infty $ outside $K$. Let $B$ be a common lower bound. In a combination $\sum _ j\lambda _ jG_{i_ j}(x_ j)$ as in proposition 18.2.3(2), the terms with the same function $G_ i$ can be merged: if $\Lambda _ i$ is the sum of their weights, the convexity of $G_ i$ gives $\sum _{j:i_ j=i}\lambda _ jG_ i(x_ j)\geq \Lambda _ iG_ i(y_ i)$ with $y_ i=\Lambda _ i^{-1}\sum _{j:i_ j=i}\lambda _ jx_ j$, and the barycenter is unchanged. Hence it suffices to consider combinations $\sum _{\lambda _ i>0}\lambda _ iG_ i(x_ i)$ with one point $x_ i\in \operatorname {dom}G_ i$ for each $i$ and weights $\lambda _ i\geq 0$ summing to one; for an index $i$ with $\lambda _ i=0$ we let $x_ i$ be a fixed point of $\operatorname {dom}G_ i$, and the corresponding term is omitted.

Let $x_\nu \to x$ with $\varliminf _\nu H(x_\nu )=L<+\infty $. Passing to a subsequence, we may assume that $H(x_\nu )\to L$, and we choose combinations for $x_\nu $, with points $x_{i,\nu }$ and weights $\lambda _{i,\nu }$, whose values tend to $L$. Since $K$ and the simplex of weights are compact, a further subsequence satisfies $x_{i,\nu }\to x_ i\in K$ and $\lambda _{i,\nu }\to \lambda _ i$ for every $i$. The limiting weights are nonnegative and sum to one, and since the $x_{i,\nu }$ are bounded, the terms with $\lambda _ i=0$ disappear in the limit of the barycenters, so $\sum _{\lambda _ i>0}\lambda _ ix_ i=x$. The nonnegative numbers $\lambda _{i,\nu }(G_ i(x_{i,\nu })-B)$ have sum tending to $L-B$. For $\lambda _ i>0$, lower semicontinuity gives $\varliminf _\nu \lambda _{i,\nu }(G_ i(x_{i,\nu })-B)\geq \lambda _ i(G_ i(x_ i)-B)$, and therefore

\[ \sum _{\lambda _ i>0}\lambda _ i(G_ i(x_ i)-B) \leq \varliminf _\nu \sum _ i \lambda _{i,\nu }(G_ i(x_{i,\nu })-B)=L-B. \]

In particular, all the values $G_ i(x_ i)$ with $\lambda _ i>0$ are finite, and the combination with points $x_ i$ and weights $\lambda _ i>0$ is admissible for $x$, with value at most $L$. Thus $H(x)\leq L$, which proves that $H$ is lower semicontinuous. Moreover $B\leq H\leq G_1$, so $H$ is proper. Applying the same argument to the constant sequence $x_\nu =x$ and admissible combinations whose values tend to $H(x)<+\infty $, we see that the infimum defining $H(x)$ is attained. By proposition 18.2.3(2), $H^*=\max _ iG_ i^*$, and since $H$ is closed and proper, theorem 18.2.2 gives $H=H^{**}=(\max _ iG_ i^*)^*$.

For the last assertion, the conjugates $F_ i^*$ are closed proper convex functions whose effective domains lie in the compact set $P$, by theorem 18.2.2, proposition 18.3.2. Since $F_ i^{**}=F_ i$, the first part gives the formula $(\max _ iF_ i)^*$ for their convex envelope. $\square$

Lemma 18.5.2. Let $\mu _ j$ and $\mu $ be finite positive Radon measures on a finite-dimensional real vector space $W$ such that $\mu _ j\to \mu $ vaguely and $\mu _ j(W)\to \mu (W)$. Then $\int h\, \mathrm d\mu _ j\to \int h\, \mathrm d\mu $ for every bounded continuous function $h$ on $W$, that is, $\mu _ j\to \mu $ narrowly (section 1.5). In particular, if $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ is a convex body, $F_ j,F\in \mathcal E(N_{\mathbb R},P)$ and $F_ j\to F$ locally uniformly, then $\operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F_ j)\to \operatorname {MA}_{\mathbb R}(F)$ narrowly.

Proof. Let $\varepsilon >0$. By inner regularity there is a compact set $S$ with $\mu (W\setminus S)<\varepsilon $; let $\eta $ be a continuous function with compact support, $0\leq \eta \leq 1$ and $\eta =1$ on $S$. Since $\int \eta \, \mathrm d\mu _ j\to \int \eta \, \mathrm d\mu $ and the total masses converge, we have

\[ \lim _ j\int (1-\eta )\, \mathrm d\mu _ j =\int (1-\eta )\, \mathrm d\mu <\varepsilon , \]

so that $\mu _ j(W\setminus \operatorname{Supp}\eta )\leq \int (1-\eta )\, \mathrm d\mu _ j<\varepsilon $ for all large $j$. Thus the sequence $(\mu _ j)$ is eventually uniformly tight, and proposition 1.5.3 gives narrow convergence. (If $W$ is a point, every function on $W$ has compact support, and vague and narrow convergence coincide.) For the real Monge–Ampère measures, theorem 18.4.2 gives vague convergence and the common total mass $n!\operatorname {vol}(P)$. $\square$

18.6 Lattice points and volume

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

By theorem 18.8.5 below, the dimension of a space of sections on a projective toric manifold is the number of lattice points in a dilated polytope. We show that $k^{-n}$ times the number of lattice points in $kK$ tends to the volume of the convex body $K$, and that this remains true if the lattice points are prescribed only up to a sublinear distance from $kK$. The reason is that the boundary of a convex body has volume zero.

In this section we fix a basis of $M$. Its coordinates identify $M_{\mathbb R}$ with $\mathbb R^ n$ and $\operatorname {vol}$ with Lebesgue measure; we use the Euclidean norm of these coordinates on $M_{\mathbb R}$ and the dual norm on $N_{\mathbb R}$. For a convex body $K\subset M_{\mathbb R}$, $k>0$ and $\varepsilon >0$ put $kK=\{ kp:p\in K\} $ and

\[ K^\varepsilon =\{ p:\operatorname {dist}(p,K)\leq \varepsilon \} , \qquad K_\varepsilon =\{ p:\overline{B(p,\varepsilon )}\subset K\} , \]

and $K^0=K$. Since the distance to the compact set $K$ is attained, the outer parallel body $K^\varepsilon $ equals $K+\overline{B(0,\varepsilon )}$, the image of the compact convex set $K\times \overline{B(0,\varepsilon )}$ under addition; so it is a convex body. The inner set $K_\varepsilon =\bigcap _{|y|\leq \varepsilon }(K-y)$ is compact and convex but may be empty; it is empty whenever $K$ has empty interior and $n>0$, because a closed ball of positive radius has interior points.

Definition 18.6.1. The Hausdorff distance between convex bodies $K,L\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ is

\[ d_{\mathrm H}(K,L)=\max \left\{ \sup _{p\in K}\operatorname {dist}(p,L), \sup _{q\in L}\operatorname {dist}(q,K)\right\} . \]

Proposition 18.6.2. If $n>0$, then for convex bodies $K,L\subset M_{\mathbb R}$

\[ d_{\mathrm H}(K,L)=\max _{|x|=1}|h_ K(x)-h_ L(x)|. \]

If convex bodies $K_ j$ converge to a convex body $K$ in this distance, then $\operatorname {vol}(K_ j)\to \operatorname {vol}(K)$. In particular,

\[ \lim _{\varepsilon \downarrow 0}\operatorname {vol}(K^\varepsilon ) =\operatorname {vol}(K)= \lim _{\varepsilon \downarrow 0}\operatorname {vol}(K_\varepsilon ). \]

In dimension zero the only convex body is the point, which has volume one.

Proof. Let $n>0$. If $d_{\mathrm H}(K,L)\leq \varepsilon $, then every $p\in K$ has a point $q\in L$ with $|p-q|\leq \varepsilon $, and for $|x|=1$ we get $\langle p,x\rangle \leq \langle q,x\rangle +\varepsilon \leq h_ L(x)+\varepsilon $; hence $h_ K\leq h_ L+\varepsilon $ on the unit sphere, and by symmetry $|h_ K-h_ L|\leq \varepsilon $ there. Conversely, suppose that $|h_ K-h_ L|\leq \varepsilon $ on the unit sphere. The support function of the convex body $L+\overline{B(0,\varepsilon )}$ is $h_ L(x)+\varepsilon |x|$. If some $p\in K$ were outside $L+\overline{B(0,\varepsilon )}$, lemma 18.1.2(1) would give a unit vector $x$ with $\langle p,x\rangle >h_ L(x)+\varepsilon \geq h_ K(x)$, which is impossible. Hence $K\subset L+\overline{B(0,\varepsilon )}$, and by symmetry $d_{\mathrm H}(K,L)\leq \varepsilon $. This proves the formula. It shows that $d_{\mathrm H}$ satisfies the triangle inequality, and by lemma 18.1.2(1) a convex body is determined by its support function; thus $d_{\mathrm H}$ is a metric.

Let $\varepsilon _ j=d_{\mathrm H}(K_ j,K)\to 0$. For large $j$ all $K_ j$ lie in the bounded set $K+\overline{B(0,1)}$. A point $p$ outside $K$ has positive distance from $K$, so $p\notin K_ j$ once $\varepsilon _ j<\operatorname {dist}(p,K)$. If $p$ is an interior point of $K$, choose $r>0$ with $\overline{B(p,r)}\subset K$. Then, for $|x|=1$,

\[ h_{K_ j}(x)\geq h_ K(x)-\varepsilon _ j \geq \langle p,x\rangle +r-\varepsilon _ j. \]

If $\varepsilon _ j<r$ and $p\notin K_ j$, strict separation would give a unit vector $x$ with $\langle p,x\rangle >h_{K_ j}(x)$, contradicting this inequality; hence $p\in K_ j$ for large $j$. Thus $\mathbf1_{K_ j}\to \mathbf1_ K$ outside the boundary of $K$. If $K$ has nonempty interior, its boundary is a null set by lemma 18.1.2(4); if $K$ has empty interior, it lies in a proper affine subspace and is itself a null set, and $\mathbf1_{K_ j}\to 0=\mathbf1_ K$ almost everywhere by the outside-point argument. In either case dominated convergence, with the dominating function $\mathbf1_{K+\overline{B(0,1)}}$, gives $\operatorname {vol}(K_ j)\to \operatorname {vol}(K)$.

The outer parallel bodies $K^\varepsilon $ have finite volume and decrease to the compact set $K$ as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, so continuity of measure from above gives the first limit. The inner sets $K_\varepsilon $ increase, as $\varepsilon $ decreases, to the interior of $K$, which differs from $K$ by a null set, as we saw in the previous paragraph; monotone convergence gives the second limit. In dimension zero both $K^\varepsilon $ and $K_\varepsilon $ equal the single point for every $\varepsilon $. $\square$

Theorem 18.6.3. Let $K\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ be a convex body, let $a_ k\in M_{\mathbb R}$ be arbitrary, and let $r_ k\geq 0$ satisfy $r_ k/k\to 0$. Then for every continuous function $f\colon M_{\mathbb R}\to \mathbb R$,

\[ \lim _{k\to \infty }\frac1{k^ n} \sum _{m\in (kK)^{r_ k}\cap (M+a_ k)}f(m/k) =\int _ K f(p)\, \mathrm dp. \]

In particular $k^{-n}\# (kK\cap M)\to \operatorname {vol}(K)$. Here the exponent $n$ and the measure are those of the ambient space, also when $K$ has empty interior. The convergence is uniform with respect to the translations $a_ k$.

Proof. For $n=0$ every set in question is the single point, and both sides equal its value under $f$. Suppose that $n>0$, and let $Q=[0,1)^ n$ be the half-open unit cube in the chosen coordinates. The cells

\[ \frac mk+\frac1k Q,\qquad m\in M+a_ k, \]

partition $M_{\mathbb R}$; each has volume $k^{-n}$, and its points lie within distance $c/k$ of its anchor $m/k$, where $c=\sqrt n$. Let $U_ k$ be the union of the cells whose anchors $m/k$ occur in the sum, that is, with $m/k\in K^{r_ k/k}$. Then

\[ K_{c/k}\subset U_ k\subset K^{(r_ k+c)/k}. \]

For the first inclusion, if $p\in K_{c/k}$, the anchor of the cell containing $p$ lies within $c/k$ of $p$, hence in $K$, so the cell is selected. For the second, every point of a selected cell lies within $c/k$ of its anchor, which lies within $r_ k/k$ of $K$. Since $K_{c/k}\subset K\subset K^{(r_ k+c)/k}$ and $(r_ k+c)/k\to 0$, proposition 18.6.2 gives $\operatorname {vol}(U_ k\mathbin \triangle K)\to 0$, and the volumes $\operatorname {vol}(U_ k)$ stay bounded.

For large $k$ the selected cells lie in the fixed compact set $K^1$. Let $\omega _ f$ be the modulus of continuity of $f$ on $K^1$. Since $k^{-n}f(m/k)$ is the integral of the constant $f(m/k)$ over the cell of $m$, we obtain

\[ \left|k^{-n}\sum _ m f(m/k)-\int _{U_ k}f(p)\, \mathrm dp\right| \leq \omega _ f(c/k)\operatorname {vol}(U_ k)\longrightarrow 0. \]

Since $f$ is bounded on $K^1$, the symmetric-difference estimate gives $\int _{U_ k}f\, \mathrm dp\to \int _ Kf\, \mathrm dp$. Taking $f=1$, $r_ k=0$ and $a_ k=0$ proves the counting formula. All the estimates depend only on the size of the cells, and not on their position, so they hold uniformly in the translations $a_ k$. $\square$

Corollary 18.6.4. Let $K\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ be a convex body, and suppose that subsets $S_ k\subset M$ satisfy

\[ kK\cap M\subset S_ k\subset (kK)^{r_ k}\cap M, \qquad r_ k\geq 0,\quad r_ k/k\to 0. \]

Then $k^{-n}\# S_ k\to \operatorname {vol}(K)$. In particular, a boundary error of bounded size does not affect the leading term of the count, even when $K$ has zero volume.

Proof. By theorem 18.6.3 with $f=1$ and $a_ k=0$, both $k^{-n}\# (kK\cap M)$ and $k^{-n}\# ((kK)^{r_ k}\cap M)$ tend to $\operatorname {vol}(K)$, and $\# S_ k$ lies between these two counts. $\square$

18.7 Smooth lattice polytopes and projective charts

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

So far a convex body $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ has served as a bound for the slopes of convex functions. When $P$ is a lattice polytope, it also determines a compactification of the complex torus with character group $M$. If $P$ is smooth, the primitive edge directions at each vertex form a basis of $M$, and the corresponding characters become holomorphic coordinates on a chart isomorphic to $\mathbb C^ n$, which reaches across the boundary of the torus.

Definition 18.7.1. A lattice polytope in $M_{\mathbb R}$ is the convex hull of finitely many points of $M$. A face of a lattice polytope $P$ is a set of the form $\{ m\in P:\langle m,x\rangle =\min _{m'\in P}\langle m',x\rangle \} $ with $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$; faces are nonempty, and $P$ itself is the face for $x=0$. Vertices, edges and facets are the faces of dimension zero, of dimension one and of codimension one, respectively. A nonzero vector of a lattice is primitive if it is not of the form $ky$ with $y$ in the lattice and an integer $k\geq 2$. Let $P$ have nonempty interior. Its vertices are lattice points, each edge is a segment $[v,v']$ between two vertices, and a vertex contained in an edge is one of its endpoints (lemma 18.7.2). Thus an edge $[v,v']$ containing a vertex $v$ determines the primitive edge direction at $v$, the unique primitive vector of $M$ that is a positive multiple of $v'-v$ (see below). The polytope $P$ is smooth, or Delzant, if at each vertex its primitive edge directions form a basis of $M$. In dimension zero the single point is smooth, with the empty basis.

Every nonzero vector $y$ of a lattice is a positive multiple of exactly one primitive vector. Indeed, if $d$ is the greatest common divisor of the coordinates of $y$ in a basis of the lattice, then $y/d$ is primitive, since its coordinates are coprime. If $y'$ and $y''$ are primitive and $y'=cy''$ with $c>0$, then $c$ is a quotient of integer coordinates, say $c=a/b$ with coprime positive integers $a$ and $b$, and $by'=ay''$ shows that $b$ divides all coordinates of $y''$ and $a$ all those of $y'$; hence $a=b=1$.

The cone $\operatorname {cone}(S)$ generated by a set $S$ consists of its finite nonnegative linear combinations. A ray is a half-line $\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$ with $y\neq 0$, and a ray contained in a cone $C$ is extreme if, whenever a vector of the ray is the sum of two vectors of $C$, both summands lie on the ray.

An affine function $\ell $ that is nonnegative on a convex set $C$ and vanishes at a point $b\in \operatorname {ri}C$ vanishes on $C$. Indeed, for $y\in C$ the point $b-\varepsilon (y-b)$ lies in $C$ for small $\varepsilon >0$, and $0=\ell (b)$ is a convex combination, with positive weights, of the nonnegative numbers $\ell (y)$ and $\ell (b-\varepsilon (y-b))$; hence $\ell (y)=0$.

Lemma 18.7.2. Let $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ be a lattice polytope; in (2)–(4) assume moreover that $P$ has nonempty interior.

  1. The vertices of $P$ are lattice points, $P$ is their convex hull, every face of $P$ is the convex hull of the vertices it contains, and $P$ has finitely many faces. Every edge of $P$ is a segment $[v,v']$ between two distinct vertices, and a vertex contained in an edge is one of its endpoints.

  2. $P$ has an irredundant facet presentation

    \[ P=\{ m:\ell _\rho (m)=\langle m,u_\rho \rangle +a_\rho \geq 0 \text{ for every facet }\rho \} , \]

    where, for each facet $\rho $, the normal $u_\rho \in N$ is primitive and inward pointing, $a_\rho \in \mathbb Z$, and $\rho =\{ m\in P:\ell _\rho (m)=0\} $.

  3. At a vertex $v$, the tangent cone

    \[ C_ v=\{ y:\langle y,u_\rho \rangle \geq 0 \text{ for the facets }\rho \ni v\} =\operatorname {cone}(P-v) \]

    is generated by its extreme rays, and these are the rays spanned by the edge directions at $v$.

  4. If $P$ is smooth and $v$ is a vertex with primitive edge basis $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$, then exactly $n$ facets contain $v$, and their normals $u_1,\ldots ,u_ n$ form the dual basis of $N$, that is, $\langle e_ i,u_ j\rangle =\delta _{ij}$. Thus

    \[ m-v=\sum _ i t_ i e_ i,\qquad t_ i=\ell _ i(m)\geq 0\quad (m\in P), \]

    and $v+e_ i\in P\cap M$ for each $i$.

Proof. (1) Let $P$ be the convex hull of a finite set $S\subset M$, and discard points of $S$ one at a time as long as some point lies in the convex hull of the others; this does not change $P$. If only one point remains, $P$ is that point, it is the only face, and all assertions hold. Otherwise, for a remaining point $s$, lemma 18.1.2(1) gives $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ with $\langle s,x\rangle <\langle s',x\rangle $ for all $s'$ in the convex hull of $S\setminus \{ s\} $. Every point of $P$ is a convex combination $\lambda s+(1-\lambda )s''$ with $s''$ in that hull, so $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ attains its minimum on $P$ only at $s$, and $\{ s\} $ is a vertex. Conversely, let $Q$ be any face, the set where $\langle \cdot ,x'\rangle $ is minimal for some $x'\in N_{\mathbb R}$, and write a point $m\in Q$ as a convex combination of points of $S$: all points with positive coefficient minimize $\langle \cdot ,x'\rangle $, hence lie in $Q$. Thus $Q$ is the convex hull of the points of $S$ it contains. Applied to a vertex $\{ v\} $, this shows $v\in S$. Hence the vertices are exactly the remaining points of $S$, and in particular lattice points. Every face is the convex hull of the vertices it contains, so there are only finitely many faces.

Let $\{ v\} $ be a vertex, the set where some $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ is minimal on $P$. Then $v$ is not of the form $(1-t)a+tb$ with $a\neq b$ in $P$ and $0<t<1$: the values $\langle a,x\rangle $ and $\langle b,x\rangle $ are at least $\langle v,x\rangle $, and their weighted mean equals it, so $a$ and $b$ would also be minimum points, that is, $a=b=v$. An edge is the convex hull of the finitely many vertices it contains, which lie on a line, so it is the segment between the two outermost ones; a vertex in the edge is not an interior point of the segment, hence it is an endpoint.

(2) We first show that every boundary point $b$ of $P$ lies in a facet. By lemma 18.1.2(2), $b$ lies in a face $Q$ where some $x\neq 0$ is minimal; this face is proper, since $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ is not constant on the full-dimensional set $P$. Suppose that $Q$ has dimension $d<n-1$, and let $c$ be the minimum of $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ on $P$. The linear functionals vanishing on the directions of $Q$ form a space of dimension $n-d\geq 2$, which contains $x$; choose $y$ in it, independent of $x$, and put $x_ s=x+sy$. On $Q$ the functional $\langle \cdot ,y\rangle $ is constant, say equal to $c'$, and for each vertex $w\notin Q$ consider the gap $g_ w(s)=\langle w,x_ s\rangle -(c+sc')=(\langle w,x\rangle -c)+s(\langle w,y\rangle -c')$, which is positive at $s=0$. Some vertex $w\notin Q$ has $\langle w,y\rangle \neq c'$, since otherwise $y$ would be constant on $P$ and hence zero; replacing $y$ by $-y$ if necessary, we may assume that $\langle w,y\rangle <c'$ for some such $w$. Let $s_*>0$ be the first value of $s>0$ at which some gap vanishes. Then $x_{s_*}\neq 0$ by independence, all vertices of $Q$ and at least one vertex $w\notin Q$ minimize $\langle \cdot ,x_{s_*}\rangle $ on the vertices, and hence, by (1), on $P$. The new face contains $Q$ and $w$, and $w$ does not lie in the affine span of $Q$, because $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ equals $c$ on that span and is larger at $w$. So the dimension of the face has increased, and after finitely many steps we reach a facet containing $b$.

A facet $\rho $ contains $n$ affinely independent vertices, which are lattice points by (1). The linear functionals vanishing on the differences of these points form a line, which is defined by linear equations with integral coefficients in the coordinates of bases of $M$ and $N$; Gaussian elimination over $\mathbb Q$ produces a nonzero rational solution, and clearing denominators gives a nonzero vector of $N$ on the line. Since $\rho $ is a face, some $x\neq 0$ attains its minimum on $P$ exactly on $\rho $; this $x$ lies on the line, and we let $u_\rho $ be the primitive vector of $N$ that is a positive multiple of $x$, which exists and is unique by the observation following definition 18.7.1. Then $a_\rho =-\langle v,u_\rho \rangle $, for any vertex $v\in \rho $, is an integer, $\ell _\rho \geq 0$ on $P$, and $\rho =\{ m\in P:\ell _\rho (m)=0\} $.

Next we show that $P$ is the intersection of the half-spaces $\{ \ell _\rho \geq 0\} $. Let $q\notin P$ and let $p$ be an interior point of $P$. The set of $s\in [0,1]$ with $p+s(q-p)\in P$ is an interval $[0,s_0]$ with $s_0<1$, and $b=p+s_0(q-p)$ is a boundary point of $P$, hence lies in some facet $\rho $. The affine function $s\mapsto \ell _\rho (p+s(q-p))$ is positive at $s=0$, because the nonconstant affine function $\ell _\rho $, which is nonnegative on $P$, cannot attain its minimum at the interior point $p$. It vanishes at $s_0<1$, so $\ell _\rho (q)<0$.

For irredundancy, let $b$ be a point of the relative interior of a facet $\rho $, which exists by lemma 18.1.2(4). If $\ell _{\rho '}(b)=0$ for another facet $\rho '$, then $\ell _{\rho '}$, which is nonnegative on $\rho $, vanishes on $\rho $ by the observation preceding the lemma. Then $u_{\rho '}$ is constant on $\rho $, whose differences span a hyperplane, so $u_{\rho '}$ lies on the line of $u_\rho $, and $u_{\rho '}=\pm u_\rho $, because both vectors are primitive. If $u_{\rho '}=-u_\rho $, then $\ell _{\rho '}=-\ell _\rho $, since both vanish on $\rho $; as both are nonnegative on $P$, $\ell _\rho $ would vanish on $P$, which is impossible at an interior point. Hence $\ell _{\rho '}=\ell _\rho $ and $\rho '=\rho $. Thus all other facet functions are positive at $b$, and a point near $b$ on the side where $\ell _\rho <0$ violates only the inequality of $\rho $.

(3) If $m\in P$ and $v\in \rho $, then $\langle m-v,u_\rho \rangle =\ell _\rho (m)\geq 0$; hence $\operatorname {cone}(P-v)\subset C_ v$. Conversely, if $y\in C_ v$, then $v+sy\in P$ for all small $s>0$, because the inequalities of the facets containing $v$ hold along this half-line and the finitely many other facet functions are positive at $v$; hence $y\in \operatorname {cone}(P-v)$. The cone $C_ v$ contains no line: if $y\neq 0$ and $\pm y\in C_ v$, then $v\pm sy\in P$ for small $s>0$, and $v$ would be the midpoint of two points of $P$, which is impossible for a vertex. Let $u$ be the sum of the normals $u_\rho $ of the facets containing $v$. If $y\in C_ v$ and $\langle y,u\rangle =0$, then $\langle y,u_\rho \rangle =0$ for all these facets, so $-y\in C_ v$ and $y=0$. Consequently, the slice $\Sigma =\{ y\in C_ v:\langle y,u\rangle =1\} $ is bounded (otherwise a limit of normalized unbounded points would give a nonzero $y\in C_ v$ with $\langle y,u\rangle =0$), and $C_ v=\operatorname {cone}(\Sigma )$.

Call a point $y\in \Sigma $ basic if the normals $u_\rho $ of the facets $\rho \ni v$ with $\langle y,u_\rho \rangle =0$ span a space of dimension $n-1$. Every point $y\in \Sigma $ is a convex combination of basic points. Indeed, if the active normals of $y$, together with $u$, span a space of dimension less than $n$, choose $d\neq 0$ annihilated by all of them. For small $|s|$ the points $y+sd$ remain in $\Sigma $, and since $\Sigma $ is bounded there are maximal $s_\pm >0$ with $y\pm s_\pm d\in \Sigma $. At $y\pm s_\pm d$ some new facet function $\langle \cdot ,u_{\rho '}\rangle $ vanishes, and $\langle d,u_{\rho '}\rangle \neq 0$, so $u_{\rho '}$ is not in the span of the previous active normals and $u$. Thus $y$ is a convex combination of two points with a larger span of active normals and $u$. Induction on the codimension of this span therefore proves the claim, because a point $y\in \Sigma $ whose active normals together with $u$ span $N_{\mathbb R}$ is basic: its active normals annihilate $y\neq 0$, so they span a space of dimension at most $n-1$, and at least $n-1$, since adding $u$ raises the dimension by at most one.

Next, $y\in \Sigma $ is basic if and only if $\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$ is an extreme ray. If $y$ is basic, its active normals annihilate exactly the line $\mathbb Ry$; if $y=y_1+y_2$ with $y_1,y_2\in C_ v$, then for each active normal both pairings $\langle y_ k,u_\rho \rangle $ are nonnegative with sum zero, hence zero, so $y_1,y_2\in \mathbb Ry\cap C_ v=\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$. Conversely, if $\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$ is extreme, write $y$ as a convex combination of basic points; extremality forces each of them to lie on the ray, hence to equal $y$. Therefore $C_ v$ is generated by its extreme rays.

It remains to identify the extreme rays with the edges at $v$. If $y\in \Sigma $ is basic, the set $\{ m\in P:\ell _\rho (m)=0\text{ for the active facets of }y\} $ is a face, namely the set where the sum of the corresponding functionals $\langle \cdot ,u_\rho \rangle $ is minimal; it contains $v+sy$ for small $s>0$ and is contained in $v+(C_ v\cap (\mathbb Ry))=v+\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$. Hence it is an edge at $v$ with direction $y$. Conversely, let $E$ be an edge at $v$ with direction $y$, so $y\in C_ v$. If $y=y_1+y_2$ with $y_1,y_2\in C_ v$, then for small $s>0$ the point $v+sy\in E$ is the midpoint of $v+2sy_1$ and $v+2sy_2$, which lie in $P$. Since $E$ is the set where some linear functional is minimal on $P$, both points lie in $E\subset v+\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$, and so $y_1,y_2\in \mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$. Thus the ray of $y$ is extreme.

(4) Let $P$ be smooth and $v$ a vertex. By (3), $C_ v=\operatorname {cone}(e_1,\ldots ,e_ n)$. Let $e_1^*,\ldots ,e_ n^*\in N$ be the dual basis, so that $C_ v=\{ y:\langle y,e_ i^*\rangle \geq 0\text{ for all }i\} $. If $\rho $ is a facet containing $v$, then $u_\rho \geq 0$ on $C_ v$, so $u_\rho =\sum _ ic_ ie_ i^*$ with $c_ i=\langle e_ i,u_\rho \rangle \geq 0$. The set $\rho -v$ lies in $C_ v\cap u_\rho ^\perp =\operatorname {cone}\{ e_ i:c_ i=0\} $ and spans a space of dimension $n-1$, so exactly one coefficient $c_ i$ is positive. Since $u_\rho $ and $e_ i^*$ are primitive, $u_\rho =e_ i^*$. Distinct facets have distinct normals, because a facet is the set where its normal is minimal; so at most $n$ facets contain $v$. If some $e_ i^*$ were not the normal of a facet through $v$, the vector $-e_ i$ would satisfy all the inequalities defining $C_ v$, although $-e_ i\notin \operatorname {cone}(e_1,\ldots ,e_ n)$. Hence exactly $n$ facets contain $v$, and numbering them suitably, $u_ i=e_ i^*$. For $m\in P$ this gives $m-v=\sum _ i\langle m-v,u_ i\rangle e_ i=\sum _ i\ell _ i(m)e_ i$, because $\ell _ i(v)=0$. Finally, the edge in direction $e_ i$ is a segment $[v,v']$ with $v'$ a lattice point, so $v'-v=ke_ i$ for a positive integer $k$, and $v+e_ i\in [v,v']\subset P$. In dimension zero there are no facets and no edges, and the assertions are empty. $\square$

For the rest of the chapter we fix a smooth lattice polytope $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$, which by definition has nonempty interior, with the notation $\ell _\rho $, $u_\rho $ and $a_\rho $ of lemma 18.7.2, and we put $A=P\cap M$, a finite set containing the vertices. Let $T=\operatorname {Hom}(M,\mathbb C^*)$ be the group of homomorphisms from $M$ to the multiplicative group $\mathbb C^*$, and $T_ c=\operatorname {Hom}(M,S^1)$ its compact subgroup, where $S^1=\{ \zeta \in \mathbb C:|\zeta |=1\} $. Each $m\in M$ defines a character $\chi ^ m\colon T\to \mathbb C^*$, $\chi ^ m(t)=t(m)$, so that $\chi ^{m+m'}=\chi ^ m\chi ^{m'}$. A basis $m_1,\ldots ,m_ n$ of $M$ identifies $T$ with $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ through $t\mapsto (\chi ^{m_1}(t),\ldots ,\chi ^{m_ n}(t))$, since a homomorphism on $M$ is determined by its values on a basis, which may be arbitrary. The resulting structure of complex manifold on $T$ does not depend on the basis, because a change of basis acts on the coordinates by a monomial map whose inverse is again monomial. In these coordinates $\chi ^ m$ becomes the Laurent monomial $z_1^{c_1}\cdots z_ n^{c_ n}$ for $m=\sum _ jc_ jm_ j$, and $T_ c$ becomes $(S^1)^ n$, acting on $T=(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ by multiplication, that is, by separate rotations of the coordinates. The tropicalization map $\operatorname {Trop}\colon T\to N_{\mathbb R}$ is defined by

\[ \langle m,\operatorname {Trop}(t)\rangle =\log |\chi ^ m(t)|^2\qquad (m\in M); \]

in the coordinates above, with $N_{\mathbb R}$ identified with $\mathbb R^ n$ by the dual basis, it is $z\mapsto (\log |z_1|^2,\ldots ,\log |z_ n|^2)$. By corollary 18.1.6, the $T_ c$-invariant plurisubharmonic functions on $T$ are exactly the functions $F\circ \operatorname {Trop}$ with $F$ finite and convex on $N_{\mathbb R}$. (In dimension zero, $T$ and $N_{\mathbb R}$ are points, and by definition 8.1.1 both classes consist of the real constants.)

Let $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$ be the complex projective space of lines in $\mathbb C^ A$, with homogeneous coordinates $Z=[Z_ m]_{m\in A}$. By example 14.5.5, with homogeneous coordinates indexed by the finite set $A$, it is a compact complex manifold of dimension $|A|-1$, with the affine charts $\{ Z_{m_0}\neq 0\} $, $m_0\in A$, and the coordinates $Z_ m/Z_{m_0}$, $m\neq m_0$. It carries the hyperplane bundle $\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^{|A|-1}}(1)$, dual to the tautological line bundle, with the Fubini–Study metric, and each linear form $Z\mapsto Z_ m$ defines a holomorphic coordinate section of $\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^{|A|-1}}(1)$, which we denote again by $Z_ m$. Define

\[ \iota \colon T\longrightarrow \mathbb P^{|A|-1},\qquad t\longmapsto [\chi ^ m(t)]_{m\in A}, \qquad X_ P=\overline{\iota (T)}. \]

The torus $T$ acts on $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$ by $\tau \cdot [Z_ m]=[\chi ^ m(\tau )Z_ m]$. Since $\iota (\tau t)=\tau \cdot \iota (t)$, this action preserves $\iota (T)$ and hence its closure $X_ P$.

Theorem 18.7.3. The set $X_ P$ is an $n$-dimensional complex submanifold of $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$; it is the common zero set of finitely many homogeneous polynomials and is irreducible, so it is a smooth projective variety of dimension $n$. The map $\iota $ is a biholomorphism of $T$ onto a dense open subset of $X_ P$. For each vertex $v$ of $P$, with primitive edge basis $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$, the set $U_ v=X_ P\cap \{ Z_ v\neq 0\} $ is open in $X_ P$, and

\[ w=(w_1,\ldots ,w_ n),\qquad w_ i=Z_{v+e_ i}/Z_ v, \]

is a biholomorphism of $U_ v$ onto $\mathbb C^ n$ satisfying $w_ i\circ \iota =\chi ^{e_ i}$. These vertex charts cover $X_ P$. For $m\in A$ write $m-v=\sum _ i b_ i e_ i$; then the $b_ i$ are nonnegative integers and $Z_ m/Z_ v=\prod _ i w_ i^{b_ i}$ on $U_ v$. Finally, the restriction $L=\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^{|A|-1}}(1)|_{X_ P}$ of the hyperplane bundle is very ample, and the restriction of the Fubini–Study metric is a smooth quadratic metric on $L$ with positive definite curvature form.

Proof. Every point of $X_ P$ has a nonzero vertex coordinate. Let $m\in A$. By lemma 18.7.2(1), $m$ lies in the convex hull of the vertices of $P$, so by lemma 18.1.2(3) it is a convex combination of affinely independent vertices. The coefficients of this combination are the unique solution of a linear system with integral coefficients, hence rational. Clearing denominators, we obtain

\[ d m=\sum _ v c_ v v,\qquad c_ v\in \mathbb Z_{\geq 0},\quad \sum _ v c_ v=d\geq 1. \]

Since $\chi ^{dm}=\prod _ v(\chi ^ v)^{c_ v}$, the homogeneous equation $Z_ m^ d=\prod _ v Z_ v^{c_ v}$ of degree $d$ holds on $\iota (T)$, hence on its closure $X_ P$. If all vertex coordinates of a point of $X_ P$ vanished, these equations would force all its coordinates to vanish, which is impossible.

The vertex charts. Fix a vertex $v$. For $m\in A$, lemma 18.7.2(4) gives $m-v=\sum _ ib_ i(m)e_ i$ with $b_ i(m)=\ell _ i(m)=\langle m,u_ i\rangle +a_ i$, a nonnegative integer; we write $b(m)=(b_1(m),\ldots ,b_ n(m))$. In particular $b(v+e_ i)$ is the $i$th unit vector, and on $\iota (T)$

\[ \frac{Z_ m}{Z_ v}=\chi ^{m-v}=\prod _ i(\chi ^{e_ i})^{b_ i(m)}=\prod _ iw_ i^{b_ i(m)}. \]

Let $\Phi _ v\colon \mathbb C^ n\to \{ Z_ v\neq 0\} $ be the map whose coordinates $Z_ m/Z_ v$ are the monomials $\prod _ iw_ i^{b_ i(m)}$. Its image $\Gamma _ v$ is the graph of a polynomial map $\gamma $ from the coordinates $w_ i=Z_{v+e_ i}/Z_ v$ of the affine chart $\{ Z_ v\neq 0\} $ to the remaining coordinates $y=(Z_ m/Z_ v)_{m\neq v,v+e_1,\ldots ,v+e_ n}$. Hence $\Gamma _ v$ is closed in the chart, and in the holomorphic coordinates $(w,y-\gamma (w))$ of the chart it is the coordinate plane $\{ y-\gamma (w)=0\} $; so $\Gamma _ v$ is a closed complex submanifold of the chart, and $w$ is a biholomorphism of $\Gamma _ v$ onto $\mathbb C^ n$, with inverse $\Phi _ v$. Since $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$ is a basis of $M$, the map $t\mapsto (\chi ^{e_1}(t),\ldots ,\chi ^{e_ n}(t))$ is a bijection of $T$ onto $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$, and the last display shows that $\iota (T)=\Phi _ v((\mathbb C^*)^ n)$. As $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ is dense in $\mathbb C^ n$ and $\Gamma _ v$ is closed in the chart, the closure of $\iota (T)$ in the chart is $\Gamma _ v$; that is, $U_ v=\Gamma _ v$. By the first step the sets $U_ v$ cover $X_ P$, so $X_ P$ is a complex submanifold of dimension $n$ with the asserted charts. The coordinates $w_ i$ recover $t$ from $\iota (t)$, so $\iota $ is injective with holomorphic inverse $w$ on $\iota (T)$, and $\iota (T)=\Phi _ v((\mathbb C^*)^ n)$ is open in $U_ v$. Thus $\iota $ is a biholomorphism of $T$ onto a dense open subset of $X_ P$.

Algebraicity and irreducibility. In addition to the equations of the first step, consider for each vertex $v$ and each $m\in A\setminus \{ v\} $ the homogeneous equation

\[ Z_ m Z_ v^{\sum _ i b_ i(m)-1}=\prod _ i Z_{v+e_ i}^{b_ i(m)}, \]

where $\sum _ ib_ i(m)\geq 1$ because $m\neq v$. It holds on $\iota (T)$, hence on $X_ P$. Let $Y$ be the common zero set of all these equations in $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$, so that $X_ P\subset Y$. By the equations of the first step, every point of $Y$ has a nonzero vertex coordinate $Z_ v$, and on $\{ Z_ v\neq 0\} $ the equations for $v$ say exactly that $Z_ m/Z_ v=\prod _ iw_ i^{b_ i(m)}$; thus $Y\cap \{ Z_ v\neq 0\} \subset \Gamma _ v=U_ v$. Hence $Y=X_ P$. To prove irreducibility, suppose that $X_ P=Y_1\cup Y_2$, where $Y_1,Y_2\subsetneq X_ P$ are common zero sets of homogeneous polynomials, and choose homogeneous polynomials $f_1,f_2$ with $f_ k=0$ on $Y_ k$ and $f_ k\not\equiv 0$ on $X_ P$. Then $g_ k(t)=f_ k((\chi ^ m(t))_{m\in A})$ is a Laurent polynomial in the coordinates of $T\cong (\mathbb C^*)^ n$. It is not identically zero: otherwise $f_ k$ would vanish on $\iota (T)$, hence on its closure $X_ P$, since the zero set of a homogeneous polynomial is closed. On the other hand $g_1g_2=0$ on $T$, because $f_1f_2=0$ on $X_ P$. This is impossible. Indeed, since $g_1$ is continuous and not identically zero, it does not vanish on some nonempty open set $W\subset (\mathbb C^*)^ n$, and $g_2$ vanishes on $W$. A suitable monomial multiple of $g_2$ is a polynomial that vanishes on $W$, so all its derivatives vanish at a point of $W$; being the sum of its Taylor expansion at that point, it is zero. Hence $g_2=0$, a contradiction. Thus $X_ P$ is irreducible.

The line bundle. The coordinate sections $s_ m=Z_ m|_{X_ P}$, $m\in A$, of $L$ have no common zero and define the inclusion $X_ P\subset \mathbb P^{|A|-1}$. By theorem 18.8.5 with $k=1$, whose proof uses only the vertex charts constructed above, they form a basis of $H^0(X_ P,L)$; in the notation of that theorem, $s_ m=\chi ^ ms_0$. Hence the full space of sections of $L$ defines an embedding, and $L$ is very ample in the sense of section 14.5. On $U_ v$ the section $s_ v$ has no zeros, and the Fubini–Study metric of example 14.5.5, example 14.5.6, restricted to $L$, gives it the squared norm

\[ |s_ v|^2=\frac{|Z_ v|^2}{\sum _{m\in A}|Z_ m|^2}=\mathrm e^{-H_ v},\qquad H_ v=\log \sum _{m\in A}|w^{b(m)}|^2, \]

where $w^ b=\prod _ iw_ i^{b_ i}$. The curvature on $U_ v$ is $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}H_ v$, by definition 13.1.8. The complex Hessian of $H_ v$ is positive definite. Indeed, put $f_ m=w^{b(m)}$ and $S=\sum _ m|f_ m|^2$, and for $\xi \in \mathbb C^ n$ write $\xi f_ m=\sum _ j\xi _ j\partial f_ m/\partial w_ j$. Then

\[ \sum _{j,k}\frac{\partial ^2H_ v}{\partial w_ j\partial \bar w_ k}\xi _ j\bar\xi _ k =\frac{S\sum _ m|\xi f_ m|^2-\bigl |\sum _ m\bar f_ m\, \xi f_ m\bigr |^2}{S^2}\geq 0 \]

by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, with equality only if $(\xi f_ m)_ m=\lambda (f_ m)_ m$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb C$. The constant monomial $f_ v=1$ forces $\lambda =0$, and then the monomials $f_{v+e_ i}=w_ i$ give $\xi _ i=0$ for every $i$. Thus the restricted Fubini–Study metric is a smooth quadratic metric on $L$ with positive definite curvature form. In dimension zero $X_ P$ is a point and there is nothing to prove. $\square$

18.8 Fans, divisors and character sections

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

We now use the vertex charts to describe the complement of the torus in $X_ P$ and the global sections of the powers of $L$. The combinatorics of this description is organized by the inner normals of $P$. For a face $Q$ of $P$, let $\sigma _ Q\subset N_{\mathbb R}$ be the cone generated by the inward normals $u_\rho $ of the facets $\rho $ containing $Q$; thus $\sigma _ P=\{ 0\} $. A face of a cone $\sigma \subset N_{\mathbb R}$ is a set $\{ x\in \sigma :\langle m,x\rangle =0\} $, where $m\in M_{\mathbb R}$ is nonnegative on $\sigma $.

Proposition 18.8.1. For every face $Q$ of $P$, a vector $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ lies in $\sigma _ Q$ if and only if the minimum of $\langle m,x\rangle $ over $m\in P$ is attained at every point of $Q$, and $Q$ is the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing it. For faces $Q_1,Q_2$, the intersection $Q$ of $P$ with the facets containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$ is the smallest face containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$, and $\sigma _{Q_1}\cap \sigma _{Q_2}=\sigma _ Q$. The cones $\sigma _ Q$ form a complete smooth fan, the inner normal fan of $P$: they cover $N_{\mathbb R}$, each is generated by part of a basis of $N$, every face of each $\sigma _ Q$ is one of the cones $\sigma _{Q'}$, and any two of them intersect in a face of each. If $n>0$, there is a constant $\Lambda $, depending only on $P$ and the fixed Euclidean norm, such that every $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ with $|x|=1$ can be written as

\[ x=\lambda \sum _ i c_ i u_{\rho _ i},\qquad 0<\lambda \leq \Lambda ,\quad c_ i>0,\quad \sum _ i c_ i=1, \]

where all $u_{\rho _ i}$ lie in a single cone of this fan.

Proof. Let $Q$ be a face, choose a vertex $v\in Q$, and use the notation of lemma 18.7.2(4). Since $u_1,\ldots ,u_ n$ is the basis dual to $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$, every $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ can be written as $x=\sum _ i\alpha _ iu_ i$ with $\alpha _ i=\langle e_ i,x\rangle $, and $\langle m-v,x\rangle =\sum _ it_ i\alpha _ i$ for $m=v+\sum _ it_ ie_ i\in P$. If $v$ minimizes $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ on $P$, then $\alpha _ i=\langle (v+e_ i)-v,x\rangle \geq 0$, because $v+e_ i\in P$. If moreover every point of $Q$ minimizes it, and the $i$th facet at $v$ does not contain $Q$, choose $m\in Q$ with $t_ i=\ell _ i(m)>0$; then $\sum _ jt_ j\alpha _ j=0$ with nonnegative terms, so $\alpha _ i=0$. Hence $x$ is a nonnegative combination of the normals of the facets containing $Q$, all of which pass through $v$, that is, $x\in \sigma _ Q$. Conversely, if $x=\sum _\rho c_\rho u_\rho $ with $c_\rho \geq 0$ and all $\rho $ containing $Q$, then $\langle m,x\rangle =\sum _\rho c_\rho (\ell _\rho (m)-a_\rho )$, and each $\ell _\rho $ attains its minimum $0$ on $P$ at every point of $Q$; so does $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $. This proves the characterization, and it shows that $\sigma _ Q$ is generated by part of the basis $u_1,\ldots ,u_ n$ of $N$.

Every face $Q$ is the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing it. Indeed, if $Q$ is the set where $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ is minimal, then $x\in \sigma _ Q$, and writing $x=\sum _\rho c_\rho u_\rho $ as above we see that the minimum is attained exactly where $\ell _\rho =0$ for all $\rho $ with $c_\rho >0$; this set contains the intersection of the facets containing $Q$, which in turn contains $Q$.

The faces of $\sigma _ Q$ are the cones generated by subsets of its generators. Indeed, if $m\in M_{\mathbb R}$ is nonnegative on $\sigma _ Q$ and $x$ is a nonnegative combination of the generators, then $\langle m,x\rangle $ is a sum of nonnegative terms, so the face $\{ x\in \sigma _ Q:\langle m,x\rangle =0\} $ is generated by the generators annihilated by $m$. Conversely, since the generators are part of a basis of $N_{\mathbb R}$, for every subset of them there is an $m$ that vanishes on this subset and equals $1$ on the remaining generators, and $m$ is nonnegative on $\sigma _ Q$. If $J$ is a set of facets containing $v$, the set $Q'=\{ m\in P:\ell _\rho (m)=0\text{ for }\rho \in J\} $ is a face, namely the set where $\sum _{\rho \in J}\langle \cdot ,u_\rho \rangle $ is minimal. It contains $v+se_ i$ for small $s>0$ whenever the $i$th facet at $v$ is not in $J$, so the facets containing $Q'$ are exactly those in $J$, and $\sigma _{Q'}=\operatorname {cone}\{ u_\rho :\rho \in J\} $. Thus every face of $\sigma _ Q$ is in the family.

Every $x$ attains its minimum on $P$ on some face $Q$, and then $x\in \sigma _ Q$ by the characterization; hence the cones cover $N_{\mathbb R}$. Let $Q_1,Q_2$ be faces, and let $Q$ be the intersection of $P$ with all facets containing both. It is a face (if no facet contains both, $Q=P$; otherwise it is the set where the sum of the corresponding functionals is minimal), and every face containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$ contains $Q$, since it is the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing it. Since $Q\supset Q_1\cup Q_2$, it is the smallest face containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$. By the characterization, $x\in \sigma _{Q_1}\cap \sigma _{Q_2}$ if and only if the face where $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ is minimal contains $Q_1\cup Q_2$, that is, if and only if it contains $Q$; so $\sigma _{Q_1}\cap \sigma _{Q_2}=\sigma _ Q$. The generators of $\sigma _ Q$, the normals of the facets containing $Q_1\cup Q_2$, form a subset of the generators of $\sigma _{Q_ k}$, so $\sigma _ Q$ is a face of $\sigma _{Q_1}$ and of $\sigma _{Q_2}$.

For the last assertion, let $|x|=1$ and choose a vertex $v$ minimizing $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ on $P$. The coefficients $\alpha _ i=\langle e_ i,x\rangle $ above are nonnegative and satisfy $\alpha _ i\leq |e_ i|$. Their sum $\lambda $ is positive, because $x\neq 0$, and at most $\sum _ i|e_ i|$. Let $\Lambda $ be the maximum of these finite sums over all vertices. Dividing by $\lambda $ and omitting the zero coefficients gives the required expression, with all $u_{\rho _ i}$ in the cone $\sigma _{\{ v\} }$. In dimension zero the fan consists of the zero cone, and there is no unit vector. $\square$

Proposition 18.8.2. The $T$-orbits in $X_ P$ are indexed by the faces $Q$ of $P$, and the orbit indexed by $Q$ has dimension $\dim Q$. The closure of the orbit indexed by $Q$ is the union of the orbits indexed by the faces of $P$ contained in $Q$. The complement of $\iota (T)$ is the union of the $T$-invariant prime divisors $D_\rho $, the closures of the orbits indexed by the facets $\rho $. For every $m\in M$ and every facet $\rho $, the character $\chi ^ m$ extends to a meromorphic function on $X_ P$, with order

\[ \operatorname {ord}_{D_\rho }\chi ^ m=\langle m,u_\rho \rangle \]

along $D_\rho $. Finally, for every vertex $v$ of $P$ and every $m\in M$, the character $\chi ^ m$ is holomorphic on the chart $U_ v$ if and only if $\langle m,x\rangle \geq 0$ for all $x$ in the cone $\sigma _{\{ v\} }$ of proposition 18.8.1.

Proof. Fix a vertex $v$ and use the chart $U_ v\cong \mathbb C^ n$ of theorem 18.7.3. For $\tau \in T$ we have $w_ i(\tau \cdot z)=\chi ^{e_ i}(\tau )w_ i(z)$, and $\tau \mapsto (\chi ^{e_ i}(\tau ))_ i$ maps $T$ onto $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$; so $T$ acts on $U_ v$ by independent scalings of the coordinates. The orbits in $U_ v$ are therefore the sets

\[ O_{v,I}=\{ w:w_ i=0\text{ for }i\in I,\ w_ j\neq 0\text{ for }j\notin I\} ,\qquad I\subset \{ 1,\ldots ,n\} , \]

and $O_{v,I}$ has dimension $n-|I|$. Let $Q_{v,I}=\{ m\in P:\ell _ i(m)=0\text{ for }i\in I\} $. It is a face, namely the set where $\sum _{i\in I}\langle \cdot ,u_ i\rangle $ is minimal on $P$. It contains $v$ and the points $v+se_ j$ for $j\notin I$ and small $s>0$, and it lies in $v+\operatorname {span}\{ e_ j:j\notin I\} $, because $m-v=\sum _ i\ell _ i(m)e_ i$; so $\dim Q_{v,I}=n-|I|$. Since $Z_ m/Z_ v=\prod _ iw_ i^{\ell _ i(m)}$, a homogeneous coordinate $Z_ m$, $m\in A$, is nonzero on $O_{v,I}$ if and only if $\ell _ i(m)=0$ for all $i\in I$, that is, if and only if $m\in Q_{v,I}$.

The charts $U_ v$ are $T$-invariant, because the action of $T$ multiplies $Z_ v$ by $\chi ^ v(\tau )\neq 0$. Hence every orbit $O$ in $X_ P$ meets, and therefore lies in, some chart $U_ v$, so it is one of the sets $O_{v,I}$. The set of $m\in A$ with $Z_ m\neq 0$ on $O$ does not depend on the chart, and it equals $Q_{v,I}\cap A$; since a face is the convex hull of its vertices, which are lattice points, $Q_{v,I}$ is the convex hull of this set. Thus each orbit $O$ determines a face $Q(O)$, with $\dim Q(O)=\dim O$. Distinct orbits determine distinct faces: if $Q(O)=Q(O')=Q$ and $v$ is a vertex of $Q$, then $Z_ v\neq 0$ on both orbits, so both lie in $U_ v$, where an orbit is determined by the set of indices $i$ with $v+e_ i\notin Q$. Every face $Q$ arises: choose a vertex $v\in Q$ and let $I$ be the set of indices of the facets at $v$ that contain $Q$; then $Q_{v,I}=Q$, because $Q$ is the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing it (proposition 18.8.1), and these facets all pass through $v$. We write $O_ Q$ for the orbit of the face $Q$; in particular $O_ P=\iota (T)$.

The closure $\overline{O_ Q}$ is $T$-invariant, hence a union of orbits, and it lies in the closed set $\{ Z_ m=0\text{ for }m\in A\setminus Q\} $. If $O_{Q'}\subset \overline{O_ Q}$, then $Q'\cap A\subset Q$, and so $Q'\subset Q$. Conversely, let $Q'\subset Q$ be faces, let $v$ be a vertex of $Q'$, and let $I\subset I'$ be the index sets of $Q$ and $Q'$ at $v$. A point $w\in O_{Q'}=O_{v,I'}$ is the limit as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ of the points obtained from $w$ by replacing the coordinates $w_ i=0$, $i\in I'\setminus I$, by $\varepsilon $, and these points lie in $O_{v,I}=O_ Q$. Hence $O_{Q'}\subset \overline{O_ Q}$.

Let $\rho $ be a facet and $D_\rho =\overline{O_\rho }$. If $v\in \rho $ and $\rho $ is the $i$th facet at $v$, the orbits in $U_ v$ indexed by faces contained in $\rho $ are the sets $O_{v,I}$ with $i\in I$, so $D_\rho \cap U_ v=\{ w_ i=0\} $; if $v\notin \rho $, then $D_\rho \cap U_ v=\varnothing $, because $Z_ v=0$ on $D_\rho $. Hence $D_\rho $ is a $T$-invariant closed submanifold of codimension one. Every proper face is contained in a facet, being the intersection of the facets containing it; hence $X_ P\setminus \iota (T)$, the union of the orbits of the proper faces, is the union of the divisors $D_\rho $.

Each $D_\rho $ is irreducible, that is, not the union of two analytic subsets different from $D_\rho $. First, $D_\rho $ is connected, being the closure of the orbit $O_\rho $: in a chart $U_ v$ in which $\rho $ is the $i$th facet at $v$, the coordinates $w_ j$, $j\neq i$, identify $O_\rho =O_{v,\{ i\} }$ with the connected set $(\mathbb C^*)^{n-1}$. Next, let $Z\subsetneq D_\rho $ be an analytic subset. The set of interior points of $Z$ in $D_\rho $ is open, and it is closed in $D_\rho $. Indeed, if $p$ lies in its closure, then $p\in Z$, and on a connected coordinate neighborhood of $p$ in $D_\rho $ the local holomorphic functions defining $Z$ vanish on a nonempty open set, hence identically by the identity theorem, so that $p$ is an interior point. As $D_\rho $ is connected and $Z\neq D_\rho $, the set $Z$ has empty interior in $D_\rho $. If $D_\rho =Z_1\cup Z_2$ with analytic subsets $Z_1,Z_2\neq D_\rho $, then the nonempty open set $D_\rho \setminus Z_1$ would be contained in $Z_2$, which has empty interior.

Let $m\in M$. Since $m=\sum _ j\langle m,u_ j\rangle e_ j$, the character $\chi ^ m$ is the Laurent monomial $\prod _ jw_ j^{\langle m,u_ j\rangle }$ in the coordinates of $U_ v$. Hence it extends to a meromorphic function on each $U_ v$, and these extensions agree on the dense set $\iota (T)$, hence on overlaps. Near each point of the dense orbit $O_\rho \cap U_ v$ of $D_\rho \cap U_ v=\{ w_ i=0\} $, it is $w_ i^{\langle m,u_ i\rangle }$ times a nowhere vanishing holomorphic function; so its order along $D_\rho $, the difference of the orders of numerator and denominator in the sense of definition 12.6.3, is $\langle m,u_ i\rangle =\langle m,u_\rho \rangle $. In particular, $\chi ^ m$ is holomorphic on $U_ v\cong \mathbb C^ n$ if and only if all the exponents $\langle m,u_ j\rangle $ are nonnegative, that is, if and only if $\langle m,x\rangle \geq 0$ for every $x$ in $\sigma _{\{ v\} }=\operatorname {cone}(u_1,\ldots ,u_ n)$. $\square$

The vertex charts are glued along open sets determined by the fan, as follows.

Proposition 18.8.3. Let $v$ and $v'$ be vertices of $P$, with primitive edge bases $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$ and $e_1',\ldots ,e_ n'$ and vertex charts $w$ and $w'$, and let $u_1,\ldots ,u_ n$ be the inward normals of the facets through $v$, as in lemma 18.7.2(4).

  1. A lattice point $m\in M$ satisfies $\langle m,x\rangle \geq 0$ for all $x\in \sigma _{\{ v\} }$ if and only if $m=\sum _ jc_ je_ j$ with integers $c_ j\geq 0$. Consequently, the characters that are holomorphic on $U_ v$ are exactly the monomials $w^ c=\prod _ jw_ j^{c_ j}$, $c\in \mathbb Z_{\geq 0}^ n$.

  2. The character $\chi ^{v'-v}$ is holomorphic on $U_ v$, and $U_ v\cap U_{v'}$ is the set of points of $U_ v$ at which it does not vanish. On this set

    \[ w'_ i=\prod _ jw_ j^{\langle e'_ i,u_ j\rangle }\qquad (1\leq i\leq n), \]

    where the factors with negative exponents do not vanish.

  3. Let $Q$ be the intersection of $P$ with the facets containing both $v$ and $v'$, the smallest face of $P$ containing $v$ and $v'$. Then

    \[ \sigma _{\{ v\} }\cap \sigma _{\{ v'\} }=\sigma _ Q=\{ x\in \sigma _{\{ v\} }:\langle v'-v,x\rangle =0\} . \]

Proof. (1) Since $u_1,\ldots ,u_ n$ is the basis of $N$ dual to $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$, every $m\in M$ is $m=\sum _ j\langle m,u_ j\rangle e_ j$ with integer coefficients, and $\sigma _{\{ v\} }=\operatorname {cone}(u_1,\ldots ,u_ n)$. Hence $\langle m,\cdot \rangle \geq 0$ on $\sigma _{\{ v\} }$ if and only if all coefficients $\langle m,u_ j\rangle $ are nonnegative. The second assertion follows from the last assertion of proposition 18.8.2, since $\chi ^ m=\prod _ jw_ j^{\langle m,u_ j\rangle }$ on $U_ v$.

(2) By theorem 18.7.3, $Z_{v'}/Z_ v=\prod _ jw_ j^{b_ j(v')}$ on $U_ v$, with $b_ j(v')=\ell _ j(v')=\langle v'-v,u_ j\rangle \geq 0$; this holomorphic function is the extension of $\chi ^{v'-v}$ to $U_ v$. Since $U_ v\cap U_{v'}=U_ v\cap \{ Z_{v'}\neq 0\} $, the first assertion follows. On $U_ v\cap U_{v'}$ we have $w'_ i=Z_{v'+e_ i'}/Z_{v'}=w^{b(v'+e_ i')}/w^{b(v')}$, a quotient of monomials whose denominator does not vanish there, and $b_ j(v'+e'_ i)-b_ j(v')=\langle e'_ i,u_ j\rangle $. A negative exponent $\langle e'_ i,u_ j\rangle $ forces $b_ j(v')>0$, so the corresponding factor $w_ j$ divides the denominator and does not vanish on $U_ v\cap U_{v'}$.

(3) By proposition 18.8.1, applied to the faces $\{ v\} $ and $\{ v'\} $, the set $Q$ is the smallest face containing $v$ and $v'$, and $\sigma _{\{ v\} }\cap \sigma _{\{ v'\} }=\sigma _ Q$. The facets containing $Q$ are the facets $\rho _ j$ through $v$ with $\ell _ j(v')=0$. If $x=\sum _ j\alpha _ ju_ j\in \sigma _{\{ v\} }$ with $\alpha _ j\geq 0$, then $\langle v'-v,x\rangle =\sum _ j\alpha _ j\ell _ j(v')$ is a sum of nonnegative terms, so it vanishes if and only if $\alpha _ j=0$ whenever $\ell _ j(v')>0$, that is, if and only if $x\in \operatorname {cone}\{ u_ j:\ell _ j(v')=0\} =\sigma _ Q$. $\square$

Thus $X_ P$ is obtained by gluing the affine spaces $U_ v\cong \mathbb C^ n$ along the open subsets $\{ \chi ^{v'-v}\neq 0\} $ by the monomial coordinate changes of proposition 18.8.3(2). The holomorphic characters on $U_ v$ are the $\chi ^ m$ with $m$ in the semigroup $\{ m\in M:\langle m,\cdot \rangle \geq 0\text{ on }\sigma _{\{ v\} }\} $, and the open subset $\{ \chi ^{v'-v}\neq 0\} $ corresponds to the common face $\sigma _{\{ v\} }\cap \sigma _{\{ v'\} }=\sigma _{\{ v\} }\cap (v'-v)^\perp $. These are the gluing data of the toric variety of the inner normal fan of $P$ in [Section 3.1, CLS11], in which $U_ v$ is the affine toric variety of the cone $\sigma _{\{ v\} }$; in this sense $X_ P$ is the toric variety of the inner normal fan of $P$.

Lemma 18.8.4. Let $n\geq 1$, and let $f$ be a holomorphic function on $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$. For $m\in \mathbb Z^ n$ and $r\in (0,\infty )^ n$ put

\[ c_ m(r)=\frac1{(2\pi )^ n}\int _{[0,2\pi ]^ n}f(r_1\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta _1},\ldots ,r_ n\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta _ n})\, r^{-m}\mathrm e^{-\mathrm i\langle m,\theta \rangle }\, \mathrm d\theta , \]

where $r^{-m}=\prod _ jr_ j^{-m_ j}$ and $\langle m,\theta \rangle =\sum _ jm_ j\theta _ j$. Then $c_ m=c_ m(r)$ does not depend on $r$, and the Laurent series $\sum _{m\in \mathbb Z^ n}c_ mz^ m$, where $z^ m=\prod _ jz_ j^{m_ j}$, converges normally on every compact subset of $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$, with sum $f$. If a series $\sum _ mc'_ mz^ m$ converges normally to $f$ on one torus $\{ z:|z_ j|=r_ j\text{ for all }j\} $, then $c'_ m=c_ m$ for every $m$. If $f$ extends holomorphically to $\mathbb C^ n$, then $c_ m=0$ unless $m_ j\geq 0$ for every $j$.

Proof. Fix $j$ and the variables other than $z_ j$. The resulting function $g(\zeta )$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb C^*$, and for $k\in \mathbb Z$ the integral $\frac1{2\pi }\int _0^{2\pi }g(\varrho \mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta })\varrho ^{-k}\mathrm e^{-\mathrm ik\theta }\, \mathrm d\theta =\frac1{2\pi \mathrm i}\int _{|\zeta |=\varrho }g(\zeta )\zeta ^{-k-1}\, \mathrm d\zeta $ does not depend on $\varrho >0$, by Cauchy's theorem on the annulus between two such circles. Integrating over the other angles, by Fubini's theorem, we see that $c_ m(r)$ does not depend on $r_ j$; hence it does not depend on $r$. The definition gives the Cauchy estimates $|c_ m|\leq C(r)r^{-m}$, where $C(r)$ is the maximum of $|f|$ on the torus $\{ |z_ j|=r_ j\} $. Let $K\subset (\mathbb C^*)^ n$ be compact, and choose $0<a\leq b$ with $a\leq |z_ j|\leq b$ on $K$ for all $j$. Given $m$, take $r_ j=2b$ if $m_ j\geq 0$ and $r_ j=a/2$ if $m_ j<0$. Then, for $z\in K$,

\[ |c_ mz^ m|\leq C_ K\prod _ j\Bigl (\frac{|z_ j|}{r_ j}\Bigr )^{m_ j}\leq C_ K2^{-(|m_1|+\cdots +|m_ n|)}, \]

where $C_ K$ is the largest of the numbers $C(r)$ for the $2^ n$ tori with $r_ j\in \{ a/2,2b\} $. Since $\sum _{m\in \mathbb Z^ n}2^{-(|m_1|+\cdots +|m_ n|)}=3^ n$, the series converges normally on $K$.

We show by induction on $n$ that its sum is $f$. For $n=1$ this is the Laurent theorem of one-variable theory, with the coefficients given by the integral above. Let $n\geq 2$, and write $z=(z',z_ n)$ with $z'\in (\mathbb C^*)^{n-1}$. For fixed $z'$, the one-variable theorem gives $f(z',z_ n)=\sum _ ka_ k(z')z_ n^ k$ with $a_ k(z')=\frac1{2\pi }\int _0^{2\pi }f(z',\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta })\mathrm e^{-\mathrm ik\theta }\, \mathrm d\theta $. Differentiation under the integral sign shows that $a_ k$ is smooth and satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann equations, so it is holomorphic on $(\mathbb C^*)^{n-1}$, and by Fubini's theorem its Laurent coefficients are the numbers $c_{(m',k)}$, $m'\in \mathbb Z^{n-1}$. By the induction hypothesis, $a_ k(z')=\sum _{m'}c_{(m',k)}z'^{m'}$, so $f(z)=\sum _ k\sum _{m'}c_{(m',k)}z'^{m'}z_ n^ k$. This double series converges absolutely, by the previous paragraph, so it may be summed as $\sum _ mc_ mz^ m$.

For the uniqueness, integrate the normally convergent series $\sum _{m''}c'_{m''}r^{m''}\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\langle m'',\theta \rangle }=f(r_1\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta _1},\ldots )$ term by term against $(2\pi )^{-n}r^{-m}\mathrm e^{-\mathrm i\langle m,\theta \rangle }\, \mathrm d\theta $; since $\int _0^{2\pi }\mathrm e^{\mathrm il\theta }\, \mathrm d\theta =0$ for every integer $l\neq 0$, only the term $m''=m$ survives, and we obtain $c'_ m=c_ m(r)=c_ m$. Finally, if $f$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb C^ n$, its Taylor series at the origin converges normally on every polydisc by (7.1), in particular on every torus, so by uniqueness the coefficients $c_ m$ are the Taylor coefficients for $m\in \mathbb Z_{\geq 0}^ n$ and vanish for all other $m$. $\square$

Theorem 18.8.5. There is a meromorphic section $s_0$ of $L$ with divisor

\[ D_ P=\sum _\rho a_\rho D_\rho , \]

so that $L$ is isomorphic to the line bundle $\mathcal O_{X_ P}(D_ P)$ of this divisor. For every integer $k\geq 0$,

\[ H^0(X_ P,L^ k)=\bigoplus _{m\in kP\cap M}\mathbb C\, \chi ^ m s_0^ k, \]

where $0P=\{ 0\} $. Each summand is a line on which the natural action of $T$ is given by a character, and the dimensions $h^0(X_ P,L^ k)=\dim H^0(X_ P,L^ k)$ satisfy

\[ \lim _{k\to \infty }\frac{n!}{k^ n}h^0(X_ P,L^ k)=n!\operatorname {vol}(P). \]

Proof. For vertices $v$ and $v'$, the coordinate sections satisfy $s_ v/s_{v'}=Z_ v/Z_{v'}=\chi ^{v-v'}$ on $\iota (T)$. Hence the section $s_0=\chi ^{-v}s_ v$ of $L$ over $\iota (T)$ does not depend on the vertex $v$. In the chart $U_ v$, its coefficient $\chi ^{-v}$ with respect to the frame $s_ v$ is a Laurent monomial in $w$, so $s_0$ extends to a meromorphic section on each $U_ v$; the extensions agree on overlaps, because they agree on the dense set $\iota (T)$. By proposition 18.8.2, if $v$ is a vertex of the facet $\rho $, then $\operatorname {ord}_{D_\rho }s_0=\operatorname {ord}_{D_\rho }\chi ^{-v}=-\langle v,u_\rho \rangle =a_\rho $, since $\ell _\rho (v)=0$. As $s_0$ has no zeros or poles on $\iota (T)$, its divisor is $D_ P$. Similarly, the meromorphic function $f_ v=\chi ^{-v}$ on $U_ v$ has divisor $\sum _{\rho \ni v}a_\rho (D_\rho \cap U_ v)$, which is the restriction of $D_ P$ to $U_ v$, because $D_\rho \cap U_ v=\varnothing $ for $v\notin \rho $. The line bundle $\mathcal O_{X_ P}(D_ P)$ of the divisor therefore has over $U_ v$ the local frame $1/f_ v$, and its transition functions, in the sense of definition 13.1.8, are $f_{v'}/f_ v=\chi ^{v-v'}$. These are the transition functions of $L$ in the frames $s_ v$, since $s_ v=\chi ^{v-v'}s_{v'}$. Hence the map sending $s_ v$ to $1/f_ v$ for every vertex $v$ is an isomorphism $L\cong \mathcal O_{X_ P}(D_ P)$.

Let $s$ be a holomorphic section of $L^ k$ and write $s=fs_0^ k$ on $\iota (T)$, with $f$ holomorphic on $T\cong (\mathbb C^*)^ n$. By lemma 18.8.4, applied in the coordinates of a basis of $M$, we have $f=\sum _{m\in M}c_ m\chi ^ m$, with normal convergence on compact subsets of $T$. The coefficients $c_ m$ do not depend on the basis: a change of basis replaces each character by the corresponding monomial in the new coordinates and preserves normal convergence on compact sets, so the uniqueness assertion of the lemma applies. Now fix a vertex $v$. The coefficient $g_ v$ of $s$ with respect to the frame $s_ v^ k$ is a holomorphic function on $U_ v\cong \mathbb C^ n$, and on $\iota (T)$

\[ g_ v=f\chi ^{-kv}=\sum _ mc_ m\prod _ iw_ i^{\langle m-kv,u_ i\rangle }. \]

Since $u_1,\ldots ,u_ n$ is the basis of $N$ dual to the basis $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$ of $M$, the map $m\mapsto (\langle m-kv,u_ i\rangle )_ i$ is a bijection of $M$ onto $\mathbb Z^ n$, so this is the Laurent expansion of $g_ v$ in the coordinates $w$. By the last assertion of lemma 18.8.4, $c_ m=0$ unless $\langle m-kv,u_ i\rangle \geq 0$ for every facet $i$ at $v$. Since $\langle v,u_\rho \rangle =-a_\rho $ for $v\in \rho $, this condition reads $\langle m,u_\rho \rangle +ka_\rho \geq 0$. Every facet contains a vertex, so for $k>0$ the conditions at all vertices say exactly that $m/k\in P$, that is, $m\in kP$. For $k=0$ they say that $\langle m,u_\rho \rangle \geq 0$ for every $\rho $; then $p+sm\in P$ for $p\in P$ and all $s\geq 0$, so $m=0$ by boundedness. Thus only the finitely many $m\in kP\cap M$ occur. Hence $s=\sum _{m\in kP\cap M}c_ m\chi ^ ms_0^ k$ on the dense set $\iota (T)$, and therefore on $X_ P$, once we know that the sections on the right are holomorphic. Indeed, for $m\in kP\cap M$ all exponents $\langle m-kv,u_ i\rangle $ are nonnegative, so $\chi ^ ms_0^ k$ extends holomorphically to every chart. Finally, the uniqueness of Laurent coefficients shows that the sections $\chi ^ ms_0^ k$ are linearly independent.

The action of $T$ on $\mathbb C^ A$ by $\tau \cdot (Z_ m)=(\chi ^ m(\tau )Z_ m)$ induces actions on the tautological line bundle (example 14.5.5), whose fiber over $[Z]$ is the line $\mathbb CZ$, on its dual $\mathcal O(1)$, on $L^ k$, and on $H^0(X_ P,L^ k)$ by $(\tau \cdot s)(x)=\tau \cdot s(\tau ^{-1}\cdot x)$. It multiplies the linear form $Z_ v$, and hence $s_ v$, by $\chi ^{-v}(\tau )$, and it maps the function $\chi ^ v$ to $\chi ^ v(\tau ^{-1}\, \cdot \, )=\chi ^{-v}(\tau )\chi ^ v$; therefore it fixes $s_0$ and multiplies $\chi ^ ms_0^ k$ by $\chi ^{-m}(\tau )$. So each summand is a line on which $T$ acts through a character. Finally, $h^0(X_ P,L^ k)=\# (kP\cap M)$, and the limit follows from theorem 18.6.3. In dimension zero there is one section, and the volume is one. $\square$

18.9 The Guillemin reference metric

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

The Fubini–Study metric of theorem 18.7.3 already gives a smooth metric on $L$ with positive definite curvature form. In integrability calculations, however, it is useful to have a reference metric whose potential on $N_{\mathbb R}$ is the Legendre transform of an explicit function on $P$ whose gradient has logarithmic singularities along the facets. Guillemin's metric, defined through the entropy function $G_0$ below, has this property; the content of the next theorem is that it is nevertheless smooth on all of $X_ P$, with positive definite curvature form.

Theorem 18.9.1. With the convention $0\log 0=0$, define the closed proper convex function

\[ G_0(m)=\sum _\rho \ell _\rho (m)\log \ell _\rho (m)\quad (m\in P), \qquad G_0=+\infty \quad \text{outside }P, \]

and put $F_0=G_0^*$. Then $F_0$ is smooth on $N_{\mathbb R}$, its Hessian is positive definite, and $F_0\sim h_ P$. The gradient $\nabla G_0$ is a diffeomorphism of $\operatorname{Int}P$ onto $N_{\mathbb R}$. For each vertex $v$, the function

\[ H_ v=F_0\circ \operatorname {Trop}-\log |\chi ^ v|^2 \]

on $\iota (T)\cong T$ extends smoothly to $U_ v$, and the squared norms $|s_ v|^2=\mathrm e^{-H_ v}$ of the coordinate sections define a smooth quadratic metric on $L$ with positive definite curvature form, invariant under $T_ c$. Its curvature form is the unique smooth extension of $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(F_0\circ \operatorname {Trop})$ from $T$ to $X_ P$, and it represents $c_1(L)$.

Proof. The main step is to invert $\nabla G_0$ near the boundary of $P$ in the coordinates of a vertex chart (Step 2); the logarithmic singularities in $H_ v$ then cancel.

Step 1: the potentials on $N_{\mathbb R}$. The function $\lambda \mapsto \lambda \log \lambda $ is convex and continuous on $[0,+\infty )$, and each $\ell _\rho $ is affine and nonnegative on $P$. Hence $G_0$ is convex, continuous and bounded on $P$, and closed and proper on $M_{\mathbb R}$. On $\operatorname{Int}P$,

\[ \nabla G_0=\sum _\rho (\log \ell _\rho +1)u_\rho , \qquad D^2G_0=\sum _\rho \frac{u_\rho \otimes u_\rho }{\ell _\rho }. \]

The normals $u_\rho $ span $N_{\mathbb R}$, since a nonzero $y\in M_{\mathbb R}$ annihilated by all of them would give $P+\mathbb Ry\subset P$ by the facet presentation, which is impossible because $P$ is bounded; so $D^2G_0(y,y)=\sum _\rho \langle y,u_\rho \rangle ^2/\ell _\rho >0$ for $y\neq 0$, and the Hessian is positive definite.

Let $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$. The continuous function $G_0-\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ attains its minimum on the compact set $P$, and not at a boundary point $m_0$. Indeed, let $p\in \operatorname{Int}P$ and move from $m_0$ towards $p$: for each facet with $\ell _\rho (m_0)=0$ we have $\ell _\rho (m_0+s(p-m_0))=s\ell _\rho (p)$ with $\ell _\rho (p)>0$, and the term $s\ell _\rho (p)\log (s\ell _\rho (p))$ has right derivative $-\infty $ at $s=0$, while all other terms have finite derivatives; so the function decreases initially. The minimum is therefore attained in $\operatorname{Int}P$, at a solution of $\nabla G_0(m)=x$. Conversely, every solution of $\nabla G_0(m)=x$ in $\operatorname{Int}P$ is a minimum point, by convexity, and the minimum point is unique because $G_0$ is strictly convex on $\operatorname{Int}P$. Hence $\nabla G_0$ is a smooth bijection of $\operatorname{Int}P$ onto $N_{\mathbb R}$ with invertible derivative, and so a diffeomorphism by the inverse function theorem. Writing $m(x)=(\nabla G_0)^{-1}(x)$, we have $F_0(x)=\langle m(x),x\rangle -G_0(m(x))$, so $F_0$ is smooth, and differentiating gives $\nabla F_0(x)=m(x)$ and $D^2F_0(x)=(D^2G_0(m(x)))^{-1}$, which is positive definite. Since $G_0$ is bounded on $P$, say $|G_0|\leq C$ there, we have $\chi _ P-C\leq G_0\leq \chi _ P+C$, and conjugation gives $h_ P-C\leq F_0\leq h_ P+C$.

Step 2: local inversion at a boundary point. Fix a vertex $v$, number the facets at $v$ as in lemma 18.7.2(4), and use on $M_{\mathbb R}$ the coordinates $t_ k=\ell _ k(m)$, so that $m=v+\sum _ kt_ ke_ k$, and on $N_{\mathbb R}$ the dual coordinates $x_ k=\langle e_ k,x\rangle $, so that $x=\sum _ kx_ ku_ k$ and $\langle m-v,x\rangle =\sum _ kt_ kx_ k$. If $z\in \iota (T)$ and $x=\operatorname {Trop}(z)$, then $x_ k=\log |\chi ^{e_ k}(z)|^2=\log |w_ k(z)|^2$. Let $w^0\in U_ v$, let $I=\{ i:w^0_ i=0\} $ and $J$ its complement, and let $Q=\{ m\in P:t_ i=0\text{ for }i\in I\} $ be the corresponding face (proposition 18.8.2). Put $x^0_ j=\log |w^0_ j|^2$ for $j\in J$.

The facet functions other than $t_ i$, $i\in I$, are positive on the relative interior of $Q$. Indeed, if such a function vanished at a point of the relative interior of $Q$, it would vanish on $Q$ by the observation preceding lemma 18.7.2, so its facet would contain $Q$ and $v$. It would then be one of the facets at $v$ containing $Q$, and these are the facets $i\in I$, because $v+se_ j\in Q$ for $j\in J$ and small $s>0$. Now consider the function $G_0-\sum _{j\in J}t_ jx^0_ j$ on the compact set $Q$, on which the terms of the facets $i\in I$ vanish identically. As in Step 1, applied within the affine span of $Q$, its minimum is not attained on the relative boundary of $Q$. Indeed, if all facet functions other than $t_ i$, $i\in I$, are positive at a point of $Q$, then they are positive on a neighborhood of this point in the affine span $\{ t_ I=0\} $ of $Q$, which therefore lies in $Q$. Hence at a relative boundary point $m_0$ some facet function that is positive on the relative interior vanishes, and moving from $m_0$ towards a relative interior point decreases the function initially, as in Step 1. The minimum point $q$ is unique, because on the relative interior of $Q$ the Hessian of $G_0$ in the directions $e_ j$, $j\in J$, of $Q$ dominates the positive definite matrix $\operatorname {diag}(1/t_ j)_{j\in J}$ coming from the terms $t_ j\log t_ j$.

Near $q$ all facet functions except $t_ i$, $i\in I$, are positive, so on a neighborhood $W$ of $q$ in $M_{\mathbb R}$

\[ G_0(t)=\sum _{i\in I}t_ i\log t_ i+g(t)\qquad (t\in P\cap W), \]

where $g$, the sum of the remaining terms, is smooth on $W$. Write $g_ k=\partial g/\partial t_ k$ and $g_{jk}=\partial ^2g/\partial t_ j\partial t_ k$. The point $q$ is characterized by $g_ j(q)=x_ j^0$ for $j\in J$, since on $Q$ we have $G_0=g$. At points of $\operatorname{Int}P\cap W$ the equation $\nabla G_0(t)=x$ reads

\[ x_ i=\log t_ i+1+g_ i(t)\quad (i\in I),\qquad x_ j=g_ j(t)\quad (j\in J). \]

Exponentiating the first equations and putting $y_ i=\mathrm e^{x_ i}$, which equals $|w_ i|^2$ on $\iota (T)$, we obtain the system $\Psi (t)=(y_ I,x_ J)$ with

\[ \Psi (t)=\Bigl (\bigl (t_ i\exp (1+g_ i(t))\bigr )_{i\in I},\ \bigl (g_ j(t)\bigr )_{j\in J}\Bigr ), \]

a smooth map on all of $W$. At $t=q$, where $t_ I=0$, the Jacobian matrix of $\Psi $ is block triangular: its $I\times I$ block is the diagonal matrix with entries $\exp (1+g_ i(q))>0$, its $I\times J$ block vanishes, and its $J\times J$ block $(g_{jk}(q))$ is positive definite, because $g$ contains the terms $t_ j\log t_ j$, $j\in J$, and its other terms are convex. Since $\Psi (q)=(0,x_ J^0)$, the inverse function theorem gives a smooth local inverse $t=\Psi ^{-1}(y_ I,x_ J)$ near $(0,x_ J^0)$, with values near $q$. For $y_ I\geq 0$ the solution satisfies

\[ t_ i=y_ i\exp (-1-g_ i(t))\geq 0\quad (i\in I), \]

and its other facet functions are positive, so it lies in $P$. If $y_ I>0$, it lies in $\operatorname{Int}P$ and solves $\nabla G_0(t)=x$ with $x_ i=\log y_ i$; since $\nabla G_0$ is injective on $\operatorname{Int}P$, $t=\nabla F_0(x)$.

Step 3: smooth extension. Let $z\in \iota (T)$ be close to $w^0$, put $x=\operatorname {Trop}(z)$, and let $t$ be the coordinates of $\nabla F_0(x)$. Since $F_0(x)=\langle \nabla F_0(x),x\rangle -G_0(\nabla F_0(x))$ and $\log |\chi ^ v(z)|^2=\langle v,x\rangle $, we obtain, using the equations for $i\in I$,

\[ H_ v=\sum _ kt_ kx_ k-\sum _{i\in I}t_ i\log t_ i-g(t) =\sum _{i\in I}t_ i(1+g_ i(t))+\sum _{j\in J}t_ jx_ j-g(t). \]

The singular logarithms have canceled. By Step 2, $t=\Psi ^{-1}(|w_ I|^2,\log |w_ J|^2)$, so the right-hand side is a smooth function $h(y_ I,x_ J)$ of $(y_ I,x_ J)=(|w_ I|^2,\log |w_ J|^2)$, which in turn is a smooth function of $w$ near $w^0$, because $w^0_ j\neq 0$ for $j\in J$. Hence $H_ v$ extends smoothly to a neighborhood of $w^0$, and, $w^0\in U_ v$ being arbitrary, to $U_ v$.

Step 4: positivity. On $\iota (T)$ we have $\partial F_0/\partial x_ k=\langle \nabla F_0(x),u_ k\rangle =\langle v,u_ k\rangle +t_ k$, hence $\partial H_ v/\partial x_ k=t_ k$. In the variables $(y_ I,x_ J)$ this says

\[ \frac{\partial h}{\partial x_ j}=t_ j\quad (j\in J),\qquad \frac{\partial h}{\partial y_ i}=\frac{t_ i}{y_ i}=\exp (-1-g_ i(t))\quad (i\in I), \]

and both identities persist at $y_ I=0$, because both sides are continuous functions of $(y_ I,x_ J)$. We now compute the complex Hessian of $H_ v$ at $w^0$, where $w_ I=0$. Since $\partial y_ i/\partial \bar w_ k=\delta _{ik}w_ i$ for $i\in I$ and $\partial x_ j/\partial \bar w_ k=\delta _{jk}/\bar w_ k$ for $j\in J$, the chain rule gives at $w^0$

\[ \frac{\partial ^2H_ v}{\partial w_ i\partial \bar w_ k}=\delta _{ik}\frac{\partial h}{\partial y_ i}\quad (i\in I),\qquad \frac{\partial ^2H_ v}{\partial w_ j\partial \bar w_ k}=\frac1{w_ j\bar w_ k}\frac{\partial ^2h}{\partial x_ j\partial x_ k}\quad (j,k\in J); \]

all other terms contain a factor $w_ i$ or $\bar w_ i$ with $i\in I$. On $\{ y_ I=0\} $ we have $t_ I=0$ and $x_ J=g_ J(t)$, so $\partial ^2h/\partial x_ j\partial x_ k=\partial t_ j/\partial x_ k$ is the inverse of the positive definite matrix $(g_{jk})_{j,k\in J}$. Hence the complex Hessian of $H_ v$ at $w^0$, where $t=q$, is block diagonal, with the positive numbers $\exp (-1-g_ i(q))$ on the $I$ block and, on the $J$ block, a positive definite matrix congruent to $(\partial t_ j/\partial x_ k)$ by the invertible diagonal matrix $\operatorname {diag}(1/w_ j)$. It is therefore positive definite at $w^0$. Every point of $U_ v$ arises as such a $w^0$ (with $I=\varnothing $ on $\iota (T)$), so $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}H_ v$ is positive definite on $U_ v$.

Step 5: the metric. On $\iota (T)$, for vertices $v$ and $v'$, we have $H_ v-H_{v'}=\log |\chi ^{v'-v}|^2$ and $s_ v=\chi ^{v-v'}s_{v'}$. The function $\chi ^{v-v'}=s_ v/s_{v'}$ is holomorphic and nowhere zero on $U_ v\cap U_{v'}$, so both sides of the identity $\mathrm e^{-H_ v}=|\chi ^{v-v'}|^2\mathrm e^{-H_{v'}}$ are continuous on $U_ v\cap U_{v'}$; as they agree on the dense subset $\iota (T)$, the identity holds on $U_ v\cap U_{v'}$. Thus the squared norms $|s_ v|^2=\mathrm e^{-H_ v}$ satisfy the transition law of definition 13.1.8 and define a smooth quadratic metric on $L$, with curvature $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}H_ v$ on $U_ v$, which is positive definite by Step 4. For $\tau \in T_ c$ we have $|\chi ^ m(\tau )|=1$, so $\operatorname {Trop}(\tau z)=\operatorname {Trop}(z)$, the weights $H_ v$ are $T_ c$-invariant, and $T_ c$ multiplies the frames $s_ v$ by factors of modulus one; hence the metric is $T_ c$-invariant. On $\iota (T)$ the curvature is $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}H_ v=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(F_0\circ \operatorname {Trop})$, because $\log |\chi ^ v|^2$ is pluriharmonic; since $\iota (T)$ is dense, the curvature form is the unique smooth extension of this form and represents $c_1(L)$ (section 14.5). In dimension zero the facet sum and both potentials vanish, $L$ is trivial over a point, and its curvature is zero. $\square$
