8.1 Complex spaces and modifications

Definitions, results, and proofs on this subject.

A Complex spaces and modifications

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

Analytic sets enter pluripotential theory as the pole sets of potentials with analytic singularities, as the zero sets of the ideals that describe these poles, and as the exceptional divisors of modifications. This appendix collects the facts about analytic sets, reduced complex spaces and modifications that are used from chapter 12 on: for multiplier ideals and Lelong numbers along divisors in chapter 12, for analytic singularities and their log resolutions in chapter 13, for currents supported on analytic sets in chapter 14, and for the non-Kähler locus in chapter 16. It can be consulted when these tools first appear. Besides results of the earlier chapters, such as the Weierstrass preparation theorem (theorem 7.4.2), only two existence theorems are used without proof: the resolution of singularities and the principalization of coherent ideals, stated as theorem A.5.4, theorem A.5.5.

The local theory rests on the Weierstrass division theorem. It shows that the ring of germs of holomorphic functions is Noetherian and, through Oka's theorem, that the sheaf of holomorphic functions is coherent, so that statements about one stalk extend to a neighborhood. The local parametrization theorem (theorem A.2.3) presents an irreducible germ of an analytic set as a finite branched covering of a polydisc. From it we deduce the analytic Nullstellensatz, the basic properties of dimension, Cartan's theorem on the coherence of the ideal sheaf of an analytic set, and the decomposition of an analytic set into irreducible components. We also show that a closed real $(1,1)$-current with measure coefficients supported on a nowhere dense analytic set is a combination of currents of integration along hypersurfaces (theorem A.2.17). One phenomenon requires care: an irreducible analytic set can have several local branches at one point. The normalization (theorem A.3.6) separates these branches; its holomorphic functions are the weakly holomorphic functions, that is, the functions that are holomorphic on the regular locus and locally bounded near the singular locus. The cusp shows that a space with only one branch at each point need not be normal (example A.3.10).

For modifications, explicit charts describe blowups along submanifolds and the transformation of ideals under them (proposition A.5.2, proposition A.5.3); together with the two existence theorems they give log resolutions of coherent ideals. Remmert's theorem, proved together with the Remmert–Stein extension theorem, shows that images of analytic sets under proper holomorphic maps, in particular the images of exceptional divisors, are analytic (theorem A.6.2). We then prove a relative principalization theorem, which controls an ideal and its restriction to a submanifold simultaneously (theorem A.6.5), show that proper bimeromorphic maps onto spaces with one local branch at each point have connected fibers (theorem A.7.1), and prove that every prime divisor over a compact complex space appears on a model obtained by finitely many blowups with smooth centers (theorem A.7.6).

The last section defines plurisubharmonic functions on reduced complex spaces by local extension to an ambient space, proves that they are constant on compact connected spaces, and shows that they are locally bounded above near analytic subsets of codimension at least two. Holomorphic curves through singular points, constructed in section A.4, reduce these statements to the maximum principle in one variable. These results on reduced spaces are used in [XiaSampleBook]. Historical remarks and references for this appendix are collected in section B.21.

A.1 Division and finite analytic modules

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

Let $n\geq 0$. We write $\mathcal O_ n$ for the ring of germs at $0\in \mathbb C^ n$ of holomorphic functions; two functions defined near $0$ have the same germ if they agree on some neighborhood of $0$, and identities between germs are understood in this sense. By the iterated Cauchy formula of section 7.1, every germ is given near $0$ by its Taylor series (7.1), which converges normally on a small polydisc. Conversely, a power series $\sum _\alpha c_\alpha z^\alpha $ with $\sum _\alpha |c_\alpha |r^{|\alpha |}<\infty $ for some $r>0$ defines a holomorphic function on the polydisc $\{ |z_ j|<r\} $: since $|c_\alpha |\leq Cr^{-|\alpha |}$, the series of partial derivatives of each order converge normally on every polydisc $\{ |z_ j|\leq s\} $ with $s<r$, so the sum is smooth, may be differentiated termwise, and satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann equations. Thus germs are the same as convergent power series, $\mathcal O_ n=\mathbb C\{ z_1,\ldots ,z_ n\} $, and $\mathcal O_0=\mathbb C$.

The ring $\mathcal O_ n$ is an integral domain: if $fg=0$ near $0$ and $f\neq 0$, then $g$ vanishes on the nonempty open subset $\{ f\neq 0\} $ of a connected neighborhood of $0$, so $g=0$ by the identity theorem. A germ that does not vanish at the origin has a holomorphic reciprocal near the origin; hence $\mathcal O_ n$ is a local ring, whose maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ consists of the germs vanishing at the origin. The order $\operatorname {ord}_0f$ of a nonzero germ $f$ is the degree of the first nonzero homogeneous term of its Taylor expansion. The order of a product is the sum of the orders. Indeed, if $P_ s$ and $Q_ t$ are the first nonzero homogeneous terms of $f$ and $g$, then $P_ sQ_ t$ is the homogeneous term of degree $s+t$ of $fg$. It is not zero: a polynomial vanishing on a nonempty open set is zero, so the sets $\{ P_ s\neq 0\} $ and $\{ Q_ t\neq 0\} $ are open and dense, and therefore meet. Hence every element of the $k$th power $\mathfrak m^ k$ has order at least $k$.

Conversely, let $f=\sum _\beta c_\beta z^\beta $ have order at least $k$. Assign to each $\beta $ with $|\beta |\geq k$ the first multi-index $\alpha \leq \beta $ with $|\alpha |=k$ in the lexicographic order, and let $f_\alpha =\sum c_\beta z^{\beta -\alpha }$, the sum over the $\beta $ assigned to $\alpha $. These series converge normally wherever the series of $f$ does, so $f_\alpha \in \mathcal O_ n$, and $f=\sum _{|\alpha |=k}z^\alpha f_\alpha \in \mathfrak m^ k$. Thus $\operatorname {ord}_0f\geq k$ if and only if $f\in \mathfrak m^ k$. A holomorphic change of coordinates fixing $0$ induces a ring automorphism of $\mathcal O_ n$ that preserves $\mathfrak m$ and its powers; so the order does not depend on the coordinates. For a holomorphic function $f$ near a point $a$ of a complex manifold whose germ at $a$ is nonzero, $\operatorname {ord}_ af$ is the order of its germ in local coordinates centered at $a$. This is the order used for Lelong numbers in proposition 12.1.4; chapter 12 also defines the order of a coherent ideal at a point and the order along a prime divisor (definition 12.6.3).

Write points of $\mathbb C^ n$, $n\geq 1$, as $(z',w)$ with $z'\in \mathbb C^{n-1}$. A Weierstrass polynomial of degree $m\geq 0$ in $w$ is a polynomial

\[ P(z',w)=w^ m+a_1(z')w^{m-1}+\cdots +a_ m(z'),\qquad a_ j\in \mathcal O_{n-1},\quad a_ j(0)=0. \]

We shall often use the following bound for the roots $t$ of a monic polynomial with complex coefficients:

A.1
\begin{equation} t^ m+d_1t^{m-1}+\cdots +d_ m=0\quad \Longrightarrow \quad |t|\le 2\max _{1\leq j\leq m}|d_ j|^{1/j}. \end{equation}

Indeed, otherwise $|d_ j|<(|t|/2)^ j$ for all $j$, and dividing the equation by $t^ m$ gives $1\le \sum _{j=1}^ m|d_ j/t^ j|<\sum _{j=1}^ m2^{-j}<1$. In particular, the roots of $P(z',\cdot )$ tend to $0$ as $z'\to 0$.

A germ $f\in \mathcal O_ n$ is distinguished in $w$ if $f(0,w)$ is not identically zero. If $f(0,w)$ vanishes to order $m\geq 1$ at $w=0$, the Weierstrass preparation theorem (theorem 7.4.2) writes $f$ as a unit times a Weierstrass polynomial of degree $m$; if $f(0)\neq 0$, then $f$ is itself a unit, which is the case $m=0$. Every nonzero germ becomes distinguished in the last coordinate after a linear change of coordinates. Indeed, let $P_ s$ be the first nonzero homogeneous term of $f$ and choose $v$ with $P_ s(v)\neq 0$; in linear coordinates whose last basis vector is $v$, we have $f(0,w)=P_ s(v)w^ s+O(w^{s+1})$, which vanishes to order exactly $s$, the order of $f$. Since $P_ s\neq 0$, the admissible vectors $v$ form a dense open set. Hence the new coordinates can be chosen arbitrarily close to given ones, and finitely many nonzero germs become distinguished simultaneously if $v$ avoids the zero set of the product of their first homogeneous terms.

Proposition A.1.1 (Weierstrass division). Let $n\geq 1$ and $m\geq 0$, and let $P$ be a Weierstrass polynomial of degree $m$ in $w$. Then every germ $f\in \mathcal O_ n$ can be written uniquely as

\[ f=Pq+R,\qquad q\in \mathcal O_ n,\quad R\in \mathcal O_{n-1}[w],\quad \deg _ w R<m. \]

Moreover, let $\Delta '\subset \mathbb C^{n-1}$ be a polydisc centered at $0$ and $\Delta =\{ |w|<r\} $ a disc such that the coefficients of $P$ are holomorphic near $\overline{\Delta '}$ and all roots of $P(z',\cdot )$, $z'\in \Delta '$, lie in $\{ |w|\leq \rho \} $ for some $\rho <r$; such polydiscs $\Delta '\times \Delta $ exist with arbitrarily small radii. Then every holomorphic function $f$ on $\Delta '\times \Delta $ can be written uniquely as $f=Pq+R$, where $q$ is holomorphic on $\Delta '\times \Delta $ and $R$ is a polynomial in $w$ of degree less than $m$ with holomorphic coefficients on $\Delta '$; for every $s\in (\rho ,r)$, $z'\in \Delta '$ and $|w|<s$,

A.2
\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} q(z',w)& =\frac1{2\pi \mathrm i}\int _{|\zeta |=s} \frac{f(z',\zeta )}{P(z',\zeta )(\zeta -w)}\, \mathrm d\zeta ,\\ R(z',w)& =\frac1{2\pi \mathrm i}\int _{|\zeta |=s} \frac{f(z',\zeta )}{P(z',\zeta )}\, \frac{P(z',\zeta )-P(z',w)}{\zeta -w}\, \mathrm d\zeta . \end{aligned} \end{equation}

Division is a bounded linear operation on bounded holomorphic functions on $\Delta '\times \Delta $: there is a constant $C$, independent of $f$, with

\[ \sup _{\Delta '\times \Delta }|q|+\sup _{\Delta '\times \Delta }|R|\le C\sup _{\Delta '\times \Delta }|f|. \]

Proof. Polydiscs as in the statement exist with arbitrarily small radii, because the roots of $P(z',\cdot )$ tend to $0$ as $z'\to 0$, by (A.1).

Let $\Delta '\times \Delta $ be such a polydisc and $f$ a holomorphic function on it. For $\rho <s<r$ and $|w|<s$ define $q$ and $R$ by (A.2). For fixed $z'$ and $w$, both integrands are holomorphic in $\zeta $ on the annulus $\{ \rho <|\zeta |<r\} $, except for the pole of the first one at $\zeta =w$; by Cauchy's theorem the integrals do not depend on $s$ as long as $|w|<s$, and the second one does not depend on $s\in (\rho ,r)$ at all. Hence $q$ and $R$ are defined on $\Delta '\times \Delta $, and they are holomorphic by differentiation under the integral sign. The difference quotient $(P(z',\zeta )-P(z',w))/(\zeta -w)$ is a polynomial in $w$ of degree $m-1$ whose coefficients are polynomials in $\zeta $ and in the $a_ j(z')$; hence $R$ is a polynomial in $w$ of degree less than $m$ with holomorphic coefficients on $\Delta '$. Adding the two integrals, we obtain $Pq+R=(2\pi \mathrm i)^{-1}\int _{|\zeta |=s}f(z',\zeta )(\zeta -w)^{-1}\, \mathrm d\zeta =f$ by Cauchy's formula.

For the estimate, let $f$ be bounded, and fix $s_0=(\rho +r)/2$. For $|\zeta |\geq s_0$ we have $|P(z',\zeta )|=\prod _ l|\zeta -\lambda _ l(z')|\geq (s_0-\rho )^ m$, where $\lambda _ l(z')$ are the roots, and the coefficients of the difference quotient are bounded for $z'\in \Delta '$, $|\zeta |=s_0$ and $|w|<r$, since the $a_ j$ are bounded on $\Delta '$. The integral over $|\zeta |=s_0$ therefore gives $|R|\leq C_1\sup |f|$ on $\Delta '\times \Delta $. For $s_0\leq |w|<r$ we have $|q|=|f-R|/|P|\leq (1+C_1)(s_0-\rho )^{-m}\sup |f|$, and the maximum principle for the holomorphic functions $q(z',\cdot )$ on the discs $\{ |w|\leq s\} $, $s_0\leq s<r$, extends this bound to all of $\Delta '\times \Delta $.

For uniqueness, suppose that $Pq+R=0$ on a polydisc as above, with $R$ a polynomial in $w$ of degree less than $m$. For each $z'$, the polynomial $R(z',\cdot )=-P(z',\cdot )q(z',\cdot )$ vanishes at the $m$ roots of $P(z',\cdot )$, counted with multiplicity, all of which lie in $\Delta $. Its degree is less than $m$, so $R(z',\cdot )=0$. Then $P(z',\cdot )q(z',\cdot )=0$, and since $P(z',\cdot )$ has only finitely many zeros, the continuous function $q(z',\cdot )$ vanishes on a dense set, hence everywhere. Finally, every germ is represented by a bounded holomorphic function on some polydisc as above, and an identity between germs holds on some such polydisc; so the assertions for germs follow. $\square$

The first consequence of division is the Noetherian property of $\mathcal O_ n$.

Proposition A.1.2. Every ideal of $\mathcal O_ n$ is finitely generated, and every submodule of $\mathcal O_ n^ r$, $r\geq 1$, is finitely generated. Consequently, every increasing sequence of ideals of $\mathcal O_ n$, or of submodules of a finitely generated $\mathcal O_ n$-module, is eventually constant.

Proof. We first recall three algebraic facts about a commutative ring $\Lambda $ all of whose ideals are finitely generated. Every submodule $N$ of $\Lambda ^ r$ is finitely generated, by induction on $r$: the projection of $N$ to the last coordinate is an ideal; choose elements of $N$ whose last coordinates generate it, and add generators of $N\cap (\Lambda ^{r-1}\times \{ 0\} )$, which exist by the induction hypothesis. A finitely generated module is a quotient of some $\Lambda ^ r$, and the inverse image of a submodule is a submodule of $\Lambda ^ r$; hence submodules of finitely generated modules are finitely generated. Finally, the union of an increasing sequence of submodules is a submodule; its finitely many generators already lie in one member of the sequence, which is therefore stationary from then on.

It remains to prove that the ideals of $\mathcal O_ n$ are finitely generated, which we do by induction on $n$. The ring $\mathcal O_0=\mathbb C$ is a field. Let $n\geq 1$ and let $I\subset \mathcal O_ n$ be an ideal. If $I=0$ or $I$ contains a unit, it is generated by one element. Otherwise choose a nonzero germ in $I$, and coordinates in which it is distinguished in $w$. By the preparation theorem it is a unit times a Weierstrass polynomial $P$ of some degree $m\geq 1$, and $P\in I$. Divide every $f\in I$ by $P$ using proposition A.1.1; the remainder $R_ f=f-Pq$ again belongs to $I$. The remainders of degree less than $m$ that lie in $I$ form an $\mathcal O_{n-1}$-submodule of $\mathcal O_{n-1}[w]$, which we identify, through their coefficients, with a submodule of $\mathcal O_{n-1}^ m$. By the induction hypothesis and the first paragraph, it is generated by finitely many remainders $R_1,\ldots ,R_ k$. Then $P,R_1,\ldots ,R_ k$ generate $I$. $\square$

Division also factors a monic polynomial near each of its roots.

Lemma A.1.3. Let $n\geq 1$, let $P(z',w)=w^\mu +a_1(z')w^{\mu -1}+\cdots +a_\mu (z')$ be a monic polynomial in $w$ whose coefficients are holomorphic near a point $x'\in \mathbb C^{n-1}$, and let $w_0$ be a root of multiplicity $\mu '\geq 1$ of $P(x',\cdot )$. Then, near $x=(x',w_0)$,

\[ P=P_ xQ_ x, \]

where $P_ x$ is a Weierstrass polynomial of degree $\mu '$ in $w-w_0$ with respect to the coordinates $(z'-x',w-w_0)$, and $Q_ x$ is a monic polynomial of degree $\mu -\mu '$ in $w$ with coefficients holomorphic near $x'$ and $Q_ x(x)\neq 0$.

Proof. In the coordinates $(z'-x',w-w_0)$ centered at $x$, the function $w\mapsto P(x',w)$ has a zero of order $\mu '$ at $w_0$. By the Weierstrass preparation theorem (theorem 7.4.2), $P$ is, near $x$, a nowhere vanishing holomorphic function $U$ times a Weierstrass polynomial $P_ x$ of degree $\mu '$ in $w-w_0$. Since $P_ x$ is monic in $w$, Euclidean division of the polynomial $P$ by $P_ x$, with coefficients holomorphic near $x'$, gives $P=P_ xQ+R$, where $Q$ is a monic polynomial of degree $\mu -\mu '$ in $w$ and $R$ is a polynomial in $w$, equivalently in $w-w_0$, of degree less than $\mu '$. The identities $P=P_ xQ+R$ and $P=P_ xU+0$ are two divisions of the germ of $P$ at $x$ by $P_ x$; by the uniqueness in proposition A.1.1, applied in the coordinates centered at $x$, we get $Q=U$ near $x$ and $R=0$. Hence $Q_ x=Q$ has the required properties, with $Q_ x(x)=U(x)\neq 0$. $\square$

We use sheaves in the following elementary form. A sheaf $\mathcal F$ of modules on a topological space assigns to each open set $U$ a module $\mathcal F(U)$ of sections, with restriction maps, such that sections that agree locally are equal and compatible local sections glue. The stalk $\mathcal F_ x$ consists of the germs at $x$ of sections defined near $x$. Kernels and images of morphisms of sheaves are defined stalkwise, and a sequence of morphisms is exact if it is exact on every stalk; in particular, a section of the image of a morphism is, near each point, the image of a section of the source. Similarly, the quotient $\mathcal S/\mathcal F$ of a sheaf of modules by a subsheaf has the stalks $\mathcal S_ x/\mathcal F_ x$, and its sections are the families of classes that are, near each point, the classes of a section of $\mathcal S$; so sections of $\mathcal S/\mathcal F$ can be lifted locally to sections of $\mathcal S$. The cokernel of a morphism is the quotient of its target by its image.

Let $\mathcal A$ be a sheaf of rings and $\mathcal F$ a sheaf of $\mathcal A$-modules. We say that $\mathcal F$ is locally finitely generated if every point has a neighborhood $U$ and finitely many sections $s_1,\ldots ,s_ p\in \mathcal F(U)$ whose germs generate $\mathcal F_ y$ over $\mathcal A_ y$ for every $y\in U$. This requires one family of generators on a whole neighborhood, not merely finite generation of each stalk. The sheaf of relations of sections $s_1,\ldots ,s_ p\in \mathcal F(U)$ is the kernel of the morphism $\mathcal A^ p|_ U\to \mathcal F|_ U$, $(a_ i)\mapsto \sum _ ia_ is_ i$. The sheaf $\mathcal F$ is coherent if it is locally finitely generated and the sheaf of relations of every finite family of sections over every open set is locally finitely generated.

Lemma A.1.4. Let $\mathcal A$ be a sheaf of rings. Kernels, images and cokernels of morphisms between coherent $\mathcal A$-modules are coherent. If two of the three sheaves in an exact sequence $0\to \mathcal F\to \mathcal S\to \mathcal G\to 0$ of $\mathcal A$-modules are coherent, so is the third. Finite direct sums of coherent sheaves are coherent. If $\mathcal A$ is coherent as a module over itself, every locally finitely generated $\mathcal A$-submodule of $\mathcal A^ r$ is coherent.

Proof. A locally finitely generated submodule of a coherent sheaf is coherent, because its sections have the same relations when they are regarded as sections of the larger sheaf. The image of a morphism from a coherent sheaf is locally generated by the images of local generators of the source, so the image of a morphism between coherent sheaves is coherent. For the kernel of a morphism $\phi \colon \mathcal F\to \mathcal G$ between coherent sheaves, choose local generators $F_1,\ldots ,F_ p$ of $\mathcal F$ and local generators $R_ j=(R_{j1},\ldots ,R_{jp})$ of the relations of $\phi (F_1),\ldots ,\phi (F_ p)$. If $\sum _ ia_ iF_ i$ lies in the kernel, then $(a_ i)$ is a relation of the $\phi (F_ i)$, hence a combination of the $R_ j$; so the sections $\sum _ iR_{ji}F_ i$ generate the kernel, which is therefore coherent.

Let $\mathcal F\subset \mathcal S$ be coherent sheaves and consider $\mathcal G=\mathcal S/\mathcal F$; this covers cokernels, because images are coherent. Local generators of $\mathcal S$ map to local generators of $\mathcal G$. Given sections $G_1,\ldots ,G_ p$ of $\mathcal G$, lift them locally to sections $S_1,\ldots ,S_ p$ of $\mathcal S$, and choose local generators $F_1,\ldots ,F_ k$ of $\mathcal F$. A tuple $(a_ i)$ is a relation of the $G_ i$ exactly when $\sum _ ia_ iS_ i\in \mathcal F$, that is, when $(a,b)$ is a relation of $(S_1,\ldots ,S_ p,F_1,\ldots ,F_ k)$ in $\mathcal S$ for some $b$. Hence the projections to the first $p$ coordinates of local generators of the latter relations generate the relations of the $G_ i$, and $\mathcal G$ is coherent.

Now let $\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal G$ be coherent. Local lifts of generators of $\mathcal G$, together with local generators of $\mathcal F$, generate $\mathcal S$. Given sections $S_1,\ldots ,S_ p$ of $\mathcal S$, let $R_1,\ldots ,R_ l$ generate the relations of their images in $\mathcal G$. The combinations $H_ j=\sum _ iR_{ji}S_ i$ lie in $\mathcal F$; let $Q_1,\ldots ,Q_ k$ generate their relations. Every relation $(a_ i)$ of the $S_ i$ is first a relation of their images, so $a=\sum _ jc_ jR_ j$, and then $\sum _ jc_ jH_ j=\sum _ ia_ iS_ i=0$, so $c$ is a combination of $Q_1,\ldots ,Q_ k$. Hence the tuples $\sum _ jQ_{\nu j}R_ j$, $1\leq \nu \leq k$, which are relations of the $S_ i$, generate all of them, and $\mathcal S$ is coherent. The remaining case of the exact sequence, with $\mathcal S$ and $\mathcal G$ coherent, is the kernel case. A finite direct sum is an extension $0\to \mathcal F\to \mathcal F\oplus \mathcal G\to \mathcal G\to 0$. Finally, if $\mathcal A$ is coherent, so is $\mathcal A^ r$, and the last assertion follows from the first sentence of the proof. $\square$

Theorem A.1.5 (Oka's coherence theorem). The sheaf $\mathcal O_ M$ of holomorphic functions on a complex manifold $M$ is coherent. In particular, the kernel of the morphism $\mathcal O_ U^ q\to \mathcal O_ U^ p$ defined by a $p\times q$ matrix of holomorphic functions on an open set $U\subset M$ is locally finitely generated.

Proof. The sheaf $\mathcal O_ M$ is generated by the constant $1$, and the statement is local, so we must show that the relations of finitely many holomorphic functions $F_1,\ldots ,F_ q$ near a point of $\mathbb C^ n$, which we take to be the origin, are locally finitely generated. We argue by induction on $n$: we reduce to relations that are polynomials in one variable of bounded degree, and apply the induction hypothesis to their coefficients. For $n=0$ the assertion is finite-dimensional linear algebra. Let $n\geq 1$. If the germ of some $F_ j$ at $0$ is zero, then $F_ j$ vanishes on a connected neighborhood, and the relations of the $F_ i$ are those of the remaining functions, with an arbitrary $j$th coordinate; so we may assume that no germ $F_ j$ is zero (if all are zero, every tuple is a relation). Choose coordinates $(z',w)$ in which all $F_ j$ are distinguished in $w$. By the preparation theorem, on a small polydisc each $F_ j$ is a holomorphic unit $u_ j$ times a Weierstrass polynomial; since $(g_ j)$ is a relation of the $F_ j$ exactly when $(u_ jg_ j)$ is a relation of the Weierstrass polynomials, we may assume that each $F_ j$ is a Weierstrass polynomial in $w$ whose coefficients are holomorphic on a polydisc $\Delta '\subset \mathbb C^{n-1}$. Renumbering, let $F_ q$ have the maximal degree $\mu $.

We claim that at every point $x=(x',w_0)$ of the polydisc, the stalk of relations is generated by relations whose coordinates are polynomials in $w$ of degree at most $\mu $ with coefficients holomorphic near $x'$. If $F_ q(x)\neq 0$, then for a relation $g=(g_1,\ldots ,g_ q)$ we have $g_ q=-\sum _{j<q}g_ jF_ j/F_ q$, so $g=\sum _{j<q}(g_ j/F_ q)(F_ qe_ j-F_ je_ q)$ is a combination of the elementary relations $F_ qe_ j-F_ je_ q$, which are polynomial of degree at most $\mu $. Suppose that $F_ q(x)=0$, and let $\mu '\geq 1$ be the order of the root $w_0$ of $F_ q(x',\cdot )$. By lemma A.1.3, near $x$ the monic polynomial $F_ q$ is the product of a Weierstrass polynomial $P_ x$ of degree $\mu '$ in $w-w_0$ and a monic polynomial $Q_ x$ in $w$ of degree $\mu -\mu '$:

\[ F_ q=P_ xQ_ x,\qquad Q_ x(x)\neq 0. \]

Let $g$ be a relation at $x$. Dividing each $g_ j$, $j<q$, by $P_ x$ (proposition A.1.1) and writing $P_ x=F_ qQ_ x^{-1}$, we obtain $g_ j=F_ qt_ j+r_ j$ with $\deg _ wr_ j<\mu '$. Subtracting $\sum _{j<q}t_ j(F_ qe_ j-F_ je_ q)$ from $g$, we obtain a relation whose first $q-1$ coordinates are $r_1,\ldots ,r_{q-1}$; we call its last coordinate $r_ q$. Then

\[ P_ xQ_ xr_ q=-\sum _{j<q}F_ jr_ j. \]

The right-hand side is a polynomial in $w$; dividing it by $P_ x$ as a polynomial and comparing with the uniqueness of division at $x$, we find that $Q_ xr_ q$ is a polynomial of degree less than $\mu $. Each $Q_ xr_ j$, $j<q$, is also a polynomial of degree at most $(\mu -\mu ')+(\mu '-1)<\mu $. Thus $(Q_ xr_1,\ldots ,Q_ xr_ q)$ is a polynomial relation of degree less than $\mu $, and $(r_ j)$ is its product with the unit $Q_ x^{-1}$. This proves the claim.

A tuple of polynomials $g_ j=\sum _{k=0}^{\mu }c_{jk}(z')w^ k$ is a relation exactly when the $2\mu +1$ coefficients of the polynomial $\sum _ jF_ jg_ j$ vanish. These coefficients are given by a holomorphic matrix acting on the coefficient vectors $(c_{jk})$, with entries formed from the coefficients of the $F_ j$. By the induction hypothesis and lemma A.1.4, the kernel of this matrix is coherent, so it has finitely many generators on a smaller polydisc $\Delta '_1$, generating its stalk at every point of $\Delta '_1$. The corresponding polynomial tuples are relations of the $F_ j$, and they generate, over the rings of germs on the base, all polynomial relations of degree at most $\mu $ at every point over $\Delta '_1$. By the claim, they generate all relations at every point of the polydisc over $\Delta '_1$. This proves that $\mathcal O_ M$ is coherent. The last assertion follows from lemma A.1.4, since the kernel of a morphism between the coherent sheaves $\mathcal O_ U^ q$ and $\mathcal O_ U^ p$ is coherent. $\square$

We now turn to the zero sets of holomorphic functions. An analytic subset of a complex manifold $M$ (definition 7.1.1) is a closed subset $A\subset M$ such that every point of $M$ has a neighborhood $U$ and finitely many holomorphic functions $g_1,\ldots ,g_ k$ on $U$ with $A\cap U=\{ g_1=\cdots =g_ k=0\} $. It is proper if $A\neq M$.

Lemma A.1.6. Let $A$ be a proper analytic subset of a connected complex manifold $M$. Then $A$ is nowhere dense in $M$. Moreover, if $U\subset M$ is a nonempty connected open set and $g_1,\ldots ,g_ k$ are holomorphic functions on $U$ with $A\cap U=\{ g_1=\cdots =g_ k=0\} $, then some $g_ i$ is not identically zero on $U$. In particular, every point of $A$ has a connected open neighborhood $U$ with a holomorphic function $g\not\equiv 0$ on $U$ such that $A\cap U\subset \{ g=0\} $.

Proof. The interior of $A$ is open, and we show that it is closed in $M$. Let $a\in M$ be a limit of interior points of $A$, and choose a connected coordinate neighborhood $U$ of $a$ and functions $g_1,\ldots ,g_ k$ with $A\cap U=\{ g_1=\cdots =g_ k=0\} $. Each $g_ i$ vanishes on a nonempty open subset of $U$, hence on $U$ by the identity theorem; so $U\subset A$, and $a$ is an interior point. Since $M$ is connected and $A\neq M$, the interior of $A$ is empty. In the second assertion, if all $g_ i$ vanished identically on $U$, the nonempty open set $U$ would lie in $A$, contradicting the first. For the third, take for $U$ a connected coordinate ball on which $A$ is the common zero set of finitely many holomorphic functions, and for $g$ one of them that is not identically zero, which exists by the second assertion. $\square$

We recall some algebraic terminology. The field of fractions $\operatorname {Frac}(R)$ of an integral domain $R$ consists of the formal quotients $b/c$ with $b,c\in R$ and $c\neq 0$, where $b/c=b'/c'$ if $bc'=b'c$, with the usual addition and multiplication; it contains $R$ through $b\mapsto b/1$. More generally, the total ring of fractions of a commutative ring $R$ is defined in the same way, with denominators restricted to the nonzerodivisors of $R$. An element of a commutative ring containing $R$ is integral over $R$ if it is a root of a monic polynomial with coefficients in $R$, and $R$ is integrally closed if every element of $\operatorname {Frac}(R)$ that is integral over $R$ belongs to $R$.

Lemma A.1.7. Let $A$ be a proper analytic subset of a connected complex manifold $M$. Every holomorphic function on $M\setminus A$ which is locally bounded near $A$ extends uniquely to a holomorphic function on $M$, and $M\setminus A$ is connected. Moreover, let $b,c\in \mathcal O_ n$ with $c\neq 0$, and suppose that representatives satisfy $|b|\leq C|c|$ on a neighborhood of $0$ for a constant $C$. Then $b\in c\, \mathcal O_ n$. In particular, the ring $\mathcal O_ n$ is integrally closed.

Proof. Let $f$ be holomorphic on $M\setminus A$ and locally bounded near $A$. If $\dim M=0$, then $A=\varnothing $ and there is nothing to prove. Fix $a\in A$. By lemma A.1.6 there is a holomorphic function $g\not\equiv 0$ on a connected neighborhood of $a$ vanishing on $A$; choose linear coordinates $(z',w)$ centered at $a$ in which $g$ is distinguished in $w$. By the Weierstrass preparation theorem and (A.1), there is a polydisc $\Delta '\times \Delta $ centered at $a$, with $\Delta =\{ |w|<r\} $, on which $g$ is a unit times a Weierstrass polynomial $W$ and $f$ is bounded off $A$, such that all roots of $W(z',\cdot )$, $z'\in \Delta '$, have modulus at most some $\rho <r$. For fixed $z'\in \Delta '$, the function $f(z',\cdot )$ is holomorphic on $\Delta $ outside the finitely many points of $A$ in the fiber, which are roots of $W(z',\cdot )$, and it is bounded near them; by the removable singularity theorem in one variable it extends holomorphically to $\Delta $. For $\rho <s<r$ the function

\[ F(z',w)=\frac1{2\pi \mathrm i}\int _{|\zeta |=s}\frac{f(z',\zeta )}{\zeta -w}\, \mathrm d\zeta ,\qquad z'\in \Delta ',\ |w|<s, \]

is holomorphic, by differentiation under the integral sign, since $f$ is holomorphic near $\Delta '\times \{ |\zeta |=s\} $, a set disjoint from $A$. By Cauchy's formula for the extended functions $f(z',\cdot )$, it agrees with $f$ outside $A$. Letting $s\uparrow r$, we obtain a holomorphic extension of $f$ to $\Delta '\times \Delta $. Since $A$ is nowhere dense, two holomorphic extensions on overlapping open sets agree, being continuous functions that coincide on a dense subset; so the local extensions glue to a holomorphic function on $M$, and this extension is unique.

If $M\setminus A$ were disconnected, the function equal to $0$ on one nonempty union of its connected components and to $1$ on the others would be bounded and holomorphic on $M\setminus A$. Its holomorphic extension is continuous and takes values in $\{ 0,1\} $ on the dense set $M\setminus A$, hence everywhere; since $M$ is connected, it is constant, a contradiction.

Now let $b,c\in \mathcal O_ n$, $c\neq 0$, with $|b|\leq C|c|$ on a connected neighborhood $U$ of the origin on which both are defined. The set $\{ c=0\} $ is a proper analytic subset of $U$, and the holomorphic function $b/c$ on $U\setminus \{ c=0\} $ is bounded by $C$. By the first part it extends to a holomorphic function $h$ on $U$, and $ch=b$ on the dense set $U\setminus \{ c=0\} $, hence on $U$; so $b\in c\, \mathcal O_ n$. Finally, let $b/c\in \operatorname {Frac}(\mathcal O_ n)$, with $c\neq 0$, satisfy a monic equation $(b/c)^ m+a_1(b/c)^{m-1}+\cdots +a_ m=0$ with $a_ j\in \mathcal O_ n$. Multiplying by $c^ m$, we obtain the identity $b^ m+a_1b^{m-1}c+\cdots +a_ mc^ m=0$ between germs, which holds on a connected neighborhood of $0$ on which the $a_ j$ are bounded. At the points of this neighborhood where $c\neq 0$, the number $b/c$ is a root of a monic polynomial with bounded coefficients, so $|b/c|$ is bounded there by (A.1); at the points where $c=0$ the identity gives $b^ m=0$. Hence $|b|\leq C|c|$ on this neighborhood for some constant $C$, and by what we have just proved, $b/c\in \mathcal O_ n$, which proves that $\mathcal O_ n$ is integrally closed. $\square$

A.2 Analytic sets and their local branches

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

The local study of an analytic set rests on the parametrization of an irreducible germ as a branched covering of a polydisc (theorem A.2.3). From it we derive the Nullstellensatz, the dimension theory, Cartan's coherence theorem, the structure of the singular locus and the irreducible components. The section ends with hypersurfaces and the currents supported on analytic sets.

Let $M$ be a complex manifold and $x\in M$. Two subsets of $M$ define the same germ at $x$ if they agree on a neighborhood of $x$; an analytic germ at $x$ is the germ $(A,x)$ of an analytic subset $A$ of a neighborhood of $x$. Holomorphic coordinates identify the ring $\mathcal O_{M,x}$ of germs of holomorphic functions at $x$ with $\mathcal O_ n$. For an analytic germ $(A,x)$ let $I_{A,x}\subset \mathcal O_{M,x}$ be the ideal of germs vanishing on $(A,x)$. Conversely, for an ideal $I\subset \mathcal O_{M,x}$ let $V(I)$ be the germ at $x$ of the common zero set of finitely many generators of $I$, which exist by proposition A.1.2; it does not depend on the choice of generators. Both operations reverse inclusions, and $V(I_{A,x})=(A,x)$, because local defining functions of $A$ belong to $I_{A,x}$. Finite unions and finite intersections of analytic subsets are analytic: they are defined locally by the products of the defining functions and by the union of the families of defining functions, respectively. A nonempty analytic germ is irreducible if it is not the union of two analytic subgerms different from it.

A point $a$ of an analytic subset $A\subset M$ is regular if $A$ is a complex submanifold of $M$ near $a$; the dimension of this submanifold is the dimension of $A$ at the regular point $a$. The regular points form an open subset $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ of $A$, the regular locus, and $A_{\mathrm{sing}}=A\setminus A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is the singular locus.

Proposition A.2.1. Every analytic germ is the union of finitely many irreducible analytic germs, and this decomposition is unique up to order if none of its members is contained in another. A nonempty analytic germ is irreducible if and only if its ideal is prime. The members of the decomposition are called the irreducible branches, or local branches, of the germ.

Proof. A decreasing sequence of analytic germs is eventually constant, because the corresponding ideals increase and are eventually constant by proposition A.1.2, and $V(I_{A,x})=(A,x)$. Split a reducible germ into two analytic subgerms different from it, split each reducible piece again, and so on. If this process did not terminate, the resulting tree, in which every vertex has at most two children, would be infinite, and choosing at each level a child with infinitely many descendants would produce a strictly decreasing infinite sequence of analytic germs, which is impossible. Hence a finite decomposition into irreducible germs exists; removing members contained in others, we may assume that no member is contained in another. An irreducible germ contained in a finite union of analytic germs is contained in one of them, since it is the union of its intersections with them. Now let $\bigcup _ iA_ i=\bigcup _ jB_ j$ be two such decompositions. Each $A_ i$ is contained in some $B_ j$, which in turn is contained in some $A_ k$; then $A_ i\subset A_ k$ forces $k=i$ and $A_ i=B_ j$. By symmetry the two decompositions agree.

Let $(A,x)$ be irreducible. Its ideal is proper, since $x\in A$. If $fg$ vanishes on $(A,x)$, then $(A,x)$ is the union of its intersections with $\{ f=0\} $ and $\{ g=0\} $, so one of them is all of $(A,x)$, and $f$ or $g$ belongs to $I_{A,x}$; thus $I_{A,x}$ is prime. Conversely, suppose that $(A,x)$ is the union of two analytic subgerms $(B,x)$ and $(C,x)$ different from it. Since $V(I_{B,x})=(B,x)\neq (A,x)$, there is a germ $f\in I_{B,x}\setminus I_{A,x}$, and similarly $g\in I_{C,x}\setminus I_{A,x}$. Then $fg$ vanishes on $(A,x)$ while neither factor does, so $I_{A,x}$ is not prime. $\square$

Multiple roots of polynomials are detected by the discriminant, which we define by a polynomial formula in the coefficients. For a monic polynomial $P(T)=T^ q+a_1T^{q-1}+\cdots +a_ q$, $q\geq 1$, with coefficients in a commutative ring, let $C_ P$ be its companion matrix, the $q\times q$ matrix with entries $1$ just below the diagonal, last column $(-a_ q,\ldots ,-a_1)^{\mathsf T}$ and zeros elsewhere. Put $S_ k=\operatorname {tr}(C_ P^ k)$ for $k\geq 0$, and define the discriminant $\operatorname {Disc}(P)=\det (S_{j+l})_{0\leq j,l\leq q-1}$, a polynomial with integer coefficients in $a_1,\ldots ,a_ q$. If the $a_ j$ are complex numbers and $\tau _1,\ldots ,\tau _ q$ are the roots of $P$, counted with multiplicity, then

\begin{equation} \operatorname {Disc}(P)=\prod _{i<k}(\tau _ i-\tau _ k)^2; \end{equation}

in particular $\operatorname {Disc}(P)\neq 0$ if and only if the roots of $P$ are distinct. Indeed, the characteristic polynomial of $C_ P$ is $P$, as one sees by expanding $\det (TI-C_ P)$ along its last column, so after triangularizing $C_ P$ over $\mathbb C$ we find $S_ k=\sum _ i\tau _ i^ k$. Hence $(S_{j+l})=\Lambda ^{\mathsf T}\Lambda $ for the Vandermonde matrix $\Lambda =(\tau _ i^ j)_{1\leq i\leq q,\, 0\leq j<q}$, whose determinant is $\prod _{i<k}(\tau _ k-\tau _ i)$.

Lemma A.2.2. Let $d\geq 0$, let $K=\operatorname {Frac}(\mathcal O_ d)$, and let $B$ be an integral domain containing $\mathcal O_ d$ that is finitely generated as an $\mathcal O_ d$-module and is generated as an $\mathcal O_ d$-algebra by elements $b_1,\ldots ,b_ r$. Then the field of fractions $L$ of $B$ is a finite-dimensional vector space over $K$; let $q$ be its dimension. Moreover:

  1. Let $x\in L$ be integral over $\mathcal O_ d$. Then the minimal polynomial of $x$ over $K$, that is, the monic polynomial of least degree in $K[T]$ vanishing at $x$, has degree at most $q$ and coefficients in $\mathcal O_ d$.

  2. For all $c\in \mathbb C^ r$ outside finitely many proper linear subspaces, the powers $1,u,\ldots ,u^{q-1}$ of $u=\sum _ jc_ jb_ j$ form a basis of $L$ over $K$. In particular, the minimal polynomial $W(T)=T^ q+w_1T^{q-1}+\cdots +w_ q$ of $u$ has degree $q$, and $w_1,\ldots ,w_ q\in \mathcal O_ d$.

  3. For such $u$, the discriminant $\delta =\operatorname {Disc}(W)\in \mathcal O_ d$ is not zero, and every $g\in L$ that is integral over $\mathcal O_ d$ satisfies

    \[ \delta g=\sum _{j=0}^{q-1}d_ ju^ j,\qquad d_ j\in \mathcal O_ d. \]

Proof. We represent elements of $L$ by multiplication matrices over $K$ and evaluate these at points of $\mathbb C^ d$; integrality bounds their eigenvalues, and lemma A.1.7 turns bounded elements of $K$ into elements of $\mathcal O_ d$.

We first recall the determinant criterion for integrality. Let $R$ be a subring of an integral domain $D$, let $b\in D$, and suppose that multiplication by $b$ maps an $R$-submodule of $D$ generated by elements $v_1,\ldots ,v_ N$, not all zero, into itself: $bv_ i=\sum _ ja_{ij}v_ j$ with $a_{ij}\in R$. Multiplying the matrix $bI-(a_{ij})$ by its adjugate shows that the monic polynomial $\det (bI-(a_{ij}))$ in $b$ annihilates every $v_ i$; since $D$ is a domain, this determinant is zero, and $b$ is integral over $R$. In particular, every element of $B$ is integral over $\mathcal O_ d$; take for the $v_ i$ generators of the $\mathcal O_ d$-module $B$. Conversely, if $x_1,\ldots ,x_ k\in D$ are integral over $R$, then $R[x_1,\ldots ,x_ k]$ is a finitely generated $R$-module, since powers of each $x_ i$ can be reduced with its monic equation; by the criterion, sums and products of integral elements are integral.

The ring $KB=\{ b/a:b\in B,\ a\in \mathcal O_ d\setminus \{ 0\} \} $ is a domain, and it is spanned over $K$ by finitely many module generators of $B$, so it is a finite-dimensional vector space over $K$. Multiplication by a nonzero element is an injective linear map of $KB$, hence surjective; so $KB$ is a field, and it equals $L$. Choose a basis $e_1=1,e_2,\ldots ,e_ q$ of $L$ over $K$ consisting of elements of $B$; it can be extracted from $1$ and the module generators of $B$. For $x\in L$ let $[x]\in K^{q\times q}$ be the matrix of the $K$-linear map $y\mapsto xy$ in this basis. The map $x\mapsto [x]$ is an injective homomorphism of $K$-algebras, because the first column of $[x]$ is the coordinate vector of $xe_1=x$. Let $\operatorname {Tr}(x)=\operatorname {tr}[x]$, which does not depend on the basis, and let $\chi _ x(T)=\det (TI-[x])\in K[T]$, a monic polynomial of degree $q$; by the Cayley–Hamilton theorem $[\chi _ x(x)]=\chi _ x([x])=0$, so $\chi _ x(x)=0$. The minimal polynomial $\mu _ x$ of $x$ exists, because $1,x,\ldots ,x^ q$ are linearly dependent over $K$, and $\deg \mu _ x\leq q$. By Euclidean division in $K[T]$, $\mu _ x$ divides every polynomial in $K[T]$ that vanishes at $x$.

Values of elements of $K$. Let $U$ be a connected neighborhood of $0$ in $\mathbb C^ d$. An element of $K$ is defined at $z'\in U$ if it can be written as $a/h$ with $a,h$ holomorphic on $U$ and $h(z')\neq 0$; its value at $z'$ is then $a(z')/h(z')$. This value does not depend on the representation, since two representations $a/h=a'/h'$ satisfy $ah'=a'h$ near $0$, hence on $U$ by the identity theorem. If finitely many elements of $K$ are defined at $z'$, so are their sums and products, and their quotients by those with nonzero value at $z'$, with the corresponding values. Finitely many elements of $K$ can be written with a common denominator $h\neq 0$ and numerators holomorphic on some connected neighborhood $U$ of $0$; they are then all defined at the points of $U\setminus \{ h=0\} $, a dense open subset of $U$. We shall use the following boundedness criterion: if the values of an element $a/h$ of $K$ at the points of $V\setminus \{ h=0\} $ are bounded, for some neighborhood $V\subset U$ of $0$, then $a/h\in \mathcal O_ d$. Indeed, $|a|\leq C|h|$ on $V\setminus \{ h=0\} $, hence on $V$, since both sides are continuous functions on $V$ and $V\setminus \{ h=0\} $ is dense in $V$; so $a/h\in \mathcal O_ d$ by lemma A.1.7. (For $d=0$, the space $\mathbb C^0$ is a point and $K=\mathcal O_0=\mathbb C$.)

Proof of (1). Let $x\in L$ be integral over $\mathcal O_ d$, say $f(x)=0$ with $f(T)=T^ m+f_1T^{m-1}+\cdots +f_ m$ and $f_ j\in \mathcal O_ d$. Then $f([x])=[f(x)]=0$. Since $\chi _ x(x)=0$, we can write $\chi _ x=\mu _ x\rho $ with $\rho \in K[T]$. Write the entries of $[x]$ and the coefficients of $\mu _ x$ and $\rho $ with a common denominator $h$, so that they are all defined at the points of $U\setminus \{ h=0\} $ for a connected neighborhood $U$ of $0$; the coefficients of $\chi _ x$, polynomials in the entries of $[x]$, can then be written with the denominator $h^ q$.

At the points $z'\in U\setminus \{ h=0\} $ near $0$ the complex matrix $[x](z')$ satisfies $f(z',[x](z'))=0$. Applying this to an eigenvector, we see that every eigenvalue $\lambda $ of $[x](z')$ satisfies $f(z',\lambda )=0$, so that $|\lambda |\leq C_ f$ by (A.1), where $C_ f$ is an upper bound of $2\max _ j|f_ j|^{1/j}$ near $0$. The coefficients of $\chi _ x(z',T)$, the characteristic polynomial of $[x](z')$, are up to sign the elementary symmetric functions of these eigenvalues, so they are bounded at the points of $V\setminus \{ h=0\} $ for a neighborhood $V\subset U$ of $0$, and $\chi _ x\in \mathcal O_ d[T]$ by the boundedness criterion; in particular $\operatorname {Tr}(x)\in \mathcal O_ d$. The roots of $\mu _ x(z',\cdot )$ are roots of $\chi _ x(z',\cdot )=\mu _ x(z',\cdot )\rho (z',\cdot )$, so they are also bounded by $C_ f$; hence the coefficients of $\mu _ x(z',\cdot )$, which are up to sign the elementary symmetric functions of these roots, are bounded at the points of $V\setminus \{ h=0\} $. Therefore $\mu _ x\in \mathcal O_ d[T]$, again by the boundedness criterion, which proves (1).

Proof of (2). We evaluate the multiplication matrices at a point $z'_0$, where they span, in a suitable basis, the algebra of all diagonal matrices; then a generic combination of the matrices $[b_ k](z'_0)$ has distinct eigenvalues. We first show that the trace form is nondegenerate. Let $\Theta =\det (\operatorname {Tr}(e_ ie_ j))_{i,j}\in K$. If $\Theta =0$, there are $x_1,\ldots ,x_ q\in K$, not all zero, with $\sum _ ix_ i\operatorname {Tr}(e_ ie_ j)=0$ for all $j$; then $x=\sum _ ix_ ie_ i\neq 0$ satisfies $\operatorname {Tr}(xy)=0$ for all $y\in L$, by linearity, and $y=x^{-1}$ gives $\operatorname {Tr}(1)=q=0$, which is absurd. Write $e_ ie_ j=\sum _ l\gamma _{ijl}e_ l$ and $b_ k=\sum _ l\beta _{kl}e_ l$ with $\gamma _{ijl},\beta _{kl}\in K$, and, since $B$ is generated by the $b_ k$ as an $\mathcal O_ d$-algebra, $e_ l=G_ l(b_1,\ldots ,b_ r)$ for polynomials $G_ l$ with coefficients in $\mathcal O_ d$. Choose a connected neighborhood $U$ of $0$ and a point $z'_0\in U$ at which the finitely many elements $\Theta $, $\gamma _{ijl}$, $\beta _{kl}$, the entries of the matrices $[e_ l]$ and $[b_ k]$, and the coefficients of the $G_ l$ are defined, such that $\Theta (z'_0)\neq 0$.

Let $\mathcal N$ be the complex linear span of the matrices $N_ l=[e_ l](z'_0)$, $1\leq l\leq q$. Since $[e_ i][e_ j]=[e_ ie_ j]=\sum _ l\gamma _{ijl}[e_ l]$, the set $\mathcal N$ is a commutative algebra of complex $q\times q$ matrices, and it contains $N_1=I$. The matrix $(\operatorname {tr}(N_ iN_ j))_{i,j}$ is the value of $(\operatorname {Tr}(e_ ie_ j))_{i,j}$ at $z'_0$, which is invertible; hence the $N_ l$ are linearly independent, $\dim \mathcal N=q$, and the bilinear form $(N,N')\mapsto \operatorname {tr}(NN')$ on $\mathcal N$ is nondegenerate. If $N\in \mathcal N$ is nilpotent, then $NN'$ is nilpotent for every $N'\in \mathcal N$, because $N$ and $N'$ commute; so $\operatorname {tr}(NN')=0$ for all $N'$, and $N=0$. Consequently every $N\in \mathcal N$ is diagonalizable. Indeed, if $\lambda _1,\ldots ,\lambda _ p$ are the distinct eigenvalues of $N$, then the minimal polynomial of $N$ divides a power of $(T-\lambda _1)\cdots (T-\lambda _ p)$, so the element $(N-\lambda _1I)\cdots (N-\lambda _ pI)$ of $\mathcal N$ is nilpotent, hence zero, and the minimal polynomial of $N$ has simple roots. Commuting diagonalizable matrices are simultaneously diagonalizable; so in a suitable basis of $\mathbb C^ q$ all elements of $\mathcal N$ are diagonal, and since $\dim \mathcal N=q$, the algebra $\mathcal N$ consists of all diagonal matrices.

The matrices $[b_ k](z'_0)=\sum _ l\beta _{kl}(z'_0)N_ l$ belong to $\mathcal N$; let $\lambda ^{(i)}_ k$ be the $i$th diagonal entry of $[b_ k](z'_0)$ in this basis, and $\lambda ^{(i)}=(\lambda ^{(i)}_1,\ldots ,\lambda ^{(i)}_ r)$. Therefore, for $c$ outside the finitely many proper linear subspaces $\{ c:\sum _ kc_ k(\lambda ^{(i)}_ k-\lambda ^{(i')}_ k)=0\} $, $i<i'$, the matrix $[u](z'_0)=\sum _ kc_ k[b_ k](z'_0)$ is diagonal with $q$ distinct diagonal entries $\tau _1,\ldots ,\tau _ q$.

Fix such a $c$, and let $\Lambda \in K^{q\times q}$ be the matrix whose columns are the coordinate vectors of $1,u,\ldots ,u^{q-1}$; its entries are polynomials in the entries of the $[b_ k]$, so they are defined at $z'_0$. The linear map $\mathcal N\to \mathbb C^ q$ that takes a matrix to its first column, its product with the coordinate vector $(1,0,\ldots ,0)^{\mathsf T}$ of $e_1=1$, maps $N_ l$ to the $l$th unit vector, the coordinate vector of $e_ le_1=e_ l$; so it is bijective. Hence $\det \Lambda (z'_0)\neq 0$, so $\det \Lambda \neq 0$, and $1,u,\ldots ,u^{q-1}$ is a basis of $L$. Consequently $\mu _ u$ has degree $q$, and since $u\in B$ is integral over $\mathcal O_ d$, its coefficients lie in $\mathcal O_ d$ by (1). This proves (2).

Proof of (3). In the basis $1,u,\ldots ,u^{q-1}$, multiplication by $u$ has the companion matrix $C_ W$, because $u\cdot u^{q-1}=-\sum _ jw_ ju^{q-j}$. \[ \sum _{j=0}^{q-1}S_{j+l}\, a_ j=\operatorname {Tr}(gu^ l),\qquad 0\leq l<q. \]

Proof. We prove more generally the following statement for every prime ideal $J\subset \mathcal O_ n$: the germ $V(J)$ has coordinates and polydiscs as in the theorem, and $I_{V(J),0}=J$. The theorem then follows by taking $J=I_{A,0}$, which is prime by proposition A.2.1 and satisfies $V(J)=(A,0)$. If $J=0$, then $V(J)$ is a neighborhood of $0$; we take $d=n$, $q=1$, $u=0$ and $W(z',T)=T$, so that $\delta =\operatorname {Disc}(W)=1$, and $I_{V(0),0}=0$ by the identity theorem. Assume that $J\neq 0$. Steps 1–3 construct the covering and prove that $I_{V(J),0}=J$; Steps 4 and 5 prove the density and the connectedness of the covering locus.

Step 1: triangular equations. Since $J$ is prime, it is a proper ideal, so its elements vanish at $0$. Choose a nonzero element of $J$, of order $s_ n\geq 1$, and linear coordinates in which it is distinguished in $z_ n$. By the preparation theorem it is a unit times a Weierstrass polynomial $P_ n\in J$ of degree $s_ n$ in $z_ n$. Since a unit has order $0$, the polynomial $P_ n$ also has order $s_ n$, so its coefficient of $z_ n^{s_ n-j}$ has order at least $j$ in $(z_1,\ldots ,z_{n-1})$. The ideal $J\cap \mathcal O_{n-1}$ of germs in the first $n-1$ coordinates is again prime. If it is nonzero, we repeat the construction with a linear change of $z_1,\ldots ,z_{n-1}$ only, which preserves the properties of $P_ n$; we continue until $J\cap \mathcal O_ d=0$ for some $d$. This gives coordinates and Weierstrass polynomials

\[ P_ k=z_ k^{s_ k}+\sum _{j=1}^{s_ k}a_{jk}(z_1,\ldots ,z_{k-1})z_ k^{s_ k-j}\in J, \qquad d<k\leq n, \]

with $a_{jk}=O(|(z_1,\ldots ,z_{k-1})|^ j)$. At each step only the zero set of a nonzero homogeneous polynomial is excluded in choosing the direction, so the coordinates can be chosen arbitrarily close to given ones. Fix generators of $J$, holomorphic on a neighborhood $U$ of $0$, and let $V(J)$ also denote their common zero set in $U$. Applying (A.1) successively to $P_{d+1},\ldots ,P_ n$, we find a constant $C$ with

\[ |z''|\leq C|z'|\qquad \text{for } z\in V(J) \text{ near } 0. \]

Consequently, if $\Delta ''$ is a small polydisc and $\Delta '$ a polydisc whose radius is small compared with that of $\Delta ''$, the points of $V(J)\cap (\Delta '\times \Delta '')$ over each compact subset of $\Delta '$ form a compact set. Hence $\pi $ is proper, its fiber over $0$ is $\{ 0\} $, and its fibers are finite, since they lie in the sets of successive roots of $P_{d+1},\ldots ,P_ n$.

Successive division by $P_ n,\ldots ,P_{d+1}$ (proposition A.1.1) shows that every germ is congruent modulo $J$ to a polynomial in $z_{d+1},\ldots ,z_ n$ of degree less than $s_ k$ in $z_ k$, with coefficients in $\mathcal O_ d$. Since $J\cap \mathcal O_ d=0$, the ring $\mathcal O_ d$ injects into

\[ B=\mathcal O_ n/J, \]

and $B$ is a finitely generated $\mathcal O_ d$-module, generated by the classes of the monomials $\prod _{k>d}z_ k^{\alpha _ k}$ with $0\le \alpha _ k<s_ k$.

Step 2: a primitive element and division by the discriminant. The ring $B$ is a domain, because $J$ is prime, and by Step 1 it is generated as an $\mathcal O_ d$-algebra by the classes of $z_{d+1},\ldots ,z_ n$. We apply lemma A.2.2 to $B$ and these generators, and let $q$ be the dimension of the field of fractions of $B$ over $K=\operatorname {Frac}(\mathcal O_ d)$. Choose $c$ as in lemma A.2.2(2), and let $u=\sum _{k>d}c_ kz_ k$, which will be the primitive linear form of the statement; let $W(z',T)\in \mathcal O_ d[T]$ be its minimal polynomial, of degree $q$, and let $\delta =\operatorname {Disc}(W)\in \mathcal O_ d\setminus \{ 0\} $ be its discriminant. By lemma A.2.2(3) there are polynomials $B_ k(z',T)\in \mathcal O_ d[T]$ of degree less than $q$ with

\[ \delta z_ k\equiv B_ k(z',u)\pmod J,\qquad d<k\leq n. \]

The class of each $z_ k$, $k>d$, lies in $B$ and is therefore integral over $\mathcal O_ d$, so by lemma A.2.2(1) its minimal polynomial $W_ k(z',T)$ over $K$ is monic of degree at most $q$ with coefficients in $\mathcal O_ d$. These polynomials, and $W$, are Weierstrass polynomials in $T$. Indeed, the germ $W_ k(z',z_ k)$ belongs to $J$, so it vanishes at the origin, and $0$ is a root of $W_ k(0,\cdot )$ of some multiplicity $\mu _ k\geq 1$. By lemma A.1.3 in the variables $(z',T)$ at the origin, $W_ k=V_ kQ_ k$, where $V_ k$ is a Weierstrass polynomial in $T$ of degree $\mu _ k$ and $Q_ k$ is a polynomial in $T$ with $Q_ k(0,0)\neq 0$. Substituting $T=z_ k$, which vanishes at the origin, $Q_ k(z',z_ k)$ becomes a unit of $\mathcal O_ n$, so $V_ k(z',z_ k)\in J$. By minimality $\deg V_ k\geq \deg W_ k$, hence $W_ k=V_ k$. The same argument applies to $W$ and $u$.

Let $G\subset J$ be the ideal generated by $W(z',u)$ and the germs $\delta z_ k-B_ k(z',u)$. We shall use the following divisibility property several times: if $H\in \mathcal O_ d[T]$ and $H(z',u)\in J$, then $H$ is divisible by $W$ in $\mathcal O_ d[T]$; in particular, $H(z',u)\in G$, and $H=0$ if $\deg H<q$. Indeed, $H$ vanishes at the class of $u$ in $\operatorname {Frac}(B)$, and so does the remainder of its Euclidean division by the monic polynomial $W$; this remainder has degree less than $q$, so it is zero, because $1,u,\ldots ,u^{q-1}$ are linearly independent over $K$.

We claim that, with $m=\max \{ q,(n-d)(q-1)\} $, every germ $f\in \mathcal O_ n$ satisfies

A.6
\begin{equation} \delta ^ m f\equiv R_ f(z',u)\pmod G, \qquad f\in J\Longrightarrow R_ f=0, \end{equation}

for a unique polynomial $R_ f(z',T)\in \mathcal O_ d[T]$ of degree less than $q$. To prove this, divide $f$ successively by the Weierstrass polynomials $W_ n(z',z_ n),\ldots ,W_{d+1}(z',z_{d+1})$, whose coefficients depend only on $z'$. This writes $f$ as a polynomial $\sum _\alpha e_\alpha (z')z''^\alpha $ with $\alpha _ k\leq q-1$ for all $k$, plus multiples of the $W_ k(z',z_ k)$. Multiplying by $\delta ^ m$ and substituting $\delta z_ k\equiv B_ k(z',u)$ modulo $G$, which is possible because $|\alpha |\leq (n-d)(q-1)\leq m$, we obtain a polynomial in $u$ with coefficients in $\mathcal O_ d$; dividing it by $W$ gives $R_ f$. The discarded multiples of $W_ k(z',z_ k)$ disappear modulo $G$, because $m\geq q$ and $\delta ^ qW_ k(z',z_ k)\in G$. Indeed, the polynomial

\[ H_ k(z',T)=\delta ^ qW_ k\bigl (z',B_ k(z',T)/\delta \bigr ) \]

has coefficients in $\mathcal O_ d$, since $\deg W_ k\leq q$. The same substitution gives $\delta ^ qW_ k(z',z_ k)\equiv H_ k(z',u)$ modulo $G$; hence $H_ k(z',u)\in J$, and by the divisibility property $H_ k$ is divisible by $W$ and $H_ k(z',u)\in G$. Two admissible polynomials $R_ f$ differ by a polynomial $H$ of degree less than $q$ with $H(z',u)\in G\subset J$, so they agree, by the divisibility property. If $f\in J$, then $R_ f(z',u)\in J$, and $R_ f=0$ for the same reason.

Step 3: the covering off the discriminant. Choose polydiscs as in Step 1 so small that all the germs above and the generators of $J$ are holomorphic near the closure of $\Delta '\times \Delta ''$, and such that the following identities between germs hold there: (A.6) for the generators of $J$, the expressions of the generators of $G$ as combinations of the generators of $J$, and the divisions of $H_ k$ and of $\sum _ kc_ kB_ k(z',T)-\delta T$ by $W$ used below. Let $z'\in \Delta '$ with $\delta (z')\neq 0$. Then $W(z',\cdot )$ has $q$ distinct roots $\tau _1,\ldots ,\tau _ q$, by (A.5), and the points of $V(G)$ over $z'$ are exactly the $q$ points

\[ W(z',T)=0,\qquad z_ k=B_ k(z',T)/\delta (z'),\qquad T=\tau _1,\ldots ,\tau _ q. \]

Indeed, a point of $V(G)$ satisfies these equations with $T=u(z'')$. Conversely, the polynomial $\sum _ kc_ kB_ k(z',T)-\delta T$ takes at $T=u$ a value congruent to $\delta u-\delta u=0$ modulo $J$, so it is divisible by $W$, by the divisibility property of Step 2, and vanishes at each $\tau _ i$; hence the point defined by $\tau _ i$ satisfies $u(z'')=\tau _ i$ and lies in $V(G)$. These $q$ points are distinct, because their values of $u$ are distinct.

The simple roots $\tau _ i$, and hence the points, depend holomorphically on $z'$ locally: if $\tau $ is a simple root of $W(z'_1,\cdot )$, the map $(z',T)\mapsto (z',W(z',T))$ has invertible derivative at $(z'_1,\tau )$, and its holomorphic local inverse (proposition 7.1.2(1)) yields a holomorphic function $\tau (z')$ near $z'_1$ with $W(z',\tau (z'))=0$ and $\tau (z'_1)=\tau $. Since $H_ k$ is divisible by $W$, we have $\delta (z')^ qW_ k(z',B_ k(z',\tau _ i)/\delta (z'))=H_ k(z',\tau _ i)=0$, so the coordinate $z_ k=B_ k(z',\tau _ i)/\delta (z')$ is a root of the Weierstrass polynomial $W_ k(z',\cdot )$; by (A.1) it is small when $z'$ is small. Shrinking $\Delta '$, we may therefore assume that these points lie in $\Delta '\times \Delta ''$. They belong to $V(J)$, since $\delta ^ mf\in G$ for every generator $f$ of $J$ by (A.6), and $\delta (z')\neq 0$. As $G\subset J$, we conclude that over $\Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $ the set $V(J)\cap (\Delta '\times \Delta '')$ is the union of the $q$ disjoint local holomorphic graphs just described, on which $u$ takes the values $\tau _1,\ldots ,\tau _ q$; this gives the assertion about $u$ and $W$, which persists when the polydiscs are shrunk later. Thus $A_\delta $ is a complex submanifold of dimension $d$, open in $V(J)\cap (\Delta '\times \Delta '')$, and a $q$-sheeted covering of $\Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $; this base is connected by lemma A.1.7.

Now let $f\in \mathcal O_ n$ vanish on $V(J)$. By (A.6), the polynomial $R_ f(z',\cdot )$ vanishes at the $q$ distinct roots $\tau _ i$ for every $z'$ with $\delta (z')\neq 0$ near $0$, because $\delta ^ mf$ and the elements of $G$ vanish at the corresponding points of $V(J)$. Its degree is less than $q$, so $R_ f=0$. Hence $\delta ^ mf\in G\subset J$, and since $\delta \notin J$ and $J$ is prime, $f\in J$. This proves $I_{V(J),0}=J$.

Step 4: density of the covering locus and the bound on the fibers. For a complex linear form $\lambda $ on $\mathbb C^{n-d}$ and $z'\in \Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $, put

\[ P_\lambda (z',T)=\prod _{z\in \pi ^{-1}(z')\cap A_\delta } (T-\lambda (z'')). \]

Its coefficients are elementary symmetric functions of the values of $\lambda $ on the sheets, hence holomorphic and bounded on $\Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $; by lemma A.1.7 they extend holomorphically to $\Delta '$. The function $\delta (z')P_\lambda (z',\lambda (z''))$ vanishes on $V(J)\cap (\Delta '\times \Delta '')$: on $A_\delta $ the second factor vanishes, and elsewhere the first. By Step 3 its germ belongs to $J$, and since $\delta \notin J$, the germ of $P_\lambda (z',\lambda (z''))$ belongs to $J$; so this function vanishes on $V(J)$ near $0$.

Let $z'_0\in \Delta '$ and let $F$ be the fiber over $z'_0$ of the closure $\overline{A_\delta }$ of $A_\delta $ in $\Delta '\times \Delta ''$. We claim that the roots of $P_\lambda (z'_0,\cdot )$ are exactly the values $\lambda (y'')$, $y\in F$. Indeed, every $y\in F$ is a limit of points $y_ j\in A_\delta $ with $\pi (y_ j)\to z'_0$, and $\lambda (y_ j'')$ is a root of $P_\lambda (\pi (y_ j),\cdot )$; since the coefficients of $P_\lambda $ are continuous, $\lambda (y'')$ is a root of $P_\lambda (z'_0,\cdot )$. Conversely, choose $z'_ j\to z'_0$ with $\delta (z'_ j)\neq 0$. The $q$ points of $A_\delta $ over $z'_ j$ lie in a compact subset of $\Delta '\times \Delta ''$ by Step 1, so after passing to a subsequence they converge to points of $F$; their values of $\lambda $, which are the $q$ roots of $P_\lambda (z'_ j,\cdot )$, converge to the $q$ roots of $P_\lambda (z'_0,\cdot )$, counted with multiplicity. It follows that $F$ has at most $q$ points: a linear form taking distinct values at $q+1$ points of $F$ would give $q+1$ distinct roots of a polynomial of degree $q$.

It remains to show that $V(J)$ is contained in $\overline{A_\delta }$ near $0$; after shrinking the polydiscs once more, this gives the density of $A_\delta $ and the bound $q$ for all fibers. Suppose not. Then there are points $y_ j\in V(J)\setminus \overline{A_\delta }$ with $y_ j\to 0$. For each $j$, the forms $\lambda $ with $\lambda (y_ j'')=\lambda (w'')$ for some point $w$ of the finite fiber $F_ j$ of $\overline{A_\delta }$ over $\pi (y_ j)$ form finitely many proper linear subspaces. We choose one form $\lambda $ outside all these countably many subspaces: for instance, $\lambda $ with coefficients $(1,t,\ldots ,t^{n-d-1})$, where $t$ avoids the countably many roots of the nonzero polynomials $\sum _ kv_ kt^{k-1}$ attached to the vectors $v=y_ j''-w''$. Then $\lambda (y_ j'')$ is not a root of $P_\lambda (\pi (y_ j),\cdot )$ for any $j$, by the previous paragraph. On the other hand, $P_\lambda (z',\lambda (z''))$ vanishes on $V(J)$ near $0$, hence at $y_ j$ for all large $j$, a contradiction.

Step 5: connectedness of the covering locus. The covering $A_\delta $ of the connected manifold $\Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $ has finitely many connected components, each of which is itself a covering with a positive number of sheets. Indeed, over a small ball $N\subset \Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $ the set $A_\delta $ is the disjoint union of $q$ connected holomorphic graphs, each of which lies in a single connected component of $A_\delta $. Hence the number of points of a given component over $z'$ is locally constant, hence constant on the connected set $\Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $, and it is positive because the component is not empty.

Suppose that there are at least two components. For each component $A^{(i)}$ and each linear form $\lambda $, the product $P^{(i)}_\lambda $ of the factors $T-\lambda (z'')$ over the points of $A^{(i)}$ again has bounded holomorphic coefficients on $\Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $, which extend to $\Delta '$, and $P_\lambda =\prod _ iP^{(i)}_\lambda $. Choose points $z'_ j\to 0$ with $\delta (z'_ j)\neq 0$, and, by the countable choice of Step 4, a form $\lambda $ that takes distinct values at the $q$ points of $A_\delta $ over each $z'_ j$. Since the germ of $P_\lambda (z',\lambda (z''))$ belongs to the prime ideal $J$, so does the germ of some factor $P^{(i)}_\lambda (z',\lambda (z''))$, which then vanishes on $V(J)$ near $0$. Choose $k\neq i$. For large $j$ the points of $A^{(k)}$ over $z'_ j$ are close to $0$, by Step 1, so $P^{(i)}_\lambda (z'_ j,\cdot )$ vanishes at their values of $\lambda $; but these values differ from the roots of $P^{(i)}_\lambda (z'_ j,\cdot )$, which are the values of $\lambda $ at the points of $A^{(i)}$ over $z'_ j$. This contradiction shows that $A_\delta $ is connected, and completes the proof. $\square$

We record a consequence that will be used repeatedly.

Corollary A.2.4. Let $(A,0)\subset (\mathbb C^ n,0)$ be an irreducible analytic germ, and let $(B,0)$ be an analytic subgerm different from $(A,0)$. Let $\Delta '\times \Delta ''$ be a parametrization polydisc of $(A,0)$ as in theorem A.2.3, with its representative $A$ and covering locus $A_\delta $, so small that $(B,0)$ has a representative $B$ which is an analytic subset of $\Delta '\times \Delta ''$. Then $A_\delta \setminus B$ is dense in $A\cap (\Delta '\times \Delta '')$. In particular, $(A\cap (\Delta '\times \Delta ''))\setminus B$ is dense in $A\cap (\Delta '\times \Delta '')$, and so is the regular locus of $A\cap (\Delta '\times \Delta '')$, which contains $A_\delta $.

Proof. The set $B\cap A_\delta $ is an analytic subset of the connected manifold $A_\delta $. It is a proper subset, for otherwise the closed set $B$ would contain the closure $A\cap (\Delta '\times \Delta '')$ of $A_\delta $, and the two germs would agree. By lemma A.1.6, $A_\delta \setminus B$ is dense in $A_\delta $, and hence in $A\cap (\Delta '\times \Delta '')$, in which $A_\delta $ is dense. $\square$

A first consequence is that the closure of the difference of two analytic sets is analytic. We shall use this for closures of graphs, in the constructions of normalizations and blowups.

Lemma A.2.5. Let $Z$ and $Y$ be analytic subsets of a complex manifold $M$. Then the closure $\overline{Z\setminus Y}$ is analytic. More precisely, near each point $p\in Z$ it is the union of representatives of those branches of $(Z,p)$ that are not contained in $(Y,p)$.

Proof. Let $p\in Z$; near points outside $Z$ there is nothing to prove, since $\overline{Z\setminus Y}\subset Z$. Choose representatives $Z_1,\ldots ,Z_ k$ of the branches of $(Z,p)$ on an open neighborhood $U$ of $p$ with $Z\cap U=\bigcup _ iZ_ i$, so small that $Z_ i\subset Y$ whenever $(Z_ i,p)\subset (Y,p)$, and that, for each of the other branches, $Z_ i\setminus Y$ is dense in $Z_ i$; the latter is possible by corollary A.2.4, applied to $(Z_ i,p)$ and its subgerm $(Z_ i\cap Y,p)$, which differs from $(Z_ i,p)$. Then $(Z\setminus Y)\cap U$ is the union of the sets $Z_ i\setminus Y$ over the branches not contained in $(Y,p)$, and its closure in $U$ is the union of the corresponding sets $Z_ i$, which is analytic in $U$. $\square$

The same holds for analytic subsets of a complex space, as we see by applying the lemma in local models (section A.3).

Corollary A.2.6 (Analytic Nullstellensatz). For every ideal $I\subset \mathcal O_ n$, the ideal of germs vanishing on $V(I)$ is the radical $\sqrt I=\{ f:f^ k\in I\text{ for some }k\ge 1\} $.

Proof. Every element of $\sqrt I$ vanishes on $V(I)$. For the converse we first show that $\sqrt I$ is the intersection of the prime ideals containing $I$. A prime ideal containing $I$ contains $\sqrt I$. Let $f\notin \sqrt I$, so that the ideals containing $I$ and disjoint from $S=\{ 1,f,f^2,\ldots \} $ form a nonempty family; by proposition A.1.2 it has a maximal element $\mathfrak p$, since otherwise there would be a strictly increasing infinite sequence of ideals. The ideal $\mathfrak p$ is prime: if $a,b\notin \mathfrak p$, then $\mathfrak p+(a)$ and $\mathfrak p+(b)$ meet $S$, say $f^ k=p_1+xa$ and $f^ l=p_2+yb$ with $p_1,p_2\in \mathfrak p$; multiplying, we see that $ab\in \mathfrak p$ would give $f^{k+l}\in \mathfrak p$, which is impossible. Thus $f$ lies outside some prime ideal containing $I$. Now let $g$ vanish on $V(I)$. For every prime ideal $J\supset I$ we have $V(J)\subset V(I)$, so $g$ vanishes on $V(J)$ and therefore $g\in J$, by the equality $I_{V(J),0}=J$ proved in theorem A.2.3. Hence $g$ lies in the intersection of all these prime ideals, which is $\sqrt I$. $\square$

Proposition A.2.7. Let $(A,0)$ be an irreducible analytic germ. The regular points of every sufficiently small representative of $(A,0)$ all have the same dimension $d$, equal to the base dimension in theorem A.2.3; we call $d$ the dimension of $(A,0)$. Every sufficiently small representative is pure-dimensional: all its local irreducible branches, at all of its points, have dimension $d$. A proper analytic subgerm of an irreducible germ has strictly smaller dimension. For a reducible germ we define the dimension as the maximum of the dimensions of its branches.

Proof. Let $A$ be the representative on a parametrization polydisc. Two local parametrizations of $(A,0)$ have covering loci which are open and dense in $A$ near $0$; their intersection is therefore nonempty, and at its points $A$ is a manifold whose dimension equals both base dimensions. Hence $d$ is determined by the germ. A regular point of $A$ has a neighborhood in $A$ which is a manifold; this neighborhood meets the dense covering locus, which has dimension $d$, so the regular point has dimension $d$ as well. Now let $y\in A$, let $B_1,\ldots ,B_ p$ be representatives of the branches of $(A,y)$, and fix $i$. Since $B_ i$ is not contained in $\bigcup _{k\neq i}B_ k$, corollary A.2.4 gives points of the covering locus of a parametrization of $B_ i$ outside the other branches. Near such a point, $A$ coincides with $B_ i$ and is a manifold of dimension $\dim B_ i$; such points form a nonempty open subset of $A$, which meets the covering locus of $A$. Hence $\dim B_ i=d$.

Let $(B,0)\subset (A,0)$ be germs with both irreducible, and let $J_ A\subset J_ B$ be their prime ideals. For linear coordinates $z$ and $0\leq k\leq n$, let $\mathcal O_ k$ denote the ring of germs depending only on $z_1,\ldots ,z_ k$. For a nonzero prime ideal $J$, Step 1 of the proof of theorem A.2.3 chooses the coordinates downward from $k=n$. As long as $J\cap \mathcal O_ k\neq 0$, it makes a linear change of $z_1,\ldots ,z_ k$ in which a chosen nonzero element of $J\cap \mathcal O_ k$ is distinguished in $z_ k$; it stops at the largest $d$ with $J\cap \mathcal O_ d=0$, which is the base dimension. The rest of that proof applies to every such choice. Since finitely many nonzero germs become distinguished in the last coordinate after one suitable linear change of coordinates, we may make these changes for $J_ B$ and, at the stages $k$ with $J_ A\cap \mathcal O_ k\neq 0$, simultaneously for $J_ A$. This gives parametrizations of both germs in common coordinates (if $J_ A=0$, the germ $(A,0)$ is the germ of $\mathbb C^ n$, with $d=n$). Since $J_ A\subset J_ B$, the ideal $J_ B\cap \mathcal O_ k$ is nonzero whenever $J_ A\cap \mathcal O_ k$ is, so $\dim B\leq \dim A$.

Suppose that the dimensions are equal, so that both germs are finite over the same base, and let $\delta _ A$, $\delta _ B$ be the two discriminants. By lemma A.1.7, the set $A'$ of points of the connected covering locus of $A$ lying over $\{ \delta _ B\neq 0\} $ is a connected manifold of dimension $d$, dense in $A$. The points of $B$ over $\{ \delta _ A\delta _ B\neq 0\} $ form a nonempty submanifold of $A'$ of the same dimension, hence an open subset of $A'$, since the inclusion is an injective immersion between manifolds of the same dimension and therefore open by the inverse function theorem. Thus $B\cap A'$ is an analytic subset of the connected manifold $A'$ with nonempty interior; since proper analytic subsets of connected manifolds are nowhere dense (lemma A.1.6), $B\supset A'$. Since $B$ is closed and $A'$ is dense in $A$, we get $(B,0)=(A,0)$. Hence a proper subgerm has strictly smaller dimension. For a general proper subgerm $B$ of $(A,0)$, each branch of $B$ is a proper subgerm of $(A,0)$, and the same conclusion follows. $\square$

The dimension of an analytic set $E$ at a point $x$ is the dimension of its germ $(E,x)$. Dimension is monotone: if $(E,x)\subset (F,x)$, every branch of $(E,x)$ is contained in a branch of $(F,x)$, so $\dim (E,x)\leq \dim (F,x)$. It is also upper semicontinuous: since small representatives of the branches of $(E,x)$ are pure-dimensional, every branch of $(E,y)$, for $y$ near $x$, is contained in one of them and has dimension at most $\dim (E,x)$. We say that $E$ is pure-dimensional if its dimension is the same at all points, and that $E\subset M$ has codimension at least $k$, respectively codimension $k$, if $\dim (E,x)\leq \dim _ xM-k$, respectively $\dim (E,x)=\dim _ xM-k$, for all $x\in E$.

Theorem A.2.8 (Cartan's coherence theorem). For an analytic subset $A$ of a complex manifold $M$, the sheaf $\mathcal I_ A$ of holomorphic functions vanishing on $A$ is coherent.

Proof. The assertion is local. Near a point of $A$, the set $A$ is the union of representatives $A_1,\ldots ,A_ p$ of the branches of its germ, and $\mathcal I_ A=\bigcap _ i\mathcal I_{A_ i}$. The intersection of two coherent subsheaves $\mathcal F,\mathcal G$ of $\mathcal O_ M$ is the kernel of the morphism $\mathcal F\oplus \mathcal G\to \mathcal O_ M$, $(a,b)\mapsto a-b$, and is coherent by theorem A.1.5, lemma A.1.4. It therefore suffices to prove that $\mathcal I_ A$ is coherent near $0$ when $(A,0)\subset (\mathbb C^ n,0)$ is irreducible. We use the parametrization of theorem A.2.3, with its primitive linear form $u$, its Weierstrass polynomial $W$ and $\delta =\operatorname {Disc}(W)$, and we put $m=\max \{ q,(n-d)(q-1)\} $. Since $A$ may have several branches at points near $0$, we describe all stalks near $0$ at once: we construct holomorphic functions $G_1,\ldots ,G_ N$ vanishing on $A$ and a holomorphic function $h\not\equiv 0$ of $z'$ such that, at every point $x$ near $0$, the stalk $\mathcal I_{A,x}$ consists of the germs $f$ with $hf\in (G_{1,x},\ldots ,G_{N,x})$; such a sheaf is coherent by Oka's theorem.

Step 1: dependence on the primitive form. For $c=(c_{d+1},\ldots ,c_ n)\in \mathbb C^{n-d}$ put $u_ c=\sum _ kc_ kz_ k$. For $z'\in \Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $ let $z^{(1)},\ldots ,z^{(q)}$ be the points of $A$ over $z'$, and define

\begin{gather*} W_ c(z',T)=\prod _{i=1}^ q\bigl (T-u_ c(z^{(i)})\bigr ),\qquad \delta _ c(z')=\prod _{i<l}\bigl (u_ c(z^{(i)})-u_ c(z^{(l)})\bigr )^2,\\ B_{kc}(z',T)=\sum _{j<q}D_ c(z')D_{kcj}(z')T^ j, \end{gather*}

where $D_ c$ is the Vandermonde determinant $\det (u_ c(z^{(i)})^ j)_{i,j}$ and $D_{kcj}$ is obtained from it by replacing the $j$th column with the coordinates $z_ k^{(i)}$. These expressions do not change under permutations of the points $z^{(i)}$, since $D_ c$ and $D_{kcj}$ both change sign under a transposition. Hence their coefficients in $T$ are polynomials in $c$ whose coefficients are bounded holomorphic functions on $\Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $, and these extend holomorphically to $\Delta '$ by lemma A.1.7. On $A_\delta $ we have $W_ c(z',u_ c(z''))=0$ and, by Cramer's rule in the form $\sum _ jD_{kcj}u_ c(z^{(i)})^ j=D_ cz_ k^{(i)}$, also $\delta _ c(z')z_ k=B_{kc}(z',u_ c(z''))$, for every $c$. Let $G_1,\ldots ,G_ N$ be the coefficients, as polynomials in $c$, of the functions $W_ c(z',u_ c)$ and $\delta _ cz_ k-B_{kc}(z',u_ c)$, $d<k\leq n$. They are holomorphic on $\Delta '\times \Delta ''$ and vanish on $A_\delta $, hence on $A$, which is the closure of $A_\delta $. For the coefficient vector $c$ of the primitive linear form $u$, the values $u(z^{(i)})$ are the roots of $W(z',\cdot )$, by theorem A.2.3, so $\delta _ c=\operatorname {Disc}(W)=\delta $ by (A.5), and $\delta _ c\neq 0$ off $\{ \delta =0\} $; so the polynomial $\delta _ c^ m=\sum _\alpha h_\alpha (z')c^\alpha $ in $c$ is not identically zero, and we choose a coefficient $h=h_\alpha \not\equiv 0$.

Step 2: a description of the stalks. We claim that on a small polydisc around $0$,

A.7
\begin{equation} \mathcal I_{A,x}=\{ f\in \mathcal O_{M,x}:h f\in (G_{1,x},\ldots ,G_{N,x})\} \end{equation}

for every point $x$. If $hf\in (G_{1,x},\ldots ,G_{N,x})$, then $hf$ vanishes on $A$ near $x$, so $f$ vanishes on $A\setminus \{ h=0\} $ near $x$. This set is dense in $A$: the covering locus $A_\delta $ is dense in $A$, and since $\pi $ is a local biholomorphism on $A_\delta $, the set where $h\circ \pi $ vanishes is nowhere dense in $A_\delta $. Hence $f\in \mathcal I_{A,x}$, which proves the inclusion from right to left.

Step 3: the inclusion from left to right at points of $A$. Fix $x\in A$ and $f\in \mathcal I_{A,x}$. Call $c$ admissible if $\delta _ c\not\equiv 0$ and $u_ c$ takes distinct values at the points of the finite fiber of $A$ over $x'$; the admissible $c$ form a dense open subset of $\mathbb C^{n-d}$, the complement of an algebraic subset and finitely many proper linear subspaces. Fix an admissible $c$. Since $W_ c(z',u_ c(z''))$ vanishes on $A$ by Step 1, the number $u_ c(x'')$ is a root of $W_ c(x',\cdot )$. By lemma A.1.3, $W_ c=W_ c^ xQ_ c^ x$ near $(x',u_ c(x''))$, where $W^ x_ c$ is a Weierstrass polynomial in $T-u_ c(x'')$ and $Q^ x_ c$ is a polynomial in $T$ with $Q^ x_ c(x',u_ c(x''))\neq 0$.

We call $z'$ generic if $\delta (z')\delta _ c(z')\neq 0$. For generic $z'$ near $x'$, the roots of $W^ x_ c(z',\cdot )$ are distinct and are exactly the values of $u_ c$ at the points of $A$ over $z'$ that are close to $x$; let $q_ x$ be their number, which is the degree of $W^ x_ c$. Indeed, the roots of $W_ c(z',\cdot )$ are the $q$ distinct values of $u_ c$ at the points of $A$ over $z'$, and by properness these points are close to the points of the fiber over $x'$. The values of $u_ c$ at the points close to $x$ are close to $u_ c(x'')$, where $Q^ x_ c(z',\cdot )$ does not vanish, so they are roots of $W^ x_ c(z',\cdot )$. The values at the points close to the other points of the fiber are close to values of $u_ c$ different from $u_ c(x'')$, since $c$ is admissible, whereas the roots of the Weierstrass polynomial $W^ x_ c(z',\cdot )$ in $T-u_ c(x'')$ tend to $u_ c(x'')$ as $z'\to x'$, by (A.1).

Let $\mathfrak g_ c\subset \mathcal O_{M,x}$ be the ideal generated by the germs at $x$ of $W_ c(z',u_ c)$ and $\delta _ cz_ k-B_{kc}(z',u_ c)$, $d<k\leq n$. These functions vanish on $A$, and they are complex linear combinations of $G_1,\ldots ,G_ N$, so $\mathfrak g_ c\subset (G_{1,x},\ldots ,G_{N,x})$. The germ of $W^ x_ c(z',u_ c)$ belongs to $\mathfrak g_ c$, because it equals $Q^ x_ c(z',u_ c)^{-1}W_ c(z',u_ c)$ and $Q^ x_ c(z',u_ c)$ is a unit at $x$. We use the following divisibility property: if $H(z',T)$ is a polynomial in $T$ whose coefficients are germs at $x'$, and $H(y',u_ c(y''))=0$ at all points $y\in A$ close to $x$ with $y'$ generic, then the germ of $H(z',u_ c)$ belongs to $\mathfrak g_ c$. Indeed, the remainder of the division of $H$ by the monic polynomial $W^ x_ c$ has degree less than $q_ x$, and for generic $z'$ near $x'$ it vanishes at the $q_ x$ distinct roots of $W^ x_ c(z',\cdot )$; so it is zero, and $H(z',u_ c)$ is a multiple of $W^ x_ c(z',u_ c)$.

For $d<k\leq n$ and the unit vector $e_ k$ we have $u_{e_ k}=z_ k$, so by Step 1 the coordinate $z_ k$ of each point of $A$ is a root of the monic polynomial $W_{e_ k}(z',\cdot )$ of degree $q$. Let $W^ x_ k$ be the Weierstrass polynomial in $T-x_ k$ given by lemma A.1.3 for $W_{e_ k}$ at the root $x_ k$ of $W_{e_ k}(x',\cdot )$, so that $W_{e_ k}=W^ x_ kQ^ x_ k$ near $(x',x_ k)$ with $Q^ x_ k(x',x_ k)\neq 0$. Then $W^ x_ k(z',z_ k)$ vanishes at the points of $A$ close to $x$, and its degree $q_ k$ is at most $q$. Substituting $\delta _ cz_ k\equiv B_{kc}(z',u_ c)$ modulo $\mathfrak g_ c$, we get $\delta _ c^{q_ k}W^ x_ k(z',z_ k)\equiv H_ k(z',u_ c)$ modulo $\mathfrak g_ c$, where

\[ H_ k(z',T)=\delta _ c^{q_ k}W^ x_ k\bigl (z',B_{kc}(z',T)/\delta _ c\bigr ) \]

is a polynomial in $T$. At a point $y\in A$ close to $x$ with $y'$ generic, Step 1 gives $B_{kc}(y',u_ c(y''))=\delta _ c(y')y_ k$, so $H_ k(y',u_ c(y''))=\delta _ c(y')^{q_ k}W^ x_ k(y',y_ k)=0$. By the divisibility property, $H_ k(z',u_ c)\in \mathfrak g_ c$; hence $\delta _ c^ qW^ x_ k(z',z_ k)\in \mathfrak g_ c$.

Now divide $f$ successively by the Weierstrass polynomials $W^ x_ k(z',z_ k)$ at $x$, for $k=n,\ldots ,d+1$ (proposition A.1.1, in coordinates centered at $x$). This writes $f$ as a polynomial $\sum _\alpha e_\alpha (z')z''^\alpha $, with germs $e_\alpha $ at $x'$ and $\alpha _ k<q_ k\leq q$ for all $k$, plus multiples of the $W^ x_ k(z',z_ k)$. After multiplication by $\delta _ c^ m$, these multiples lie in $\mathfrak g_ c$, because $m\geq q$. Substituting $\delta _ cz_ k\equiv B_{kc}(z',u_ c)$, which is possible because $|\alpha |\leq (n-d)(q-1)\leq m$, we obtain $\delta _ c^ mf\equiv H(z',u_ c)$ modulo $\mathfrak g_ c$ for a polynomial $H(z',T)$ whose coefficients are germs at $x'$. Since $f$ and the elements of $\mathfrak g_ c$ vanish on $A$ near $x$, the divisibility property applies to $H$, and we conclude that

\[ \delta _ c^ m f\in \mathfrak g_ c\subset (G_{1,x},\ldots ,G_{N,x})\qquad \text{for every admissible }c. \]

The polynomials in $c$ of degree at most $\deg _ c\delta _ c^ m$ form a finite-dimensional space, and the evaluations at points of the dense open set of admissible $c$ span its dual, since a polynomial vanishing on a nonempty open set is zero. Hence each coefficient $h_\alpha $ of $\delta _ c^ m$ is a complex linear combination of finitely many values $\delta _{c^{(l)}}^ m$ with admissible $c^{(l)}$, and $h_\alpha f\in (G_{1,x},\ldots ,G_{N,x})$. This proves (A.7) at points of $A$.

Step 4: points outside $A$, and conclusion. Let $x\notin A$. Choose $c$ such that $u_ c(x'')$ differs from the values of $u_ c$ at the finitely many points of $A$ over $x'$. The roots of $W_ c(x',\cdot )$ are values of $u_ c$ at points of $A$ over $x'$. Indeed, choose $z'_ j\to x'$ with $\delta (z'_ j)\neq 0$; by properness, after passing to a subsequence, the $q$ points $z^{(1)}_ j,\ldots ,z^{(q)}_ j$ of $A$ over $z'_ j$ converge to points $y^{(1)},\ldots ,y^{(q)}$ of $A$ over $x'$, so that $W_ c(x',T)=\lim _ j\prod _ i(T-u_ c(z^{(i)}_ j))=\prod _ i(T-u_ c(y^{(i)}))$. Hence $W_ c(x',u_ c(x''))\neq 0$, so one of the coefficients $G_ l$ does not vanish at $x$, and both sides of (A.7) are the unit ideal. The right side of (A.7) is the projection to the first coordinate of the kernel of the row matrix $(h,-G_1,\ldots ,-G_ N)$, which is coherent by theorem A.1.5, lemma A.1.4. Hence $\mathcal I_ A$ is coherent. $\square$

To study the singular locus, we need the ideal of a submanifold.

Lemma A.2.9. Let $M$ be a complex manifold, let $(v,w)$, $w=(w_1,\ldots ,w_ k)$ with $k\geq 0$, be holomorphic coordinates on an open set $U\subset M$, and let $N=\{ w_1=\cdots =w_ k=0\} \subset U$. Then for every $y\in U$ the ideal $I_{N,y}$ of germs vanishing on $(N,y)$ is generated by the germs of $w_1,\ldots ,w_ k$; it is the unit ideal if $y\notin N$. Moreover, for $y\in N$ the germ $(N,y)$ is irreducible, and its dimension is $\dim _ yM-k$, the dimension of $N$ at $y$.

Proof. If $y\in U\setminus N$, then some $w_ l$ does not vanish at $y$, so it is a unit, and $(N,y)$ is empty because $N$ is closed in $U$; both ideals are the unit ideal. Let $y\in N$, and let $g$ be holomorphic on a polydisc centered at $y$ in the coordinates $(v,w)$, vanishing on $N$. Then $g(v,0)=0$, and

\[ g(v,w)=\int _0^1\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}g(v,tw)\, \mathrm dt=\sum _ lw_ l\int _0^1\partial _{w_ l}g(v,tw)\, \mathrm dt, \]

where the integrals are holomorphic by differentiation under the integral sign. Hence the germ of $g$ lies in the ideal generated by $w_1,\ldots ,w_ k$; conversely, the $w_ l$ vanish on $N$. Consequently, the map $g\mapsto g(v,0)$ identifies the quotient of $\mathcal O_{M,y}$ by this ideal with the ring of germs of holomorphic functions of $v$ at $v(y)$, which is an integral domain (section A.1); so $I_{N,y}$ is prime. Since $y\in N$, the germ $(N,y)$ is nonempty, and it is irreducible by proposition A.2.1. All points of $N$ near $y$ are regular points of $N$ of dimension $\dim _ yM-k$, so this is the dimension of $(N,y)$, by proposition A.2.7. $\square$

Corollary A.2.10. Let $A$ be an analytic subset of a complex manifold $M$.

  1. The singular locus $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is an analytic subset, and at every point of $A$ the intersection of $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ with each irreducible branch of $A$ has smaller dimension than that branch. More precisely, if $x\in A$ and $A_1,\ldots ,A_ p$ are sufficiently small representatives of the branches of $(A,x)$, then near $x$

    \[ A_{\mathrm{sing}}=\bigcup _ i(A_ i)_{\mathrm{sing}}\cup \bigcup _{i<j}(A_ i\cap A_ j); \]

    thus a point near $x$ is a regular point of $A$ if and only if it lies on exactly one $A_ i$ and is a regular point of $A_ i$.

  2. Let $A$ have dimension at most $d$ at every point, and define $A^{(0)}=A$ and $A^{(j+1)}=(A^{(j)})_{\mathrm{sing}}$. Then $A^{(d+1)}=\varnothing $, and $A$ is the union of the sets $A^{(j)}_{\mathrm{reg}}$, $0\leq j\leq d$, where $A^{(j)}_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a complex submanifold of dimension at most $d-j$ of the open set $M\setminus A^{(j+1)}$.

  3. With $d$ as in (2), the set $A$ is locally a countable union of real submanifolds of dimensions at most $2d$, and also of images of compact cubes in $\mathbb R^ r$, $r\leq 2d$, under Lipschitz maps. The image of a compact cube in $\mathbb R^ r$ under a Lipschitz map is $\mathcal H^ s$-null, in the sense of section 8.4, for every $s>r$. In particular, in every chart $A$ is locally $\mathcal H^ s$-null for every $s>2d$.

Proof. (1) Let $A$ first be a small representative of an irreducible germ of dimension $e$ in $\mathbb C^ n$, pure-dimensional by proposition A.2.7, and choose generators $g_1,\ldots ,g_ N$ of $\mathcal I_ A$ on a neighborhood, by theorem A.2.8. At a regular point, these generate the ideal of the manifold $A$, which is generated by $n-e$ normal coordinates (lemma A.2.9); differentiating, we see that the matrix of differentials of the $g_ j$ has rank $n-e$ there. Every minor of size $n-e+1$ therefore vanishes on the dense covering locus, hence on $A$, so the rank is at most $n-e$ everywhere on $A$. Conversely, if it equals $n-e$ at $y\in A$, choose $n-e$ of the $g_ j$ with independent differentials at $y$. Together with $e$ suitable coordinate functions they form holomorphic coordinates near $y$, by the holomorphic inverse function theorem (proposition 7.1.2(2)), so their common zero set is an $e$-dimensional submanifold $N$ near $y$, and it contains $A$. Every branch of $(A,y)$ has dimension $e$ and is contained in the germ $(N,y)$, which is irreducible of dimension $e$ by lemma A.2.9, so it equals $(N,y)$ by proposition A.2.7; hence $A$ is a manifold near $y$. Thus $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is the subset of $A$ where all minors of size $n-e$ of this matrix vanish, an analytic subset.

For a general analytic set $A$ near a point $x$, let $A_1,\ldots ,A_ p$ be small representatives of the branches of $(A,x)$. A point $y$ near $x$ lying on only one $A_ i$ is regular for $A$ if and only if it is regular for $A_ i$. A point $y\in A_ i\cap A_ j$, $i\neq j$, is singular. Indeed, otherwise $(A,y)$ would be irreducible, hence equal to some $(A_ k,y)$, and we may assume $k\neq i$; then $(A_ i,y)\subset (A_ i\cap A_ k,y)$. But $\dim (A_ i\cap A_ k,x)<\dim A_ i$ by proposition A.2.7, so $\dim (A_ i\cap A_ k,y)<\dim A_ i=\dim (A_ i,y)$ by upper semicontinuity and purity, contradicting monotonicity. This proves the formula for $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ near $x$, which is therefore analytic by the irreducible case. Finally, let $y\in A$ and let $B$ be a branch of $(A,y)$. By corollary A.2.4, $B$ contains points of the covering locus of a parametrization of $B$ outside the other branches of $(A,y)$, arbitrarily close to $y$, and these are regular points of $A$. Hence the germ of $A_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap B$ at $y$ differs from $B$, and it has smaller dimension than $B$, by proposition A.2.7.

(2) By (1), each $A^{(j)}$ is analytic, and $\dim (A^{(j+1)},x)<\dim (A^{(j)},x)$ at each point $x$ of $A^{(j+1)}$, because every branch of $(A^{(j+1)},x)$ lies in a branch of $(A^{(j)},x)$ and in the singular locus. Hence $A^{(j)}$ has dimension at most $d-j$ at each of its points, and $A^{(d+1)}=\varnothing $, a set of dimension $-1$ being empty. Each $A^{(j)}_{\mathrm{reg}}=A^{(j)}\setminus A^{(j+1)}$ is a complex submanifold of $M\setminus A^{(j+1)}$ of dimension at most $d-j$, and $A$ is the union of these sets.

(3) Each of the submanifolds in (2) is locally a countable union of coordinate charts, so $A$ is locally a countable union of real submanifolds of dimensions at most $2d$. Each such chart is a countable union of images of compact cubes in $\mathbb R^ r$, $r\leq 2d$, under Lipschitz maps, because the image of the chart in $\mathbb R^ r$ is a countable union of compact cubes, on each of which the smooth inverse of the chart is Lipschitz, by the mean value inequality. Such an image is $\mathcal H^ s$-null for $s>r$: covering the cube by $O(\delta ^{-r})$ cubes of side $\delta $, whose images have diameter $O(\delta )$, we cover the image by $O(\delta ^{-r})$ balls of radii $O(\delta )$, and the sum of the $s$th powers of these radii is $O(\delta ^{s-r})$, which tends to zero with $\delta $. A countable union of $\mathcal H^ s$-null sets is $\mathcal H^ s$-null, which completes the proof. $\square$

Hausdorff measures also bound the dimension of images of analytic sets under holomorphic maps (lemma A.2.12), as needed for finite maps in section A.4 and for Remmert's theorem (theorem A.6.2). Such images are controlled by the rank of the differential, through the holomorphic constant rank theorem.

Lemma A.2.11 (Constant rank theorem). Let $F$ be a holomorphic map from an open subset of $\mathbb C^ m$ to $\mathbb C^ K$ whose differential has constant rank $r$ near a point $p$. Then, after a permutation of the coordinates of $\mathbb C^ K$, there are a biholomorphic map $\phi $ from a neighborhood of $p$ onto a polydisc $Q'\times Q''\subset \mathbb C^ r\times \mathbb C^{m-r}$ and a biholomorphic map $\psi $ of $Q'\times \mathbb C^{K-r}$ onto itself such that

\[ F\circ \phi ^{-1}(y',y'')=\psi (y',0)\qquad \text{for }(y',y'')\in Q'\times Q''. \]

In particular, the fibers of $F$ near $p$ are complex submanifolds of dimension $m-r$, and $F$ maps a neighborhood of $p$ onto a closed complex submanifold of dimension $r$ of a neighborhood of $F(p)$.

Proof. After permuting the coordinates of the source and of the target, we may assume that the matrix $(\partial F_ i/\partial x_ j(p))_{i,j\leq r}$ is invertible. The map $\phi (x)=(F_1(x),\ldots ,F_ r(x),x_{r+1},\ldots ,x_ m)$ has invertible derivative at $p$, so by the holomorphic inverse function theorem (proposition 7.1.2(1)) it maps a neighborhood of $p$ biholomorphically onto a polydisc $Q'\times Q''$ centered at $\phi (p)$, on which the rank of $G=F\circ \phi ^{-1}$ is $r$. This map has the form $G(y)=(y',G''(y))$ with $y'=(y_1,\ldots ,y_ r)$ and $G''=(G_{r+1},\ldots ,G_ K)$. For $i,j>r$, the minor of the Jacobian matrix of $G$ formed by the rows $1,\ldots ,r,i$ and the columns $1,\ldots ,r,j$ equals $\partial G_ i/\partial y_ j$, because the first $r$ rows are $(I,0)$; since the rank is $r$, these derivatives vanish. As $Q''$ is convex, $G''(y',y'')=G''(y',c'')$, where $c''$ is the center of $Q''$. The map $\psi (w',w'')=(w',w''+G''(w',c''))$ is a biholomorphism of $Q'\times \mathbb C^{K-r}$ onto itself, with inverse $(w',w'')\mapsto (w',w''-G''(w',c''))$, and $G(y',y'')=\psi (y',0)$. The fibers of $G$ are the slices $\{ y'\} \times Q''$, and its image is $\psi (Q'\times \{ 0\} )$, a closed submanifold of $Q'\times \mathbb C^{K-r}$. $\square$

Lemma A.2.12. Let $N$ be a complex manifold with a countable basis.

  1. If $F\colon N\to \mathbb C^ K$ is a holomorphic map whose differential has rank at most $k$ at every point, then $F(N)$ is $\mathcal H^ s$-null for every $s>2k$.

  2. If an analytic subset $Y$ of an open subset of $\mathbb C^ K$ is $\mathcal H^{2k+2}$-null, then $\dim (Y,y)\leq k$ for every $y\in Y$.

  3. Let $A\subset N$ be an analytic subset with $\dim (A,x)\leq d$ for all $x\in A$, and let $F\colon N\to M$ be a holomorphic map to a complex manifold such that $F(A)$ is an analytic subset of an open subset of $M$. Then $\dim (F(A),w)\leq d$ for every $w\in F(A)$.

Proof. We use the properties of $\mathcal H^ s$-null sets recalled in section 8.4: subsets and countable unions of such sets, and their images under Lipschitz maps, are again $\mathcal H^ s$-null, and an $\mathcal H^ s$-null subset of $\mathbb R^ b$ with $s\leq b$ is Lebesgue null. Moreover, the image of a compact cube in $\mathbb R^ r$ under a Lipschitz map is $\mathcal H^ s$-null for $s>r$, by corollary A.2.10(3), and a $C^1$ map is Lipschitz on every compact convex subset of its domain, by the mean value inequality.

(1) We argue by induction on the dimension of $N$. Since $N$ has at most countably many connected components, each of them open with a countable basis, we may assume that $N$ is connected, of dimension $n$. If $n=0$, then $F(N)$ is a point. Let $n\geq 1$, and let $r\leq k$ be the maximal rank of $\mathrm dF$ on $N$. The set $N_1$ of points where the rank is less than $r$ is the common zero set of the minors of size $r$ of the Jacobian matrix in local coordinates, so it is an analytic subset of $N$ different from $N$. Hence it is nowhere dense, and its germs, being proper subgerms of the irreducible manifold germs of dimension $n$ (lemma A.2.9), have dimension at most $n-1$ by proposition A.2.7. On the open set $N\setminus N_1$ the rank is constantly $r$.

Let $p\in N\setminus N_1$, and choose $\phi $, $\psi $ and $Q'\times Q''$ as in lemma A.2.11, in a chart around $p$. Let $C'\subset Q'$ be a compact cube, a product of closed squares, containing the first $r$ coordinates of $\phi (p)$ in its interior, and let $V=\phi ^{-1}(\operatorname{Int}C'\times Q'')$. Then $F(V)\subset \psi (C'\times \{ 0\} )$, the image of a compact cube in $\mathbb R^{2r}$ under the restriction of $\psi $, which is Lipschitz there because $C'\times \{ 0\} $ is a compact convex subset of the convex domain of $\psi $. Hence $F(V)$ is $\mathcal H^ s$-null for $s>2r$, in particular for $s>2k$. Countably many such sets $V$ cover $N\setminus N_1$, because $N$ has a countable basis. By corollary A.2.10(2), $N_1$ is the union of finitely many complex submanifolds of open subsets of $N$, of dimensions at most $n-1$; they have countable bases, and the restriction of $F$ to each of them has rank at most $k$. By the induction hypothesis their images are $\mathcal H^ s$-null for $s>2k$, and therefore so is $F(N)$.

(2) Suppose that $\dim (Y,y)\geq k+1$ for some $y\in Y$, and let $Y_1$ be a branch of $(Y,y)$ of dimension $e\geq k+1$. By corollary A.2.4, $Y_1$ contains points of the covering locus of a parametrization of $Y_1$ outside the other branches of $(Y,y)$; near such a point $p$, the set $Y$ is a complex submanifold of dimension $e$, so it contains a complex submanifold $P$ of dimension $k+1$ through $p$, for instance a coordinate slice. The orthogonal projection $\Pi $ of $\mathbb C^ K$ onto the tangent space $T_ pP$, a complex subspace of dimension $k+1$, is $1$-Lipschitz, and its restriction to $P$ has invertible derivative at $p$. By the inverse function theorem, $\Pi (P)$ contains a nonempty open subset of $T_ pP\cong \mathbb R^{2k+2}$. On the other hand, $\Pi (P)\subset \Pi (Y)$ is $\mathcal H^{2k+2}$-null and hence Lebesgue null. This contradiction proves (2).

(3) By corollary A.2.10(2), $A$ is the union of finitely many complex submanifolds $A_ l$ of open subsets of $N$, of dimensions at most $d$. Let $(V,\chi )$ be a holomorphic chart of $M$ around a point of $F(A)$, with values in $\mathbb C^ K$. The restriction of $\chi \circ F$ to the open subset $A_ l\cap F^{-1}(V)$ of $A_ l$ has rank at most $\dim A_ l\leq d$, so by (1) the set $\chi (F(A)\cap V)=\bigcup _ l\chi \circ F(A_ l\cap F^{-1}(V))$ is $\mathcal H^{2d+2}$-null. Near each of its points it is an analytic subset of an open subset of $\mathbb C^ K$, so (2) shows that $\dim (F(A),w)\leq d$ for $w\in F(A)\cap V$. $\square$

An analytic subset $Z$ of a complex manifold is irreducible if it is nonempty and is not the union of two analytic subsets different from $Z$.

Corollary A.2.13. Let $A$ be an analytic subset of a complex manifold $M$. The regular locus $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is dense in $A$. The closures of the connected components of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ are irreducible analytic subsets of $A$, called the irreducible components of $A$; they form a locally finite family with union $A$, and none of them is contained in the union of the others. Every locally finite family of irreducible analytic subsets with union $A$, none of which is contained in another, consists of these components. If $M$ has a countable basis, there are at most countably many irreducible components. The regular locus of an irreducible component $Z$ is connected, and a holomorphic function defined near $Z$ that vanishes on a nonempty relatively open subset of $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ vanishes on $Z$.

Proof. Fix $x\in A$, let $A_1,\ldots ,A_ p$ be representatives of the branches of $(A,x)$, and for each $i$ choose a parametrization polydisc $P_ i$ of $(A_ i,x)$ so small that $A\cap P_ i=\bigcup _ j A_ j\cap P_ i$. By corollary A.2.10(1), $A_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap A_ i$ is an analytic subset of $A_ i$ whose germ at $x$ differs from $(A_ i,x)$. Hence $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ meets the connected covering locus of $A_ i$ in a proper analytic subset, and by lemma A.1.7 and corollary A.2.4, the covering locus minus $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is connected and dense in $A_ i\cap P_ i$. The set $R_ i=A_ i\cap P_ i\cap A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ lies between this connected set and its closure, so it is connected and dense in $A_ i\cap P_ i$. In particular $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is dense in $A$.

Let $\{ S_\alpha \} $ be the connected components of the manifold $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and $Z_\alpha =\overline{S_\alpha }$. Each $R_ i$ above is connected, so it lies in a single component $S_{\alpha (i)}$. Let $U=\bigcap _ iP_ i$. Then $S_\alpha \cap U$ is contained in the union of the $A_ i$ with $\alpha (i)=\alpha $, because points of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap A_ i\cap U$ lie in $R_ i$; conversely $A_ i\cap U\subset \overline{R_ i}\subset Z_{\alpha (i)}$. Hence

\[ Z_\alpha \cap U=\bigcup _{i:\, \alpha (i)=\alpha }A_ i\cap U. \]

Thus each $Z_\alpha $ is analytic, only finitely many of them meet $U$, and their union is $A$. Since $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is open in $A$ and its connected components are open in it, $S_\alpha $ is open in $A$. So every point of $S_\alpha $ has a neighborhood that meets no other $S_\beta $, and therefore does not lie in $Z_\beta =\overline{S_\beta }$ for $\beta \neq \alpha $. Thus $S_\alpha \cap Z_\beta =\varnothing $ for $\beta \neq \alpha $, and no $Z_\alpha $ is contained in the union of the others. The same remark shows that $S_\alpha \subset (Z_\alpha )_{\mathrm{reg}}$: near a point of $S_\alpha $, the set $Z_\alpha $ coincides with the manifold $A$. Hence $(Z_\alpha )_{\mathrm{reg}}$ lies between the connected set $S_\alpha $ and its closure, and is connected.

If $Z_\alpha $ were the union of two analytic subsets $Z'$, $Z''$ different from it, then $S_\alpha \cap Z'$ and $S_\alpha \cap Z''$ would be analytic subsets of the connected manifold $S_\alpha $ different from $S_\alpha $, since otherwise their closure would be $Z_\alpha $. As proper analytic subsets of a connected manifold, they would be closed and nowhere dense in $S_\alpha $ (lemma A.1.6), and so would their union, which is $S_\alpha $. This contradiction shows that $Z_\alpha $ is irreducible.

If $M$ has a countable basis, so does $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$; each $S_\alpha $ is open in $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ and contains a basic open set, and disjoint components contain different ones, so there are at most countably many components. Finally, a holomorphic function vanishing on a nonempty open subset of the connected manifold $(Z_\alpha )_{\mathrm{reg}}$ vanishes on all of it by the identity theorem, and hence on its closure $Z_\alpha $.

It remains to prove uniqueness. Let $A=\bigcup _\beta Y_\beta $ be a locally finite decomposition into irreducible analytic subsets, none contained in another. A connected manifold is not a locally finite union of closed nowhere dense subsets, since such a union is closed and nowhere dense. Since proper analytic subsets of the connected manifold $S_\alpha $ are nowhere dense (lemma A.1.6), each $S_\alpha $ lies in some $Y_\beta $, and then $Z_\alpha \subset Y_\beta $. Conversely, fix $\beta $. By what we have proved, applied to the analytic set $Y_\beta $, the set $Y_\beta $ is the union of its irreducible components, and it has only one, for otherwise it would be the union of one of them and of the union of the others, two analytic subsets different from $Y_\beta $. Hence $(Y_\beta )_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a connected manifold, dense in $Y_\beta $ and covered by the locally finite family of closed analytic subsets $(Y_\beta )_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap Z_\alpha $; as before, $Y_\beta \subset Z_\alpha $ for some $\alpha $. Choosing $\beta '$ with $Z_\alpha \subset Y_{\beta '}$, we get $Y_\beta \subset Y_{\beta '}$, so $\beta '=\beta $ and $Y_\beta =Z_\alpha $. Thus every $Y_\beta $ is an irreducible component of $A$; and every component $Z_\alpha $, being contained in some $Y_\beta =Z_{\alpha '}$, equals it, because $S_\alpha \cap Z_{\alpha '}=\varnothing $ for $\alpha '\neq \alpha $. $\square$

Corollary A.2.14. Let $Z$ be an irreducible analytic subset of a complex manifold $M$. Then $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a connected manifold, dense in $Z$, and:

  1. all branches of all germs $(Z,x)$, $x\in Z$, have the dimension of $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$; in particular, $Z$ is pure-dimensional;

  2. every analytic subset $Y\subset Z$ different from $Z$ is nowhere dense in $Z$, and $\dim (Y,x)<\dim Z$ for every $x\in Y$;

  3. if $Z'\supset Z$ is an irreducible analytic subset of $M$ with $\dim Z'=\dim Z$, then $Z'=Z$;

  4. if $Z$ is contained in the union of a locally finite family of analytic subsets of $M$, then it is contained in one of them.

Proof. By the uniqueness in corollary A.2.13, applied to the family consisting of $Z$ alone, $Z$ is its own only irreducible component; so $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is connected and dense in $Z$, and its dimension is constant. (1) Let $B$ be a branch of $(Z,x)$. By corollary A.2.4, $B$ contains points of the covering locus of a parametrization of $B$ outside the other branches of $(Z,x)$. Near such a point, $Z$ coincides with $B$ and is a manifold of dimension $\dim B$, so the point lies in $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and $\dim B=\dim Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$.

(2) The analytic subset $Y\cap Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ of the connected manifold $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ has empty interior in $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$: otherwise it would be all of $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$, by lemma A.1.6, and the closed set $Y$ would contain the closure $Z$ of $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Since $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is open and dense in $Z$, it follows that the closed set $Y$ has empty interior in $Z$. Now let $x\in Y$. Every branch $Y_1$ of $(Y,x)$ is contained in a branch $B$ of $(Z,x)$, and it differs from $B$: otherwise $Y$ would contain the points of $B$ near $x$ outside the other branches of $(Z,x)$, which form a nonempty open subset of $Z$ by corollary A.2.4. By proposition A.2.7 and (1), $\dim Y_1<\dim B=\dim Z$.

(3) Let $e=\dim Z=\dim Z'$. By corollary A.2.10(1) and by (1), $Z'_{\mathrm{sing}}$ has dimension less than $e$ at each of its points, so by (2), applied to $Z'_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap Z\subset Z$, there is a point $p\in Z_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus Z'_{\mathrm{sing}}$. Near $p$, the set $Z$ is a submanifold of dimension $e$ of the manifold $Z'$ of dimension $e$; since the germ of a manifold is irreducible (lemma A.2.9) and proper subgerms of irreducible germs have smaller dimension (proposition A.2.7), $Z$ and $Z'$ coincide near $p$. Thus the analytic subset $Z$ of $Z'$ has interior points in $Z'$, and $Z=Z'$ by (2).

(4) Let $Z\subset \bigcup _\beta Y_\beta $ with $(Y_\beta )$ locally finite. The sets $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap Y_\beta $ are closed analytic subsets of the connected manifold $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ with union $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$. A locally finite union of closed nowhere dense sets is closed and nowhere dense, so one of them has interior points and is therefore all of $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Then $Z\subset Y_\beta $, because $Y_\beta $ is closed and $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is dense in $Z$. $\square$

Corollary A.2.15. Let $A$ be an analytic subset of codimension at least two of a complex manifold $M$. Then every plurisubharmonic function $u$ on $M\setminus A$ is locally bounded above near $A$ and extends uniquely to a plurisubharmonic function on $M$, whose value at $a\in A$ is $\varlimsup _{M\setminus A\ni y\to a}u(y)$.

Proof. The assertion is local, and extensions defined on overlapping charts agree, since their values on $A$ are given by the same formula. Near a point where $M$ has dimension at most one, the codimension condition forces $A=\varnothing $. Otherwise, let $\Omega $ be a connected coordinate chart of dimension $n\geq 2$. Then $\dim A\leq n-2$, and corollary A.2.10(3) shows that $A\cap \Omega $ is locally $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null, since $2n-3>2(n-2)$. By theorem 8.4.3, $u$ extends uniquely to a plurisubharmonic function on $\Omega $ with the stated values on $A$; being upper semicontinuous, the extension is locally bounded above. $\square$

For an irreducible analytic subset $D$ of codimension one of a complex manifold $M$, we write $[D]$ for the current of integration over the complex hypersurface $D_{\mathrm{reg}}$ of $M\setminus D_{\mathrm{sing}}$ (example 7.2.7), with coefficient measures extended by zero across $D_{\mathrm{sing}}$. The following lemma shows that these coefficient measures have locally finite mass near $D_{\mathrm{sing}}$, so that $[D]$ is a well-defined current on $M$, and that $[D]$ is closed and positive.

Lemma A.2.16. Let $D$ be an irreducible analytic subset of codimension one of a complex manifold $M$ of dimension $n$. Every point $x\in D$ has an open neighborhood $V$ with a holomorphic function $f$ on $V$, a local equation of $D$, such that $D\cap V=f^{-1}(0)$, the germ of $f$ generates $\mathcal I_{D,y}$ for every $y\in V$, and $f$ vanishes to order one along $D_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V$: near each point of $D_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V$ it is a unit times a holomorphic coordinate $\tau $ with $D=\{ \tau =0\} $. On $V$ we have $[D]=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log |f|^2$. In particular, $[D]$ is a closed positive $(1,1)$-current on $M$ with measure coefficients.

Proof. By corollary A.2.14, $D_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a connected manifold, dense in $D$, and all branches of $D$ at all points have the dimension $n-1$ of $D_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Let $x\in D$ and let $D_1,\ldots ,D_ k$ be representatives of the branches of $(D,x)$. We construct a generator $f_ i$ of the ideal of each $D_ i$ near $x$, and show that $f=f_1\cdots f_ k$ has the required properties.

Step 1: an equation of one branch. Fix $i$, and take a parametrization of $(D_ i,x)$ as in theorem A.2.3, in coordinates centered at $x$. Its base dimension is $d=n-1$, by proposition A.2.7, so $z''=z_ n$ is a single coordinate. For $z'\in \Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $, the $q$ points of $D_ i$ over $z'$ are distinct, and therefore so are their coordinates $z_ n$; put $P(z',T)=\prod _{z\in \pi ^{-1}(z')}(T-z_ n)$. The coefficients of $P$ are, up to sign, the elementary symmetric functions of these coordinates; they are holomorphic, because the sheets are graphs of holomorphic maps, and bounded, because $\Delta ''$ is bounded, so they extend holomorphically to $\Delta '$ by lemma A.1.7. As $z'\to 0$, the points of $D_ i$ over $z'$ tend to $0$, because $\pi $ is proper and $\pi ^{-1}(0)=\{ 0\} $; hence the extended coefficients vanish at $0$, and $P$ is a Weierstrass polynomial of degree $q$ in $z_ n$. The function $P(z',z_ n)$ vanishes on the covering locus, hence on $D_ i\cap (\Delta '\times \Delta '')$, by density and continuity.

The germ of $P(z',z_ n)$ generates the prime ideal $I_{D_ i,x}$. Indeed, let $g\in I_{D_ i,x}$, and let $R$ be the remainder of the division of $g$ by $P$ (proposition A.1.1), a polynomial in $z_ n$ of degree less than $q$ with coefficients holomorphic near $0$ in $\mathbb C^{n-1}$; it vanishes on $D_ i$ near $0$, since $g$ and $P(z',z_ n)$ do. For $z'$ near $0$ with $\delta (z')\neq 0$, the $q$ points of $D_ i$ over $z'$ are close to $0$, so $R(z',\cdot )$ vanishes at their $q$ distinct coordinates $z_ n$ and is zero. The coefficients of $R$ thus vanish on a dense subset of a neighborhood of $0$, and $R=0$.

Let $f_ i$ be a holomorphic function near $x$ with the germ of $P(z',z_ n)$. The sheaf $\mathcal I_{D_ i}$ has finitely many generators near $x$ by theorem A.2.8, and each of them is a multiple of $f_ i$ at $x$, hence on a neighborhood; so $f_ i$ generates $\mathcal I_{D_ i,y}$ for $y$ near $x$. In particular, at every point near $x$ outside $D_ i$ the ideal of $D_ i$ is the unit ideal, so $f_ i$ does not vanish there, and $f_ i^{-1}(0)=D_ i$ near $x$.

Step 2: the product $f=f_1\cdots f_ k$. Let $y\in D_{\mathrm{reg}}$ be close to $x$. By corollary A.2.10(1), $y$ lies on only one $D_ i$, and $D=D_ i=\{ \tau =0\} $ near $y$ for a local coordinate $\tau $; then $f_ i$ is a unit times $\tau $, since both generate $\mathcal I_{D_ i,y}$ (lemma A.2.9), and $f_ j(y)\neq 0$ for $j\neq i$, by Step 1. Thus $f$ vanishes to order one along $D_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $x$, and $f^{-1}(0)=D$ near $x$. Choose a neighborhood $V$ of $x$ on which all this holds and on which, in addition, $D_ i\setminus \bigcup _{j\neq i}D_ j$ is dense in $D_ i$ for every $i$; this is possible by corollary A.2.4, since the branches are irredundant. Let $y\in V$ and $g\in \mathcal I_{D,y}$; we show by induction on $l$ that $g\in (f_1\cdots f_ l)_ y$. For $l=0$ there is nothing to prove. If $g=f_1\cdots f_ lh$ with $l<k$, then $h$ vanishes near $y$ on $D_{l+1}\setminus (D_1\cup \cdots \cup D_ l)$, where the $f_ i$, $i\leq l$, have no zeros; this set is dense in $D_{l+1}$, so $h$ vanishes on $D_{l+1}$ near $y$, and $h\in (f_{l+1})_ y$. Hence $f$ generates $\mathcal I_{D,y}$.

Finally, in the notation of section 7.4, the regular part of the zero set of $f$ is $D_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V$, with multiplicity one. By theorem 7.4.5, $[D]=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log |f|^2$ on $V$; hence $[D]$ is closed and positive, with measure coefficients of locally finite mass. $\square$

Theorem A.2.17. Let $A$ be a nowhere dense analytic subset of a complex manifold $M$, and let $R$ be a closed real $(1,1)$-current on $M$ with measure coefficients and support in $A$. Then

\[ R=\sum _ D c_ D[D], \]

where $D$ ranges over the irreducible components of $A$ of codimension one, the real coefficients $c_ D$ are uniquely determined, and the sum is locally finite. If $R$ is positive, then every $c_ D\geq 0$. In particular $R=0$ if $A$ has codimension at least two.

Proof. Working on each connected component of $M$ separately, we may assume that $M$ is connected, of dimension $n$.

Step 1: supports of high codimension. Suppose first that, near a point, the support of $R$ lies in a real submanifold $\{ g_1=\cdots =g_ k=0\} $ with independent differentials $\mathrm dg_1,\ldots ,\mathrm dg_ k$, $k\geq 3$. Since $R$ has measure coefficients and $g_ j$ vanishes on its support, $g_ jR=0$; since $R$ is closed, $\mathrm dg_ j\wedge R=\mathrm d(g_ jR)=0$. Take real coordinates $x$ whose first three are $g_1,g_2,g_3$. For $l\leq 3$ and $l\notin \{ a,b\} $, the coefficient of $\mathrm dx_ l\wedge \mathrm dx_ a\wedge \mathrm dx_ b$ in $\mathrm dx_ l\wedge R$ is, up to sign, the coefficient of $\mathrm dx_ a\wedge \mathrm dx_ b$ in $R$, so this coefficient vanishes. Since no pair $\{ a,b\} $ contains all three indices $1,2,3$, every coefficient of $R$ vanishes, and $R=0$ near the point. Now let $A$ have codimension at least two. Near a point of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$, the set $A$ is a complex submanifold of real codimension at least four, so $R$ vanishes there, and the support of $R$ lies in $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$. This is analytic of smaller dimension near each point by corollary A.2.10(1), and again of codimension at least two; repeating the argument, we find after finitely many steps near each point that $R=0$. This proves the last assertion.

Step 2: regular points of codimension one. Let $y\in A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ be a point where $A$ is a complex hypersurface. Choose holomorphic coordinates $(\zeta ,w)$ near $y$ with $A=\{ \zeta =0\} $, write $\zeta =\xi +\mathrm i\eta $, and let $t\in \mathbb R^{2n-2}$ be the real coordinates of $w$. The identities $\mathrm d\xi \wedge R=\mathrm d\eta \wedge R=0$ of Step 1 force $R=\mu \, \mathrm d\xi \wedge \mathrm d\eta $ for a real measure $\mu $ supported on $\{ \xi =\eta =0\} $, so $\mu =\delta _0(\xi ,\eta )\otimes \sigma (t)$ for a real measure $\sigma $. Since $R$ is closed, $\mathrm dR=\sum _ l(\partial \mu /\partial t_ l)\, \mathrm dt_ l\wedge \mathrm d\xi \wedge \mathrm d\eta $ vanishes, the derivatives in $\xi $ and $\eta $ disappearing after the exterior product with $\mathrm d\xi \wedge \mathrm d\eta $; so $\partial \mu /\partial t_ l=0$ as distributions. Testing this against functions $\chi (\xi ,\eta )\psi (t)$ with $\chi =1$ near $0$, we find that all distributional derivatives $\partial \sigma /\partial t_ l$ vanish. On a smaller ball in the $t$-variables, the convolutions of $\sigma $ with a smoothing kernel therefore have zero gradient and are constant; integrating them against a fixed test function of integral one, we see that these constants converge as the kernel shrinks, and that $\sigma $ is a constant multiple $c$ of Lebesgue measure. Hence $R=c[\{ \zeta =0\} ]$ near $y$, with $c$ real. (If $n=1$, $\sigma $ is a measure on a point and the same conclusion holds.)

Step 3: conclusion. Let $D=Z_\alpha $ be an irreducible component of codimension one, the closure of the connected component $S_\alpha $ of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$. By Step 2, near each point of $S_\alpha $ we have $R=c[D]$ for a real constant $c$, and this constant is locally constant on the connected manifold $S_\alpha $; we call it $c_ D$. By corollary A.2.13 the sum $\sum _ Dc_ D[D]$ is locally finite, and by lemma A.2.16 it is a closed real current with measure coefficients. The difference $R'=R-\sum _ Dc_ D[D]$ vanishes near every point of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$: near a point of $S_\alpha $ with $Z_\alpha $ of codimension one by Step 2, since the other components do not meet $S_\alpha $; and near a point of $A_{\mathrm{reg}}$ where $A$ has codimension at least two by Step 1, since no component of codimension one passes through such a point. Thus the support of $R'$ lies in $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$.

We show that $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ has codimension at least two; then $R'=0$ by Step 1. A branch of $A$ of dimension $n$ at a point $y$ would be a subgerm of the germ $(M,y)$, which is irreducible of dimension $n$ by lemma A.2.9, hence equal to it by proposition A.2.7; then $A$ would contain a neighborhood of $y$, which is impossible because $A$ is nowhere dense. So the branches of $A$ have dimension at most $n-1$, and $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$, which has smaller dimension than each branch (corollary A.2.10(1)), has codimension at least two. The coefficients are unique, because near a point of $S_\alpha $ the current $R$ determines $c_ D$; and if $R\geq 0$, then $c_ D\geq 0$, because $[D]$ is positive and nonzero near such a point. $\square$

A.3 Reduced spaces and normalization

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

The main result of this section is the existence and uniqueness of the normalization of a reduced complex space (theorem A.3.6), which we construct locally from weakly holomorphic functions.

A reduced complex space is a Hausdorff topological space $X$ with a sheaf $\mathcal O_ X$ of continuous complex-valued functions, its structure sheaf, such that every point has an open neighborhood $U$ and a homeomorphism $\phi $ of $U$ onto an analytic subset $A$ of a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb C^ N$ with the following property: a function $g$ on an open set $V\subset U$ belongs to $\mathcal O_ X(V)$ if and only if $g\circ \phi ^{-1}$ is, near each point of $\phi (V)$, the restriction of a holomorphic function on an open subset of $\Omega $. In other words, $\phi $ identifies $\mathcal O_ X|_ U$ with $\mathcal O_ A=(\mathcal O_\Omega /\mathcal I_ A)|_ A$. We call $(U,\phi )$ a local model, or local embedding, of $X$; holomorphic functions on $X$ extend locally to the ambient domains. As stated in the Conventions, all complex spaces are reduced, paracompact and Hausdorff; in this appendix we also assume that they have a countable basis of open sets. A compact space with such local models has a countable basis automatically, since finitely many local models cover it.

A continuous map $F\colon Y\to X$ of complex spaces is holomorphic if $g\circ F\in \mathcal O_ Y$ for every local section $g$ of $\mathcal O_ X$, that is, if in local models of $X$ the components of $F$ belong to $\mathcal O_ Y$. By the definition of $\mathcal O_ Y$, these components extend near each point of $Y$ to holomorphic functions on an open subset of the ambient space of a local model of $Y$; consequently, compositions of holomorphic maps are holomorphic. An isomorphism is a holomorphic bijection whose inverse is holomorphic. Two local models at the same point differ by an isomorphism of analytic sets which, together with its inverse, is locally the restriction of holomorphic maps between the ambient domains. Such an isomorphism maps analytic subsets to analytic subsets, and it maps submanifolds to submanifolds, because a holomorphic map defined on a manifold that has a holomorphic left inverse is an injective immersion and a homeomorphism onto its image, so that its image is a submanifold by lemma A.2.11. Hence regular and singular points, local branches and dimension are well defined on $X$, independently of the local model. An analytic subset of $X$ is a closed subset that is analytic in every local model; with the restricted structure sheaf it is again a complex space.

The structure sheaf is coherent as a module over itself. Indeed, in a local model let $f_1,\ldots ,f_ q$ be sections of $\mathcal O_ X$ with holomorphic lifts $F_1,\ldots ,F_ q$, and let $g_1,\ldots ,g_ N$ generate $\mathcal I_ A$ near a point, by theorem A.2.8; the relations of the $f_ i$ over $\mathcal O_ X$ are the images of the projections to the first $q$ coordinates of the relations of $F_1,\ldots ,F_ q,g_1,\ldots ,g_ N$ over $\mathcal O_\Omega $, which are locally finitely generated by theorem A.1.5. Thus lemma A.1.4 applies to $\mathcal O_ X$-modules. Each local ring $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ is a quotient of $\mathcal O_ N$, hence Noetherian. The zero set $V(\mathfrak a)$ of a coherent ideal sheaf $\mathfrak a\subset \mathcal O_ X$ is an analytic subset; conversely, the ideal sheaf $\mathcal I_ Y\subset \mathcal O_ X$ of functions vanishing on an analytic subset $Y\subset X$ is locally generated by the images of generators of the ideal sheaf of $Y$ in an ambient domain, so it is coherent by theorem A.2.8, lemma A.1.4.

A holomorphic map $F\colon Y\to X$ is proper if inverse images of compact sets are compact, and finite if it is proper with finite fibers. It is bimeromorphic if there is a nowhere dense analytic subset $E\subset X$ such that $F^{-1}(E)$ is nowhere dense in $Y$ and $F$ restricts to an isomorphism $Y\setminus F^{-1}(E)\to X\setminus E$. A proper map is closed: if $C\subset Y$ is closed and $F(y_ j)\to x$ with $y_ j\in C$, the points $y_ j$ eventually lie in the compact inverse image of a compact neighborhood of $x$, so a subsequence converges to some $y\in C$, and $F(y)=x$.

Lemma A.3.1. Let $F\colon Y\to X$ be a bimeromorphic holomorphic map of complex spaces. Then $F^{-1}(B)$ is nowhere dense in $Y$ for every nowhere dense analytic subset $B\subset X$. Consequently, the set $E$ in the definition may be enlarged by any nowhere dense analytic subset of $X$; if $U\subset X$ is the complement of a nowhere dense analytic subset, then $F^{-1}(U)$ is dense in $Y$; and every nonempty open subset of $Y$ contains a nonempty open set that $F$ maps isomorphically onto an open subset of $X$.

Proof. Let $E$ be as in the definition, and let $G\subset F^{-1}(B)$ be open in $Y$. Then $G\setminus F^{-1}(E)$ is an open subset of $Y\setminus F^{-1}(E)$ contained in $F^{-1}(B\setminus E)$, which corresponds under the isomorphism $Y\setminus F^{-1}(E)\to X\setminus E$ to the nowhere dense subset $B\setminus E$ of $X\setminus E$; so $G\setminus F^{-1}(E)$ is empty, and $G$, an open subset of the nowhere dense set $F^{-1}(E)$, is empty as well. In particular $F$ is also an isomorphism over $X\setminus (E\cup B)$, and $F^{-1}(E\cup B)=F^{-1}(E)\cup F^{-1}(B)$ is nowhere dense, as the union of two closed nowhere dense sets; so $E$ may be replaced by $E\cup B$. If $U=X\setminus B$, then $F^{-1}(U)$ is the complement of the closed nowhere dense set $F^{-1}(B)$, hence dense. Finally, a nonempty open set $G\subset Y$ meets the dense open set $Y\setminus F^{-1}(E)$, and $F$ maps the nonempty open set $G\setminus F^{-1}(E)$ isomorphically onto an open subset of $X\setminus E$. $\square$

A weakly holomorphic function on an open set $U\subset X$ is a holomorphic function on $U\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ that is locally bounded near $U\cap X_{\mathrm{sing}}$: every point of $U$ has a neighborhood $V$ such that the function is bounded on $V\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. These functions form a sheaf of rings $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_ X$, which contains $\mathcal O_ X$ because $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is dense in $X$ (corollary A.2.13). They can be computed branch by branch.

Lemma A.3.2. Let $x$ be a point of a complex space $X$, and let $X_1,\ldots ,X_ p$ be sufficiently small representatives of the branches of $(X,x)$ in a local model, on an open neighborhood $U$ of $x$ with $X\cap U=X_1\cup \cdots \cup X_ p$. Let $V\subset U$ be open. Then for each $i$ the restriction of a weakly holomorphic function $f$ on $X\cap V$ to $X_ i\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V$ extends uniquely to a weakly holomorphic function $f_ i$ on $X_ i\cap V$, and $f\mapsto (f_1,\ldots ,f_ p)$ is a bijection onto the $p$-tuples of weakly holomorphic functions on the sets $X_ i\cap V$. In particular,

\[ \widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}=\bigoplus _{i=1}^ p\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X_ i,x}. \]

Proof. By corollary A.2.10(1), $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap U$ is the disjoint union of the sets $X_ i\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}=(X_ i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus \bigcup _{j\neq i}X_ j$, each of which is open in $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. The analytic subset $(X_ i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap \bigcup _{j\neq i}X_ j$ of the manifold $(X_ i)_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is nowhere dense in it, by corollary A.2.4, applied to $(X_ i,x)$ and its subgerm $(X_ i\cap \bigcup _{j\neq i}X_ j,x)$, which differs from $(X_ i,x)$ because the branches are irredundant. Hence a bounded holomorphic function on $X_ i\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V$ extends uniquely across this analytic subset to a holomorphic function on $(X_ i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V$, by lemma A.1.7 applied on connected coordinate balls of $(X_ i)_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and the extension is bounded by the same bound. Applied near each point of $X_ i\cap V$, this shows that the restriction of $f$ has a unique extension $f_ i$, which is weakly holomorphic on $X_ i\cap V$. Conversely, if $f_ i$ is weakly holomorphic on $X_ i\cap V$ for each $i$, the function equal to $f_ i$ on $X_ i\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V$ is holomorphic and locally bounded on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V$, hence weakly holomorphic on $X\cap V$; by the uniqueness of the extensions, the two constructions are inverse to each other. Passing to germs at $x$ gives the last assertion. $\square$

The total ring of fractions of the reduced local ring $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ is obtained by inverting its nonzerodivisors. These are exactly the germs that are not identically zero on any branch of $(X,x)$. Indeed, if $h$ is such a germ and $hg=0$, then $g$ vanishes on the complement of $\{ h=0\} $ in each branch, which is dense by corollary A.2.4; so $g=0$. If instead $h$ vanishes identically on the branch $X_1$, choose for each $j\neq 1$ a germ $g_ j$ vanishing on $X_ j$ but not on $X_1$, which exists because $(X_1,x)\not\subset (X_ j,x)$; the product $v=\prod _{j\neq 1}g_ j$ does not vanish identically on $X_1$, because $I_{X_1,x}$ is prime, and $hv=0$.

Proposition A.3.3. Every point of a complex space $X$ has a neighborhood $U$ with a holomorphic function $h$ on $U$ whose germ at each $y\in U$ does not vanish identically on any branch of $(X,y)$, such that

\[ h_ y\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset \mathcal O_{X,y} \qquad (y\in U). \]

For every $x\in X$, the weakly holomorphic germs at $x$ are precisely the elements of the total ring of fractions of $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ that are integral over $\mathcal O_{X,x}$, and they form a finitely generated $\mathcal O_{X,x}$-module. If $(X,x)$ is irreducible, every weakly holomorphic function near $x$ has a finite limit at $x$ along $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$.

Proof. Step 1: an irreducible germ. Let $(X,0)$ be irreducible, with a local parametrization as in theorem A.2.3, its degree $q$, its primitive linear form $u$, its Weierstrass polynomial $W$ and $\delta =\operatorname {Disc}(W)$. We show that $\delta _ y\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset \mathcal O_{X,y}$ for every $y$ in the parametrization polydisc. Let $f$ be weakly holomorphic near $y$ and bounded on $V\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ for a neighborhood $V$ of $y$. Let $y=y^{(1)},y^{(2)},\ldots ,y^{(s)}$ be the points of $X$ over $y'$, choose disjoint neighborhoods $V_1\subset V,V_2,\ldots ,V_ s$ of them, and, using properness, a connected neighborhood $N'$ of $y'$ such that all points of $X$ over $N'$ lie in $\bigcup _ lV_ l$. Define $F$ on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap \pi ^{-1}(N')$ by $F=f$ on $V_1$ and $F=0$ on the other $V_ l$.

For $z'\in N'\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $, let $z^{(1)},\ldots ,z^{(q)}$ be the points of $X$ over $z'$; the values $u(z^{(i)})$ are the distinct roots of $W(z',\cdot )$, by theorem A.2.3, so there is a unique polynomial $\sum _{j<q}b_ j(z')T^ j$ taking the value $F(z^{(i)})$ at $T=u(z^{(i)})$ for every $i$. By Cramer's rule $\delta b_ j=D\cdot D_ j$, where $D$ is the Vandermonde determinant of the $u(z^{(i)})$, so that $D^2=\delta $ by (A.5), and $D_ j$ is obtained by replacing its $j$th column with the values $F(z^{(i)})$. The products $D\cdot D_ j$ do not depend on the order of the points; they are holomorphic, because the points depend holomorphically on $z'$ locally, and bounded, because $u$ and $F$ are bounded. By lemma A.1.7 they extend holomorphically to $N'$. The holomorphic function $H(z)=\sum _ j(\delta b_ j)(z')u(z'')^ j$ on $N'\times \Delta ''$ equals $\delta F$ on the covering locus over $N'$, hence $\delta f$ on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap V_1\cap \pi ^{-1}(N')$, by density of the covering locus and continuity. Thus $\delta f$ extends to the holomorphic germ of $H|_ X$ at $y$.

Step 2: several branches. Fix $x\in X$, and let $X_1,\ldots ,X_ p$ be sufficiently small representatives of the branches of $(X,x)$, and let $\delta _ j$ be a denominator for $X_ j$ as in Step 1, lifted to a holomorphic function of the ambient coordinates. Adding to $\delta _ j$ a linear combination $\sum _ la_ le_{jl}$ of local generators $e_{jl}$ of the ideal of $X_ j$ does not change it on $X_ j$. For each $k\neq j$ some $e_{jl}$ does not vanish identically on $X_ k$, so the coefficients $a$ for which $\delta _ j+\sum _ la_ le_{jl}$ vanishes identically on $X_ k$ form a proper affine subspace or the empty set. Choosing $a$ outside these finitely many subspaces, we obtain $\widetilde\delta _ j$, equal to $\delta _ j$ on $X_ j$ and not identically zero on any $X_ k$. Then $\eta =\prod _ j\widetilde\delta _ j$ is not identically zero on any $X_ k$, and it is a denominator for each $X_ j$ separately: if $f$ is weakly holomorphic on $X$ near a point $y$, the restriction of $f$ to $X_ j\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ extends to a weakly holomorphic function $f_ j$ on $X_ j$ near $y$, by lemma A.3.2, so by Step 1 $\eta f_ j=g_ j|_{X_ j}$ for some holomorphic $g_ j$ near $y$.

For each $j$ choose a product $v_ j$ of local equations of the branches $X_ k$, $k\neq j$, that does not vanish identically on $X_ j$, and put $h=\eta \sum _ jv_ j$. On $X_ j$ we have $h=\eta v_ j$, which is not identically zero, and $hf=\sum _ jv_ jg_ j$ holds on every $X_ j\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$, hence on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $y$. Thus $h_ y\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset \mathcal O_{X,y}$ for all $y$ near $x$. Finally, $\{ h=0\} \cap X_ j$ has smaller dimension than $X_ j$ at $x$, hence at all points near $x$, by upper semicontinuity; every branch of $(X,y)$ is a branch of some $(X_ j,y)$ and has dimension $\dim X_ j$, by purity. So, after shrinking $U$, the germ of $h$ at every $y\in U$ is not identically zero on any branch of $(X,y)$.

Step 3: integrality, finiteness and limits. The map $f\mapsto (hf)/h$ embeds $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$ into the total ring of fractions of $\mathcal O_{X,x}$; it does not depend on the choice of $h$, and it is injective because $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus \{ h=0\} $ is dense in $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Multiplication by $h$ identifies $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$ with an ideal of the Noetherian ring $\mathcal O_{X,x}$, so it is a finitely generated $\mathcal O_{X,x}$-module.

Every weakly holomorphic germ is integral. On an irreducible germ, in the notation of Step 1, the elementary symmetric functions of the values of $f$ at the $q$ points of $X$ over $z'\in \Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $ are bounded holomorphic functions, which extend to $\Delta '$ by lemma A.1.7. They are the coefficients of a monic polynomial $P(z',T)$ with $P(z',f)=0$ on the covering locus, hence on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and these coefficients belong to $\mathcal O_ d\subset \mathcal O_{X,x}$. For several branches, the product of the polynomials of the branches, with coefficients lifted to holomorphic functions, is a monic equation for $f$. Conversely, let $a/b$ be an element of the total ring of fractions, with $b$ a nonzerodivisor, that is integral over $\mathcal O_{X,x}$. On $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus \{ b=0\} $ near $x$ the function $a/b$ satisfies a monic equation with bounded coefficients, so it is bounded by (A.1). The set $\{ b=0\} $ meets each connected open subset of the manifold $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ in a nowhere dense analytic subset, so by lemma A.1.7 $a/b$ extends to a holomorphic function on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $x$, which is weakly holomorphic and is mapped to $a/b$.

Let $(X,x)$ be irreducible and $f$ weakly holomorphic near $x$, satisfying the monic equation $P(z',f)=0$ just constructed. As $Q$ runs through the parametrization polydiscs around $x$, the sets $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap Q$ are the traces on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ of a basis of neighborhoods of $x$, and each of them is connected, because it lies between the connected covering locus and its closure. The cluster set of $f$ at $x$ is the intersection of the decreasing compact connected nonempty sets $\overline{f(X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap Q)}$, hence it is nonempty, compact and connected. If $w$ is a cluster value, then $w=\lim f(z_ k)$ for points $z_ k\to x$ of $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and $P(x',w)=\lim P(z_ k',f(z_ k))=0$; so the cluster set is contained in the finite set of roots of $P(x',\cdot )$. Being connected, it is a single point, and $f$ has a finite limit at $x$. $\square$

A point $x\in X$ is normal if $(X,x)$ is irreducible and $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ is integrally closed in its field of fractions. By proposition A.3.3, this means that $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}=\mathcal O_{X,x}$. Conversely, this equality forces $(X,x)$ to be irreducible: otherwise the weakly holomorphic function equal to $1$ on one branch and to $0$ on the others would be holomorphic, hence continuous, at $x$, which is impossible because it takes both values at points arbitrarily close to $x$. Thus $x$ is normal if and only if $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}=\mathcal O_{X,x}$. The space $X$ is normal if all its points are normal. Regular points are normal by lemma A.1.7.

Lemma A.3.4. The set of nonnormal points of a complex space $X$ is an analytic subset of $X$. If $X$ is normal and $E\subset X$ is a nowhere dense analytic subset, every holomorphic function on $X\setminus E$ that is locally bounded near $E$ extends uniquely to a holomorphic function on $X$.

Proof. We show that the set of nonnormal points is the support of a coherent sheaf. We first record two facts about coherent sheaves. If $\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal G$ are coherent $\mathcal O_ X$-modules, the sheaf $\mathcal Hom(\mathcal F,\mathcal G)$ of module homomorphisms is coherent, and its stalk at $x$ is $\operatorname {Hom}_{\mathcal O_{X,x}}(\mathcal F_ x,\mathcal G_ x)$. Indeed, $\mathcal F$ has local presentations $\mathcal O_ X^ a\to \mathcal O_ X^ b\to \mathcal F\to 0$, and $\mathcal Hom(\mathcal F,\mathcal G)$ is the kernel of the induced morphism $\mathcal G^ b\to \mathcal G^ a$; we apply lemma A.1.4.

Next, the support of a coherent module $\mathcal F$ is analytic. In a local presentation as above, given by a $b\times a$ matrix $\Phi $ of holomorphic functions, $\mathcal F_ x=0$ exactly when the matrix $\Phi (x)$ has rank $b$. If $\Phi (x)$ has rank $b$, then $\mathcal F_ x=\mathfrak m_ x\mathcal F_ x$, where $\mathfrak m_ x$ is the maximal ideal, and the following determinant argument gives $\mathcal F_ x=0$: writing each of finitely many generators of $\mathcal F_ x$ as a combination of them with coefficients in $\mathfrak m_ x$, with coefficient matrix $C$, the adjugate identity shows that $\det (I-C)$ annihilates the generators, and this determinant is a unit. Conversely, if $\mathcal F_ x=0$, then $\Phi $ maps $\mathcal O_{X,x}^ a$ onto $\mathcal O_{X,x}^ b$, and reducing modulo $\mathfrak m_ x$ shows that $\Phi (x)$ has rank $b$. Hence the support of $\mathcal F$ is the common zero set of the minors of size $b$ of $\Phi $.

Let $h$ be a denominator as in proposition A.3.3 on an open set $U$, let $\mathcal I$ be the coherent ideal sheaf of $\{ h=0\} $ in $U$, and put $\mathcal F=\mathcal Hom(\mathcal I,\mathcal I)$. Multiplication embeds $\mathcal O_ U$ in $\mathcal F$, because $\mathcal I$ contains the nonzerodivisor $h$. Every $\varphi \in \mathcal F_ y$ is multiplication by an element of the total ring of fractions: if $a=\varphi (h)$, then $h\varphi (g)=\varphi (hg)=ag$ for $g\in \mathcal I_ y$, so $\varphi $ is multiplication by $a/h$. Let $g_1,\ldots ,g_ N$ generate $\mathcal I_ y$, and write $(a/h)g_ i=\varphi (g_ i)=\sum _ jc_{ij}g_ j$ with $c_{ij}\in \mathcal O_{X,y}$. Multiplying the matrix $(a/h)I-(c_{ij})$ by its adjugate, we see that its determinant, a monic polynomial in $a/h$ with coefficients in $\mathcal O_{X,y}$, annihilates every $g_ i$, hence $\mathcal I_ y$ and in particular $h$; since $h$ is invertible in the total ring of fractions, this polynomial vanishes, and $a/h$ is integral over $\mathcal O_{X,y}$. Hence, by proposition A.3.3,

\[ \mathcal O_ U\subset \mathcal F\subset \widetilde{\mathcal O}_ U. \]

We claim that the support of the coherent sheaf $\mathcal F/\mathcal O_ U$ is the set of nonnormal points of $U$; this proves the first assertion. At a normal point $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}=\mathcal O_{X,y}$, so $\mathcal F_ y=\mathcal O_{X,y}$. Let $y$ be a nonnormal point, so that $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\neq \mathcal O_{X,y}$. By the analytic Nullstellensatz (corollary A.2.6), applied in a local model, every element of $\mathcal I_ y$ has a power in $h\mathcal O_{X,y}$. Let $g_1,\ldots ,g_ N$ generate $\mathcal I_ y$, and choose $e$ with $g_ i^ e\in h\mathcal O_{X,y}$ for all $i$. Every monomial of degree $\ell =N(e-1)+1$ in the $g_ i$ contains some $g_ i$ at least $e$ times, so $\mathcal I_ y^\ell \subset h\mathcal O_{X,y}$, and therefore $\mathcal I_ y^\ell \widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset h\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset \mathcal O_{X,y}$.

Let $k$ be the least integer with $\mathcal I_ y^ k\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}\subset \mathcal O_{X,y}$; then $k\geq 1$, and we may choose $w\in \mathcal I_ y^{k-1}\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,y}$ with $w\notin \mathcal O_{X,y}$. Then $w\mathcal I_ y\subset \mathcal O_{X,y}$, and every product $wg$, $g\in \mathcal I_ y$, vanishes on $\{ h=0\} $: at regular points because $g$ vanishes there and $w$ is holomorphic, and at singular points because $w$ is locally bounded, $g$ tends to zero, and $wg$ is continuous. Thus $w\mathcal I_ y\subset \mathcal I_ y$, so multiplication by $w$ belongs to $\mathcal F_ y$; it is not multiplication by an element of $\mathcal O_{X,y}$, since $w\notin \mathcal O_{X,y}$ and $h$ is a nonzerodivisor. This proves the claim.

For the last assertion, let $f$ be a holomorphic function on $X\setminus E$ that is locally bounded near $E$. Since $E$ is nowhere dense, an extension is unique. The set $E\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ meets every connected open subset of the manifold $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ in a nowhere dense analytic subset, so $f$ extends holomorphically to $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ by lemma A.1.7. The extension is locally bounded near $X_{\mathrm{sing}}$, because on a neighborhood of each point it is bounded by a bound of $f$ on the dense subset $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus E$; hence it is weakly holomorphic, and therefore holomorphic, because $X$ is normal. It agrees with $f$ on $X\setminus E$, since both functions are continuous there and agree on the dense subset $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus E$. $\square$

Normalizations are first constructed locally, as closures of graphs of weakly holomorphic functions.

Lemma A.3.5. Let $X$ be an analytic subset of a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb C^ N$, with coordinates $z$, let $x\in X$, and let $X_1,\ldots ,X_ p$ be sufficiently small representatives of the branches of $(X,x)$. There is an open neighborhood $W\subset \Omega $ of $x$ with the following properties. For each $i$ there are functions $f_{i1},\ldots ,f_{im_ i}$, weakly holomorphic on $X_ i\cap W$, and monic polynomials $P_{ij}(z,T)$ in $T$ with coefficients holomorphic on $W$, such that $P_{ij}(z,f_{ij})=0$ on $(X_ i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$. The closure $Y_ i$ in $W\times \mathbb C^{m_ i}$ of the graph of $(f_{i1},\ldots ,f_{im_ i})$ over $(X_ i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$ is a normal analytic subset of $W\times \mathbb C^{m_ i}$, on which $P_{ij}(z,w_ j)=0$ for all $j$, and the projection $\nu _ i\colon Y_ i\to X_ i\cap W$, $(z,w)\mapsto z$, is finite and bimeromorphic, is an isomorphism over $(X_ i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$, and has a single point over $x$. The map from the disjoint union of $Y_1,\ldots ,Y_ p$ to $X\cap W$ given by the $\nu _ i$ is finite and bimeromorphic, is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$, and has $p$ points over $x$. All these properties persist when $W$ is replaced by a smaller open neighborhood of $x$ and each $Y_ i$ by its part over it.

Proof. Step 1: the construction for an irreducible germ. Suppose first that $p=1$, so that $(X,x)$ is irreducible. Choose generators $f_1,\ldots ,f_ m$ of the $\mathcal O_{X,x}$-module $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$ and a denominator $h$ as in proposition A.3.3, so that $hf_ j=g_ j$ with $g_ j$ holomorphic. Choose a neighborhood $W\subset \Omega $ of $x$ on which $h$ and the $g_ j$ are holomorphic, the $f_ j$ are weakly holomorphic, and each $f_ j$ satisfies a monic equation $P_ j(z,f_ j)=0$ whose coefficients are holomorphic on $W$. On $(X\cap W)\setminus \{ h=0\} $ the functions $g_ j/h$ are holomorphic and agree with $f_ j$ on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Let $Y$ be the closure in $W\times \mathbb C^ m$ of the graph of $(g_1/h,\ldots ,g_ m/h)$ over $(X\cap W)\setminus \{ h=0\} $. This graph is the part outside $\{ h=0\} $ of the analytic set

\[ Z=\{ (z,w)\in W\times \mathbb C^ m:\ z\in X,\ h(z)w_ j=g_ j(z)\ \text{for all }j\} . \]

By lemma A.2.5, applied in $W\times \mathbb C^ m$ to $Z$ and the analytic set $\{ h=0\} $, the closure $Y$ of $Z\setminus \{ h=0\} $ is analytic; near each point it is the union of the branches of $Z$ on which $h$ does not vanish identically.

The monic equations $P_ j(z,g_ j/h)=0$ hold on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus \{ h=0\} $, which is dense in $X\setminus \{ h=0\} $, and their left-hand sides are continuous there; so they hold on the graph, hence on its closure $Y$: $P_ j(z,w_ j)=0$. By (A.1) they bound $w$ over compact subsets of $X\cap W$ and leave finitely many possible values of $w$ over each point. Hence the projection $\nu \colon Y\to X\cap W$ is proper with finite fibers.

Over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$, the set $Y$ is the graph of $f=(f_1,\ldots ,f_ m)$: a point of $Y$ over $z_0\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is a limit of graph points over points $z_ k\to z_0$, and since the $f_ j$ are continuous at $z_0$, it equals $(z_0,f(z_0))$. Conversely, $(z_0,f(z_0))$ lies in $Y$, because $z_0$ is a limit of points of $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus \{ h=0\} $: the germ of $h$ at $z_0$ is not identically zero on the manifold germ $(X,z_0)$, so $\{ h=0\} $ is nowhere dense in $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $z_0$, by lemma A.1.6. Hence $\nu $ is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$, with inverse $z\mapsto (z,f(z))$. This graph is dense in $Y$, because $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus \{ h=0\} $ is dense in $X\setminus \{ h=0\} $ and $(g_1/h,\ldots ,g_ m/h)$ is continuous there; so $Y$ is the closure of the graph of $f$ over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$, the set $\nu ^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})$ is nowhere dense, and $\nu $ is bimeromorphic. Every point of $Y$ over $x$ is a limit of points $(z_ k,f(z_ k))$ with $z_ k\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ and $z_ k\to x$; by the last assertion of proposition A.3.3, the fiber over $x$ is the single point $y=(x,\lim _{z\to x}f(z))$.

Step 2: normality of $Y$ at $y$. Let $a$ be a weakly holomorphic function on $Y$ near $y$. Since $\nu ^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})$ is a manifold, it is contained in $Y_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and $b=a\circ (\nu |_{\nu ^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})})^{-1}$ is holomorphic on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $x$. It is bounded near $x$: by properness and since the fiber over $x$ is $\{ y\} $, the inverse image of a small neighborhood of $x$ lies in a neighborhood of $y$ on which $a$ is bounded. Thus $b\in \widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$, and $b=\sum _ ja_ jf_ j$ with $a_ j\in \mathcal O_{X,x}$. Lift the $a_ j$ to holomorphic functions $\widetilde a_ j$ near $x$ in $\Omega $. The function $\sum _ j\widetilde a_ j(z)w_ j$ is holomorphic near $y$, and it equals $a$ on $\nu ^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})$ near $y$, which is dense in $Y_{\mathrm{reg}}$; since both functions are continuous on $Y_{\mathrm{reg}}$, they agree on $Y_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $y$. Hence $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{Y,y}=\mathcal O_{Y,y}$, and $y$ is a normal point. By lemma A.3.4, all points of $Y$ near $y$ are normal, and by properness the inverse image of a small neighborhood $U$ of $x$ lies in this set of normal points. Replacing $W$ by $U$ and $Y$ by $\nu ^{-1}(U)$, we obtain the assertions of the lemma for $p=1$. They persist when $W$ is replaced by a smaller open neighborhood of $x$, because the closure of the graph over the smaller set is the part of $Y$ over it, and normality, finiteness, bimeromorphy and being an isomorphism over the regular locus are preserved under restriction over open subsets of $X$.

Step 3: several branches. In general, apply Steps 1 and 2 to each irreducible germ $(X_ i,x)$, with $X_ i$ regarded as an analytic subset of a ball around $x$ in $\Omega $, and let $W$ be the intersection of the resulting neighborhoods; by the persistence just noted, the assertions about the individual $Y_ i$ hold over $W$. The disjoint union of the $Y_ i$ is normal, and its map to $X\cap W$ is finite, with $p$ points over $x$. It is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$, because, by corollary A.2.10(1), $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$ is the disjoint union of the open sets $X_ i\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}=(X_ i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus \bigcup _{j\neq i}X_ j$, over which $\nu _ i$ is an isomorphism. It is bimeromorphic, with the nowhere dense analytic subset $X_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap W$ of $X\cap W$. Indeed, by corollary A.2.10(1), $X_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap X_ i=(X_ i)_{\mathrm{sing}}\cup (X_ i\cap \bigcup _{j\neq i}X_ j)$, which is a nowhere dense analytic subset of $X_ i\cap W$ by corollary A.2.4; so its inverse image under the bimeromorphic map $\nu _ i$ is nowhere dense in $Y_ i$, by lemma A.3.1. These properties again persist for smaller neighborhoods of $x$. $\square$

Theorem A.3.6 (Normalization). Every complex space $X$ has a normalization: a normal complex space $\widetilde X$ with a finite bimeromorphic holomorphic map $\nu \colon \widetilde X\to X$ that is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. It is unique up to a unique isomorphism over $X$. The fiber $\nu ^{-1}(x)$ has one point for each irreducible branch of $(X,x)$, and

\[ \nu _*\mathcal O_{\widetilde X}=\widetilde{\mathcal O}_ X. \]

Proof. Step 1: local normalizations. Let $x\in X$, and choose a local model of a neighborhood of $x$. By lemma A.3.5, applied to sufficiently small representatives of the branches of $(X,x)$, there is a neighborhood $W$ of $x$ such that $X\cap W$ has a normalization, namely the disjoint union of the normal spaces $Y_ i$ with the maps $\nu _ i$, whose fiber over $x$ has one point for each branch of $(X,x)$.

Step 2: uniqueness. Let $\nu _1\colon Y_1\to X$ and $\nu _2\colon Y_2\to X$ be finite bimeromorphic maps from normal complex spaces that are isomorphisms over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ there is a unique isomorphism $\phi _0=\nu _2^{-1}\circ \nu _1$ commuting with the maps to $X$; we show that it extends holomorphically. Let $p\in Y_1$ and $x=\nu _1(p)$. Choose disjoint open neighborhoods $W_ l$ of the finitely many points $q_ l$ of $\nu _2^{-1}(x)$, each relatively compact in the domain $W'_ l$ of a local model of $Y_2$, so that the coordinate functions of these models are bounded on $W_ l$. By properness of $\nu _2$, there is a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ with $\nu _2^{-1}(U)\subset \bigcup _ lW_ l$. The analytic subset $E=\nu _1^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})$ of $Y_1$ is nowhere dense, because $\nu _1$ is bimeromorphic and $X_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is nowhere dense in $X$ (lemma A.3.1).

Since $Y_1$ is normal, the germ $(Y_1,p)$ is irreducible, and a sufficiently small parametrization neighborhood $V$ of $p$ with $\nu _1(V)\subset U$ has the property that $V\setminus E$ is connected: its connected covering locus remains connected after removing the analytic subset $E$, which is nowhere dense in it, by lemma A.1.7, and the result is dense in $V\setminus E$. Hence $\phi _0(V\setminus E)$ lies in a single $W_ l$. The coordinate functions of $W'_ l$ composed with $\phi _0$ are bounded holomorphic functions on $V\setminus E$, and they extend holomorphically to $V$ by lemma A.3.4. The extended map takes values in $\overline{W_ l}\subset W'_ l$, and it satisfies the equations of the local model, which hold on the dense set $V\setminus E$; so it is a holomorphic map $V\to Y_2$ extending $\phi _0$. These local extensions are unique, by density of $V\setminus E$, and therefore glue to a holomorphic map $\phi \colon Y_1\to Y_2$ with $\nu _2\circ \phi =\nu _1$. Extending $\phi _0^{-1}$ in the same way, we obtain the inverse map, since the compositions are the identity on dense open sets. Any isomorphism over $X$ agrees with $\phi _0$ over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$, so it equals $\phi $. This proves uniqueness.

Step 3: gluing. By the uniqueness just proved, applied over the overlaps of their domains, the local normalizations of Step 1 are canonically isomorphic there, and these isomorphisms are compatible on triple overlaps, because an isomorphism over $X$ between two normalizations is unique; so they glue to a space $\widetilde X$ with a map $\nu \colon \widetilde X\to X$. The space $\widetilde X$ is Hausdorff, since points with different images are separated by inverse images of disjoint neighborhoods and points with the same image lie in one local normalization. Since $X$ has a countable basis, countably many of the open sets over which local normalizations were constructed cover $X$, and each of these local normalizations, a finite disjoint union of analytic subsets of open subsets of the spaces $\mathbb C^{N+m_ i}$, has a countable basis; hence so has $\widetilde X$. Being locally compact and Hausdorff with a countable basis, $\widetilde X$ is paracompact. Being finite, normal and bimeromorphic are local properties over $X$, so $\nu $ is a normalization. The description of the fibers follows from Step 1.

Step 4: the direct image. Let $U\subset X$ be open. If $g$ is holomorphic on $\nu ^{-1}(U)$, then $g\circ \nu ^{-1}$ is holomorphic on $U\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ and locally bounded near $U\cap X_{\mathrm{sing}}$, because $\nu $ is proper; so it is weakly holomorphic. Conversely, if $f$ is weakly holomorphic on $U$, then $f\circ \nu $ is holomorphic on $\nu ^{-1}(U\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}})$ and locally bounded near the nowhere dense analytic subset $\nu ^{-1}(U\cap X_{\mathrm{sing}})$; by lemma A.3.4, applied on the normal space $\nu ^{-1}(U)$, it extends holomorphically to $\nu ^{-1}(U)$. These two maps are inverse to each other, which proves $\nu _*\mathcal O_{\widetilde X}=\widetilde{\mathcal O}_ X$. $\square$

Lemma A.3.5 therefore describes the normalization locally: the normalization of each branch is the closure of the graph of finitely many weakly holomorphic functions satisfying monic equations.

Corollary A.3.7. Let $X$ be an analytic subset of a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb C^ N$, with coordinates $z$, let $\nu \colon \widetilde X\to X$ be its normalization, and let $x\in X$. Let $W$ and $\nu _ i\colon Y_ i\to X_ i\cap W$, $1\leq i\leq p$, be as in lemma A.3.5, for sufficiently small representatives $X_1,\ldots ,X_ p$ of the branches of $(X,x)$. Then each $\nu _ i$ is a normalization of $X_ i\cap W$ whose fiber over $x$ is a single point, and there is an isomorphism of $\nu ^{-1}(W)$ onto the disjoint union of $Y_1,\ldots ,Y_ p$ under which $\nu $ corresponds to the maps $\nu _ i$. In particular, every holomorphic function on $\widetilde X$ near a point of $\nu ^{-1}(x)$ corresponds, near that point, to the restriction to some $Y_ i$ of a holomorphic function of $(z,w)$.

Proof. By lemma A.3.5, each $Y_ i$ is normal, and $\nu _ i$ is finite, bimeromorphic and an isomorphism over $(X_ i)_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap W$, with a single point over $x$; so $\nu _ i$ is a normalization of $X_ i\cap W$. Likewise, the disjoint union of the $Y_ i$ is a normalization of $X\cap W$. So is $\nu ^{-1}(W)\to X\cap W$, because being finite, normal, bimeromorphic and an isomorphism over the regular locus are local properties over $X$. By the uniqueness in theorem A.3.6, there is an isomorphism over $X\cap W$ between them. The last assertion holds because the holomorphic functions on the analytic set $Y_ i$ are, locally, restrictions of holomorphic functions of the ambient coordinates $(z,w)$. $\square$

Definition A.3.8. A complex space is unibranch if all its germs $(X,x)$ are irreducible. Equivalently, each local ring $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ is a domain whose integral closure in its field of fractions is a local ring. Indeed, in a local model $\mathcal O_{X,x}=\mathcal O_ N/I_{X,x}$, so by proposition A.2.1 the germ $(X,x)$ is irreducible if and only if $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ is a domain. In this case, by proposition A.3.3, the integral closure is $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$, and taking the limit at $x$, which exists by the same proposition, is a ring homomorphism $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}\to \mathbb C$. An element with nonzero limit is a unit, because its reciprocal is holomorphic and bounded on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ near $x$, hence weakly holomorphic; an element with limit zero is not a unit. So the non-units form the ideal of elements with limit zero, and $\widetilde{\mathcal O}_{X,x}$ is a local ring. Thus this definition agrees with the definition of unibranch local rings in commutative algebra.

Corollary A.3.9. The normalization of a unibranch complex space is a homeomorphism. Every point $x$ of a complex space with $(X,x)$ irreducible, in particular every point of a unibranch space, has arbitrarily small neighborhoods $U$ such that $U\setminus A$ is connected for every nowhere dense analytic subset $A$ of $U$.

Proof. On a unibranch space the normalization has one-point fibers, so it is a continuous bijection; being proper, it is closed, hence a homeomorphism. For the second assertion, let $U$ be the intersection of $X$ with a parametrization polydisc of $(X,x)$. Its covering locus is a connected manifold, dense in $U$; it remains connected after deleting $A$, by lemma A.1.7, because $A$ is nowhere dense, and the result is dense in $U\setminus A$. Hence $U\setminus A$ is connected. $\square$

Example A.3.10. The curve $\{ zw=0\} \subset \mathbb C^2$ has two branches at the origin, the coordinate axes, whose germs are irreducible, being germs of submanifolds (lemma A.2.9). The disjoint union of the two axes, mapped to the curve by the inclusions, is a manifold, hence normal, and the map is finite, bimeromorphic and an isomorphism over the regular locus $\{ zw=0\} \setminus \{ 0\} $; by the uniqueness in theorem A.3.6 it is the normalization. Its fiber over the origin has two points, and the curve is not unibranch.

The cusp $C=\{ w^2=z^3\} $ is normalized by $\gamma (t)=(t^2,t^3)$. Indeed, $\gamma $ is a holomorphic bijection of $\mathbb C$ onto $C$: for $(z,w)\in C$ with $z\neq 0$, the equations $t^2=z$, $t^3=w$ force $t=w/z$, and $t=w/z$ satisfies them, since $(w/z)^2=z$ and $(w/z)^3=(w/z)z=w$; and $\gamma (t)=0$ only for $t=0$. The points of $C\setminus \{ 0\} $ are regular: at such a point $z\neq 0$, so the map $(z,w)\mapsto (w^2-z^3,w)$ has the nonzero Jacobian determinant $-3z^2$ there, and by proposition 7.1.2(1) the functions $w^2-z^3$ and $w$ form holomorphic coordinates near such a point, in which $C$ is a coordinate line; so $C$ is a submanifold there. On $C\setminus \{ 0\} $ the inverse of $\gamma $ is the holomorphic function $t=w/z$. This function satisfies $t^2=z$, so it tends to $0$ at the origin; hence $\gamma $ is a homeomorphism.

The function $t$ is not the restriction of a holomorphic function $F(z,w)$ near the origin, because $F(t^2,t^3)$ has no term of degree one in $t$. Hence the origin is a singular point of $C$. Otherwise $t$, which is bounded near the origin, would extend holomorphically across it on the manifold $C$, by lemma A.1.7, and would therefore be the restriction of a holomorphic function of $(z,w)$: in coordinates in which a submanifold is a coordinate plane, a holomorphic function on it extends independently of the other coordinates. So $t$ is weakly holomorphic on $C$, and integral, since $t^2=z$, but it is not holomorphic at the origin, so $C$ is not normal there. Thus $\gamma $ is a finite bimeromorphic map from a manifold that is an isomorphism over $C_{\mathrm{reg}}=C\setminus \{ 0\} $, and by the uniqueness in theorem A.3.6 it is the normalization. The cusp is therefore unibranch but not normal, and it distinguishes unibranch spaces from normal spaces.

A.4 Analytic curves and finite maps

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

Holomorphic curves through a point of a complex space reduce questions about functions near singular points to one complex variable: for instance, a holomorphic function whose modulus has a local maximum at a point is constant along every curve through that point. We parametrize irreducible germs of dimension one, construct curves through a given point that avoid a given analytic subgerm, and study finite maps onto balls, which control the dimension of zero sets of holomorphic functions.

Proposition A.4.1. Let $(C,x)$ be an irreducible analytic germ of dimension one. There are a disc $\Delta \subset \mathbb C$ centered at $0$, a representative $C$ of the germ and a holomorphic bijection $\gamma \colon \Delta \to C$ with $\gamma (0)=x$ that restricts to a biholomorphism of $\Delta \setminus \{ 0\} $ onto $C\setminus \{ x\} $. In the coordinates of a local parametrization of $(C,x)$, the projection of $\gamma $ is $t\mapsto t^ q$ for some integer $q\geq 1$. A normal complex space of dimension one is a complex manifold.

Proof. Embed $C$ in a local model and apply theorem A.2.3. The base is a disc $\Delta '=\{ |z|<r\} $, and since the zeros of $\delta $ are isolated, we may take the parametrization polydisc so small that $\delta $ has no zero in $\Delta '\setminus \{ 0\} $. Let $C$ be the representative in this polydisc and $C^*=C\setminus \{ x\} =\pi ^{-1}(\Delta '\setminus \{ 0\} )$. Then $C^*$ is a covering of the punctured disc $\Delta '\setminus \{ 0\} $ with $q$ sheets, and it is connected: if $\delta (0)=0$, it is the covering locus, and if $\delta (0)\neq 0$, then $q=1$, because the fiber over $0$ is $\{ x\} $. We shall show that lifting a circle around the puncture permutes the $q$ points of a fiber cyclically, and then obtain $\gamma $ by lifting the exponential map to $C^*$.

We first recall two facts about coverings. A path in $\Delta '\setminus \{ 0\} $ lifts uniquely to $C^*$ once its initial point is prescribed: subdivide the path into finitely many pieces lying in discs over which the covering is trivial, and lift piece by piece. If two paths with the same endpoints are homotopic with fixed endpoints, their lifts with the same initial point have the same endpoint. Indeed, let $h\colon [0,1]^2\to \Delta '\setminus \{ 0\} $ be the homotopy, with $h(0,s)$ and $h(1,s)$ independent of $s$, and let $e(s)$ be the endpoint of the lift of $h(\cdot ,s)$ with the prescribed initial point. By uniform continuity there is an integer $m\geq 1$ such that $h$ maps each square $Q_{jl}=[(j-1)/m,j/m]\times [(l-1)/m,l/m]$ into a disc $D_{jl}$ over which the covering is trivial. A lift of a path in $D_{jl}$ stays in the sheet over $D_{jl}$ that contains its initial point, because these sheets are open and disjoint.

Fix $l$ and $s,s'\in [(l-1)/m,l/m]$. We show by induction on $j$ that the lifts of $h(\cdot ,s)$ and $h(\cdot ,s')$ over $[(j-1)/m,j/m]$ lie in one sheet over $D_{jl}$. For $j=1$ they start at the same point. If this holds for some $j<m$, the points of the two lifts at the parameter $j/m$ are joined by the lift, inside this sheet, of the path $h(j/m,\cdot )$ between $s$ and $s'$; this lift is a connected set lying over $D_{jl}\cap D_{j+1,l}$, so it lies in one sheet over $D_{j+1,l}$, which then contains both lifts over $[j/m,(j+1)/m]$. For $j=m$ we obtain two points in one sheet over $D_{ml}$ lying over the same point $h(1,s)=h(1,s')$, so $e(s)=e(s')$. Hence $e$ is constant on each interval $[(l-1)/m,l/m]$, and therefore on $[0,1]$.

Next, every loop based at a point $b\in \Delta '\setminus \{ 0\} $ is homotopic with fixed endpoints to the $k$th power of the circle around $0$ through $b$, for some integer $k$. Indeed, lifting the argument continuously along the loop, we write the loop as $\exp $ of a path in the half-plane $H=\{ \zeta :\operatorname{Re}\zeta <\log r\} $ from some $\zeta _0$ to $\zeta _0+2\pi \mathrm ik$; the straight-line homotopy to the segment from $\zeta _0$ to $\zeta _0+2\pi \mathrm ik$ stays in the convex set $H$. Let $\sigma $ be the permutation of the fiber over $b$ obtained by lifting the circle. Lifting any loop at $b$ therefore induces a power of $\sigma $. Since $C^*$ is connected, any two points of the fiber over $b$ are joined by a path in $C^*$, whose image is a loop at $b$; hence the powers of $\sigma $ act transitively on the $q$ points of the fiber, and $\sigma $ is a cycle of length $q$.

The map $\exp \colon H\to \Delta '\setminus \{ 0\} $ lifts to a map $\Phi \colon H\to C^*$ with $\pi \circ \Phi =\exp $: lift along paths from a base point $\zeta _0$, starting at a chosen point of the fiber; since $H$ is convex, the endpoint does not depend on the path. The map $\Phi $ is holomorphic, because locally it is $\exp $ followed by the inverse of $\pi $ on a sheet. The vertical segment from $\zeta $ to $\zeta +2\pi \mathrm i$ is mapped by $\exp $ onto the circle through $\mathrm e^\zeta $; so $\Phi (\zeta +2\pi \mathrm i)=\sigma _\zeta (\Phi (\zeta ))$, where $\sigma _\zeta $ is the permutation of the fiber over $\mathrm e^\zeta $ obtained by lifting this circle. By the preceding paragraph, applied with $b=\mathrm e^\zeta $, it is a cycle of length $q$; hence $\Phi (\zeta +2\pi \mathrm iq)=\Phi (\zeta )$. Therefore

\[ \gamma (t)=\Phi (q\log t),\qquad 0<|t|<r^{1/q}, \]

does not depend on the branch of the logarithm; it is holomorphic, and $\pi (\gamma (t))=t^ q$. The coordinates of $\gamma $ are bounded, because $C$ lies in the bounded parametrization polydisc, so by the removable singularity theorem they extend holomorphically to $t=0$. Since $\pi $ is proper, $\gamma (t)$ stays in a compact subset of $C$ as $t\to 0$; hence $\gamma (0)\in C$, and $\gamma (0)=x$ because $\gamma (0)$ lies over $0$.

Let $\Delta =\{ |t|<r^{1/q}\} $. The map $\gamma \colon \Delta \to C$ is surjective: the points of $C$ over $z_0=\mathrm e^{\zeta }\neq 0$ are $\Phi (\zeta +2\pi \mathrm ik)=\gamma (\mathrm e^{(\zeta +2\pi \mathrm ik)/q})$, $0\leq k<q$, because $\sigma _\zeta $ is a $q$-cycle. It is injective: if $\gamma (t_1)=\gamma (t_2)$ with $t_1,t_2\neq 0$, then $t_1^ q=t_2^ q$, so $t_2=t_1\mathrm e^{2\pi \mathrm ik/q}$; writing $q\log t_1=\zeta $, we get $\gamma (t_2)=\Phi (\zeta +2\pi \mathrm ik)=\sigma _\zeta ^ k(\gamma (t_1))$, which forces $k\equiv 0$ modulo $q$. On $\Delta \setminus \{ 0\} $ the map $\gamma $ is a local biholomorphism into the manifold $C^*$, since $\pi \circ \gamma (t)=t^ q$ has nonzero derivative; being bijective, it is a biholomorphism of $\Delta \setminus \{ 0\} $ onto $C\setminus \{ x\} $.

Finally, let $X$ be a normal complex space of dimension one, and let $x\in X$; if $x$ is an isolated point, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise the germ $(X,x)$ has dimension one, and it is irreducible by the definition of normal points; let $\gamma \colon \Delta \to C$ be the map just constructed for a representative $C$ of this germ. Its inverse is holomorphic on the manifold $C\setminus \{ x\} $, which is contained in the regular locus, and it is bounded; so it is weakly holomorphic and, by normality, extends to a holomorphic function $\tau $ near $x$. Then $\tau \circ \gamma =\mathrm{id}$ on $\Delta \setminus \{ 0\} $, hence on $\Delta $, and $\gamma \circ \tau =\mathrm{id}$ on $C\setminus \{ x\} $, hence on $C$, because these maps are continuous and the punctured sets are dense. Thus $\gamma $ is an isomorphism of $\Delta $ onto a neighborhood of $x$, and $x$ is a regular point. $\square$

Proposition A.4.2. Let $(X,x)$ be an irreducible analytic germ of positive dimension, and let $(B,x)$ be an analytic subgerm different from $(X,x)$. There is a nonconstant holomorphic map $\gamma $ from a disc $\Delta $ centered at $0$ into $X$ with $\gamma (0)=x$ and $\gamma (t)\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus B$ for $t\neq 0$.

Proof. The germ of $B\cup X_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is again different from $(X,x)$, since $(X,x)$ is irreducible and $X_{\mathrm{sing}}$ has smaller dimension by corollary A.2.10(1). Since every analytic germ $(A,x)$ satisfies $V(I_{A,x})=(A,x)$, the ideals of these two germs differ, and we can choose a holomorphic germ $f$ that vanishes on $B\cup X_{\mathrm{sing}}$ but not identically on $X$. Take a local parametrization of $(X,x)$ over a polydisc $\Delta '\subset \mathbb C^ d$, $d\geq 1$, with covering locus $X_\delta $, and for $z'\in \Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $ let $N(z')$ be the product of the values of $f$ at the $q$ points of $X$ over $z'$. This function is holomorphic and bounded, so it extends holomorphically to $\Delta '$ by lemma A.1.7. It is not identically zero. Otherwise, choose a small ball in $\Delta '\setminus \{ \delta =0\} $ over which the $q$ points are given by holomorphic sections $s_1,\ldots ,s_ q$; the product of the functions $f\circ s_ i$ would vanish identically there, so one of these functions would vanish on an open set. Then $f$ would vanish on a nonempty open subset of the connected manifold $X_\delta $, hence on $X_\delta $ by the identity theorem, and on $X$ by density, which is not the case.

The function $g=N\delta $ is holomorphic and not identically zero on $\Delta '$. Choose a vector $v\in \mathbb C^ d$ at which the first nonzero homogeneous term of $g$ does not vanish, and $\rho >0$ such that $g(tv)\neq 0$ for $0<|t|<\rho $ and the ball $U=\{ |z'|<\rho |v|\} $ lies in $\Delta '$. Then $Y=X\cap \pi ^{-1}(\{ tv:|t|<\rho \} )$ is an analytic subset of $X\cap \pi ^{-1}(U)$. Over the punctured disc, $Y$ consists of points of $X_\delta $ at which $f\neq 0$, and it is a covering of the punctured disc; over $0$ it consists of the single point $x$. Thus $Y\setminus \{ x\} $ is a complex manifold of dimension one, and by properness of $\pi $ its points accumulate at $x$, so $x$ is not an isolated point of $Y$. Let $(C,x)$ be a branch of $(Y,x)$ of positive dimension. Its regular points near $x$ lie in the one-dimensional manifold $Y\setminus \{ x\} $, so $(C,x)$ has dimension one. Let $\gamma \colon \Delta \to C$ be the parametrization of proposition A.4.1. For $t\neq 0$, the point $\gamma (t)$ lies in $C\setminus \{ x\} \subset Y\setminus \{ x\} $, hence in $X_\delta \subset X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ and outside $\{ f=0\} \supset B$. $\square$

Corollary A.4.3. Let $f$ be a holomorphic function near a point $x$ of a complex space $X$ such that $|f|$ has a local maximum at $x$. Then $f$ is constant on each irreducible branch of $(X,x)$. Moreover, every compact analytic subset of an open subset of $\mathbb C^ N$ is finite.

Proof. Let $X_1$ be a branch of $(X,x)$. If $X_1$ has dimension zero, it is the point $x$. Otherwise, suppose that $f-f(x)$ does not vanish identically on $X_1$, and apply proposition A.4.2 to $X_1$ and its subgerm $X_1\cap \{ f=f(x)\} $. The holomorphic function $f\circ \gamma $ on the disc has a local maximum of its modulus at $0$, so it is constant by the maximum principle in one variable, and $f(\gamma (t))=f(x)$ for all $t$, contradicting the choice of $\gamma $.

For the second assertion, let $K$ be a compact analytic subset of an open set in $\mathbb C^ N$, and let $K_+$ be the set of its non-isolated points. The isolated points form an open subset of $K$, so $K_+$ is compact. Suppose that $K_+\neq \varnothing $, and let $y$ be a point of $K_+$ where $|z|^2$ attains its maximum on $K_+$. Since $y$ is not isolated, some branch of $(K,y)$ has positive dimension, and its small representatives, being pure-dimensional, consist of non-isolated points of $K$. So proposition A.4.2, applied to this branch and the subgerm $\{ y\} $, gives a nonconstant holomorphic curve $\gamma $ in $K_+$ with $\gamma (0)=y$. The function $|\gamma (t)|^2$ is subharmonic, with Laplacian $4\sum _ j|\gamma _ j'(t)|^2$, and it has a local maximum at $0$; by the maximum principle (proposition 3.1.4) it is constant near $0$, so its Laplacian vanishes, all $\gamma _ j'$ vanish, and $\gamma $ is constant, a contradiction. Hence $K_+=\varnothing $, and $K$ is compact and discrete, hence finite. $\square$

The surjectivity of finite maps onto balls (lemma A.4.5) rests on the following elementary fact.

Lemma A.4.4. Let $B\subset \mathbb R^ N$, $N\geq 2$, be an open ball, and let $E\subset B$ be relatively closed and $\mathcal H^{N-1}$-null. Then $B\setminus E$ is path connected.

Proof. Fix $a,b\in B\setminus E$. If $c\in B$ and the segment $[a,c]$ meets $E$ at a point $e=a+s(c-a)$, then $0<s\leq 1$, since $a\notin E$, so $c=a+t(e-a)$ with $t=1/s\geq 1$. We show that the set of such $c$ has $N$-dimensional Lebesgue measure zero. Exhaust $E$ by the compact sets $E_ k=E\cap \overline{B_ k}$, where $B_ k$ are concentric balls increasing to $B$. Since $a\notin E_ k$, the parameter $t$ is bounded on $\{ (e,t):e\in E_ k,\ a+t(e-a)\in B\} $, say by $T_ k$, and the map $(e,t)\mapsto a+t(e-a)$ is Lipschitz on $E_ k\times [1,T_ k]$. Cover $E_ k$ by balls of radii $r_ l$ with $\sum _ lr_ l^{N-1}$ arbitrarily small. The image of each product of such a ball with $[1,T_ k]$ is covered by at most $C_ k/r_ l$ balls of radius $C_ kr_ l$, whose total volume is at most $C'_ kr_ l^{N-1}$. Hence the image of $E_ k\times [1,T_ k]$ is Lebesgue null, and so is the union over $k$. The same holds with $b$ in place of $a$. Choose $c\in B$ outside both null sets. Then the path $[a,c]\cup [c,b]$ lies in the convex set $B$ and does not meet $E$. $\square$

Lemma A.4.5. Let $(X,x)$ be a pure $d$-dimensional analytic germ, and let $F\colon (X,x)\to (\mathbb C^ d,0)$ be a holomorphic map germ such that $x$ is an isolated point of $F^{-1}(0)$. Then $F$ has arbitrarily small representatives that map properly, with finite fibers, onto balls centered at $0$: for every neighborhood $V$ of $x$ there are an open neighborhood $X'\subset V$ of $x$ in a representative of $(X,x)$ and a ball $B_\varepsilon \subset \mathbb C^ d$ centered at $0$ such that $F\colon X'\to B_\varepsilon $ is proper, has finite fibers and is surjective. In particular, the image under $F$ of every neighborhood of $x$ in $X$ contains a neighborhood of $0$.

Proof. For $d=0$ the germ is the point $x$, and there is nothing to prove. Let $d\geq 1$, embed a representative $X$ in a local model $X\subset \Omega \subset \mathbb C^ N$ such that $X$ is pure $d$-dimensional and $F$ is the restriction of a holomorphic map on $\Omega $, also denoted $F$, and choose a closed ball $\overline{B(x,\rho )}\subset \Omega $, with $X\cap B(x,\rho )\subset V$, whose intersection with $X\cap F^{-1}(0)$ is $\{ x\} $. The compact set $X\cap \partial B(x,\rho )$ does not meet $F^{-1}(0)$, so $|F|$ has a positive minimum $\eta $ on it (if it is empty, put $\eta =\infty $). Let $B_\varepsilon $ be the ball of radius $\varepsilon <\eta $ about $0\in \mathbb C^ d$, and let $X'=X\cap B(x,\rho )\cap F^{-1}(B_\varepsilon )$. Then $F\colon X'\to B_\varepsilon $ is proper: the inverse image of a compact set $K\subset B_\varepsilon $ is $X\cap \overline{B(x,\rho )}\cap F^{-1}(K)$, which is compact and contains no point of the boundary sphere. Its fibers are compact analytic subsets of the open ball $B(x,\rho )$, hence finite by corollary A.4.3.

It remains to prove surjectivity. On each connected component of the manifold $X'_{\mathrm{reg}}$, which has dimension $d$, the differential of $F$ has rank $d$ at some point. Otherwise its maximal rank $r$ on some component would be smaller; near a point where the rank is $r$, the rank would be constantly $r$, and by lemma A.2.11, applied in a chart of $X'_{\mathrm{reg}}$, the fibers near this point would be submanifolds of positive dimension $d-r$, contradicting their finiteness.

Let $C$ be the union of $X'_{\mathrm{sing}}$ and of the set of regular points where the rank of $F$ is less than $d$. In the local model, $C$ is the union of $X'_{\mathrm{sing}}$ and of the common zero set in $X'$ of the minors of size $N$ of the matrix formed by the differentials of local generators of the ideal of $X$ and the rows of $\mathrm dF$; so $C$ is analytic. It contains no branch of $X'$ at any point. Indeed, each branch contains a nonempty open subset of $X'_{\mathrm{reg}}$, consisting of regular points of the branch outside the other branches (corollary A.2.4), and on each component of $X'_{\mathrm{reg}}$ the points of rank less than $d$ form a proper analytic subset, which is nowhere dense by lemma A.1.6. Hence $C$ has dimension at most $d-1$ by proposition A.2.7.

Its image $E=F(C)$ is closed in $B_\varepsilon $ by properness, and it is $\mathcal H^{2d-1}$-null. Indeed, by corollary A.2.10(2), $C$ is a finite union of complex submanifolds of open subsets of $\Omega $ of dimensions at most $d-1$, on each of which $F$ has rank at most $d-1$, so lemma A.2.12(1) applies.

Over $B_\varepsilon \setminus E$, the map $F$ is a local biholomorphism at every point of its source, by proposition 7.1.2(1) applied in charts of $X'_{\mathrm{reg}}$, and it is proper; so its image is open and closed in $B_\varepsilon \setminus E$. This image is nonempty: at a regular point of rank $d$, the image of a neighborhood is open, and it is not contained in $E$, which is Lebesgue null because it is $\mathcal H^{2d-1}$-null. Since $B_\varepsilon \setminus E$ is connected by lemma A.4.4, the image contains $B_\varepsilon \setminus E$, which is dense in $B_\varepsilon $; being closed in $B_\varepsilon $, it equals $B_\varepsilon $. $\square$

Proposition A.4.6. Let $X$ be a pure $d$-dimensional complex space, and let $f$ be a holomorphic function on $X$ that is not identically zero on any branch of $(X,y)$, for every $y\in X$. Then the zero set of $f$ is either empty or pure of dimension $d-1$.

Proof. If $d=0$, then $f$ has no zeros. Let $d\geq 1$ and let $x$ be a zero of $f$. We first show that $\dim (\{ f=0\} ,x)=d-1$. For each branch $X_0$ of $(X,x)$, the germ $\{ f=0\} \cap X_0$ is a subgerm of $X_0$ different from it, so $\dim (\{ f=0\} ,x)\leq d-1$ by proposition A.2.7. Fix a branch $X_0$, and choose linear forms $\ell _1,\ldots ,\ell _{d-1}$ on the ambient space of a local model, vanishing at $x$, inductively such that $\ell _ j$ does not vanish identically on any positive-dimensional branch of the germ at $x$ of $\{ f=0\} \cap X_0\cap \{ \ell _1=\cdots =\ell _{j-1}=0\} $. At each step this excludes finitely many proper linear subspaces, and, by proposition A.2.7, it lowers the dimension of this germ by at least one as long as it is positive. Since the initial dimension is at most $d-1$, the point $x$ is isolated in the common zero set of $\Phi =(f,\ell _1,\ldots ,\ell _{d-1})$ on $X_0$.

Suppose that $\dim (\{ f=0\} ,x)<d-1$; then $d\geq 2$, and by upper semicontinuity $\dim (\{ f=0\} ,y)\leq d-2$ for all zeros $y$ of $f$ near $x$. By lemma A.4.5, applied to the germ $(X_0,x)$, which is pure-dimensional, there is a neighborhood $X'_0$ of $x$ in a small representative of $X_0$, on which this bound holds, such that $\Phi $ maps $X'_0$ onto a ball $B\subset \mathbb C^ d$ centered at $0$. Hence $\Phi $ maps $\{ f=0\} \cap X'_0$ onto the set $B\cap \{ w_1=0\} $, which has positive $(2d-2)$-dimensional Lebesgue measure; here $w_1$ is the first coordinate of $\mathbb C^ d$. On the other hand, by corollary A.2.10(2), $\{ f=0\} \cap X'_0$ is a finite union of complex submanifolds of dimensions at most $d-2$ of open subsets of the ambient space, on each of which $\Phi $ has rank at most $d-2$; by lemma A.2.12(1), its image under $\Phi $ is $\mathcal H^{2d-2}$-null, hence Lebesgue null in $\{ w_1=0\} \cong \mathbb R^{2d-2}$. This contradiction shows that $\dim (\{ f=0\} ,x)=d-1$.

Now let $V_1$ be a branch of $(\{ f=0\} ,x)$. By corollary A.2.4, there are regular points $y$ of $V_1$ arbitrarily close to $x$ that do not lie on the other branches. Near such a point, $\{ f=0\} $ coincides with $V_1$, which is a manifold of dimension $\dim V_1$ by purity. The hypotheses hold at $y$, so the first part gives $\dim (\{ f=0\} ,y)=d-1$, and therefore $\dim V_1=d-1$. $\square$

A.5 Blowups and resolution

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

Blowing up an ideal replaces its zero set by the space of directions of its generators, and makes the pulled-back ideal locally principal. We construct blowups of complex spaces along coherent ideals, prove their universal property, and compute blowups of manifolds along submanifolds in explicit charts; these charts give the transformation rules for ideals of submanifolds needed in the next sections. At the end of the section we state the existence theorems for resolutions and principalizations and deduce that log resolutions exist.

Definition A.5.1. Let $X$ be a complex space and let $\mathfrak a\subset \mathcal O_ X$ be a coherent ideal sheaf that is not identically zero on any branch of $(X,x)$, for every $x\in X$. On an open set where $\mathfrak a$ is generated by $f_0,\ldots ,f_ r$, the blowup $\operatorname {Bl}_{\mathfrak a}X$ is the closure, with its reduced structure, of the graph

\[ \bigl \{ \bigl (x,[f_0(x):\cdots :f_ r(x)]\bigr ):x\in X\setminus V(\mathfrak a)\bigr \} \subset X\times \mathbb P^ r. \]

Here $\mathbb P^ r$ is the space of nonzero vectors in $\mathbb C^{r+1}$ modulo multiplication by nonzero scalars, with the charts $\{ \xi _ i\neq 0\} $ and the affine coordinates $\xi _ j/\xi _ i$. It is compact, because every point has a representative on the compact unit sphere and the quotient map is continuous. The projection to $X$ is denoted by $\pi $.

An ideal sheaf on a complex space is invertible if it is locally generated by one nonzerodivisor. For a holomorphic map $g\colon Y\to X$ we write $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_ Y$ for the ideal sheaf generated by the pullbacks $f\circ g$ of local sections $f$ of $\mathfrak a$.

Proposition A.5.2. The blowup is a complex space, independent of the chosen generators up to canonical isomorphism. Its projection $\pi $ is proper and bimeromorphic, and $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_{\operatorname {Bl}_{\mathfrak a}X}$ is an invertible ideal. If $g\colon Y\to X$ is a holomorphic map from a complex space $Y$ such that $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_ Y$ is invertible, then $g$ has a unique holomorphic lift $\widetilde g\colon Y\to \operatorname {Bl}_{\mathfrak a}X$, that is, $\pi \circ \widetilde g=g$.

Proof. In $X\times \mathbb P^ r$ consider the analytic set $Z$ defined by the equations of $X$ and by $f_ i\xi _ j=f_ j\xi _ i$ for all $i,j$; in the chart $\{ \xi _ i\neq 0\} $ these are the holomorphic equations $f_ j=f_ i\cdot (\xi _ j/\xi _ i)$ and $f_ k\cdot (\xi _ j/\xi _ i)=f_ j\cdot (\xi _ k/\xi _ i)$. Over a point $x\notin V(\mathfrak a)$, the only point of $Z$ is $(x,[f(x)])$, because $\xi $ must be proportional to the nonzero vector $f(x)$. Thus the graph is the part of $Z$ outside the analytic set $\pi ^{-1}(V(\mathfrak a))$, and by lemma A.2.5 its closure is analytic; near each point it is the union of the branches of $Z$ not contained in $\pi ^{-1}(V(\mathfrak a))$. It is closed in $X\times \mathbb P^ r$, and $\mathbb P^ r$ is compact, so $\pi $ is proper. Over $X\setminus V(\mathfrak a)$ the blowup is the graph, and $\pi $ is an isomorphism there, with inverse $x\mapsto (x,[f(x)])$, which is holomorphic because on $\{ f_ i\neq 0\} $ the affine coordinates $f_ j/f_ i$ are holomorphic. The set $V(\mathfrak a)$ is nowhere dense in $X$, by corollary A.2.4, and the graph is dense in the blowup; so $\pi $ is bimeromorphic.

On the chart $\{ \xi _ i\neq 0\} $ of the blowup, the relations $f_ j\circ \pi =(f_ i\circ \pi )\cdot (\xi _ j/\xi _ i)$ hold on the graph, hence everywhere, so the pulled-back ideal is generated by $f_ i\circ \pi $ there. This generator is not identically zero on any branch of the blowup at any point of the chart, because the graph points of the chart, at which $f_ i\circ \pi \neq 0$, are dense in every such branch. Hence it is a nonzerodivisor, and the pulled-back ideal is invertible.

Let $g\colon Y\to X$ be as in the statement. Near a point of $Y$, let $d$ be a nonzerodivisor generating $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_ Y$, and write $f_ i\circ g=db_ i$. Writing $d=\sum _ ic_ i\, (f_ i\circ g)=d\sum _ ic_ ib_ i$ and cancelling the nonzerodivisor $d$, we get $\sum _ ic_ ib_ i=1$; so the $b_ i$ have no common zero, and $y\mapsto (g(y),[b_0(y):\cdots :b_ r(y)])$ is a holomorphic map into $X\times \mathbb P^ r$. Where $d\neq 0$, some $f_ i(g(y))$ is nonzero, and this point is the graph point over $g(y)$; since $\{ d=0\} $ is nowhere dense, the whole image lies in the closure of the graph. Replacing $d$ by $ud$ for a unit $u$ replaces $b$ by $u^{-1}b$ and does not change the projective point, so these local lifts glue. A lift of $g$ is uniquely determined over $Y\setminus g^{-1}(V(\mathfrak a))=Y\setminus \{ d=0\} $, where $\pi $ is an isomorphism, hence everywhere by density.

Finally, the blowups defined by two families of generators both pull back $\mathfrak a$ to an invertible ideal. By the universal property, each maps to the other over $X$, and both compositions are the identity by uniqueness. These canonical isomorphisms also glue the local constructions on overlaps, compatibly on triple overlaps by the same uniqueness. The glued space is Hausdorff: points with different images in $X$ are separated by the inverse images of disjoint neighborhoods, and points with the same image lie in one of the local constructions, which are Hausdorff. Since $X$ has a countable basis, countably many of the open sets over which the local constructions are made cover $X$, and each local construction, an analytic subset of the product of a local model with $\mathbb P^ r$, has a countable basis; hence so has the glued space. Being locally compact and Hausdorff with a countable basis, it is paracompact. $\square$

An effective Cartier divisor $D$ on a complex space $X$ is given by an invertible ideal sheaf, written $\mathcal O_ X(-D)$; a local generator is a local equation of $D$, determined up to a unit. We write $\mathcal O_ X(D)$ for the inverse invertible sheaf, generated locally by the reciprocal of a local equation. Addition of effective Cartier divisors corresponds to multiplication of their ideals. If $g\colon Y\to X$ is holomorphic and the local equations of $D$ composed with $g$ are nonzerodivisors, they define the pullback $g^*D$, with $\mathcal O_ Y(-g^*D)=\mathcal O_ X(-D)\mathcal O_ Y$; restriction to an analytic subset is a special case.

On a complex manifold, an effective divisor has simple normal crossings support, and is then called a simple normal crossings divisor, if each irreducible component of its support is smooth and near each point there are holomorphic coordinates in which the support is $\{ z_1\cdots z_ k=0\} $. Its multiplicities may be arbitrary positive integers; the condition concerns only the support. A submanifold $C$ has normal crossings with such a divisor if near each point of $C$ there are coordinates in which the support of the divisor is a union of coordinate hyperplanes and $C$ is a coordinate subspace.

For a proper bimeromorphic map between complex manifolds, an effective divisor is called exceptional if each irreducible component of its support has image of codimension at least two; these images are analytic by theorem A.6.2, proved in the next section.

Proposition A.5.3. Let $C$ be a closed submanifold of a complex manifold $M$, of positive codimension at each of its points, and let $\pi \colon M'\to M$ be the blowup of $M$ along $\mathcal I_ C$. Then $M'$ is a complex manifold, the inverse image $E=\pi ^{-1}(C)$ is a smooth hypersurface with $\mathcal I_ C\mathcal O_{M'}=\mathcal O_{M'}(-E)$, and the fiber of $\pi $ over a point of $C$ at which $C$ has codimension $r$ is $\mathbb P^{r-1}$. If $C$ is a hypersurface, then $\pi $ is the identity and $E=C$. Explicitly, let $(z,w)$, $w=(w_1,\ldots ,w_ r)$, be holomorphic coordinates on an open set $U\subset M$ with $C\cap U=\{ w=0\} $. Then $\pi ^{-1}(U)$ is covered by $r$ charts, one for each pivot $i\in \{ 1,\ldots ,r\} $, with coordinates $(z,t,v)$, $v=(v_ j)_{j\neq i}$, in which

A.8
\begin{equation} \pi (z,t,v)=(z,t\tilde v),\qquad \tilde v_ i=1,\quad \tilde v_ j=v_ j\ (j\neq i), \end{equation}

and $E=\{ t=0\} $; the Jacobian determinant of $\pi $ in these coordinates is $\pm t^{r-1}$.

Let $W\subset M$ be a closed submanifold containing $C$, such that $C$ has positive codimension in $W$ at each of its points. Then the strict transform $W'$ of $W$, the closure of $\pi ^{-1}(W\setminus C)$, is the blowup $\operatorname {Bl}_ CW$ of $W$ along $\mathcal I_ C\mathcal O_ W$, and

A.9
\begin{equation} \mathcal I_ W\mathcal O_{M'}=\mathcal O_{M'}(-E)\mathcal I_{W'}. \end{equation}

In holomorphic coordinates $(z,w)$, $w=(w_1,\ldots ,w_ s)$, on an open set $U\subset M$ with $W\cap U=\{ w=0\} $ and $C\cap U=\{ w=0,\ z_1=\cdots =z_ k=0\} $, which exist near every point of $C$, the center is generated by $z_1,\ldots ,z_ k,w_1,\ldots ,w_ s$, and the strict transform $W'$ meets only the charts whose pivot is one of $z_1,\ldots ,z_ k$. The chart with pivot $z_ i$ has coordinates $(z'',t,u,v)$, where $z''$ consists of the coordinates $z_ l$ with $l>k$, and is given by $z_ i=t$, $z_ l=tu_ l$ for $l\leq k$, $l\neq i$, and $w_ j=tv_ j$; in it, $W'=\{ v=0\} $. If instead $W$ is a closed submanifold disjoint from $C$, the strict transform $W'=\pi ^{-1}(W)$ maps isomorphically onto $W$, and $\mathcal I_ W\mathcal O_{M'}=\mathcal I_{W'}$.

Proof. Let $(z,w)$ be coordinates on an open set $U$ as in the first part of the statement; such coordinates exist near every point of $C$. On $U$ the ideal $\mathcal I_ C$ is generated by $w_1,\ldots ,w_ r$, by lemma A.2.9. Let $Z\subset U\times \mathbb P^{r-1}$ be the analytic set defined by the equations $w_ j\xi _ i=w_ i\xi _ j$. Over a point of $U\setminus C$, its only point is $((z,w),[w])$, because $\xi $ must be proportional to the nonzero vector $w$; so the part of $Z$ over $U\setminus C$ is the graph of definition A.5.1, whose closure is the blowup of $U$. In the chart $\{ \xi _ i\neq 0\} $, the equations $w_ j\xi _ i=w_ i\xi _ j$ say that $w_ j=w_ iv_ j$ with $v_ j=\xi _ j/\xi _ i$, $j\neq i$; hence this part of $Z$ is the graph of the map $(z,t,v)\mapsto (z,w)$ with $w_ i=t$ and $w_ j=tv_ j$, $j\neq i$. It is a manifold with coordinates $(z,t,v)$, in which the graph part is $\{ t\neq 0\} $, a dense subset. Hence these charts cover the blowup, $M'$ is a manifold, and $\mathcal I_ C\mathcal O_{M'}$ is generated by $t$ in each chart. Thus $E=\{ t=0\} $ is a smooth hypersurface.

With $w_ i$ listed first among the coordinates $w$, the Jacobian matrix of (A.8) is block lower triangular with diagonal blocks $I$, $1$ and $tI_{r-1}$, because $\partial w_ i/\partial t=1$, $\partial w_ j/\partial t=v_ j$ and $\partial w_ j/\partial v_ l=t\delta _{jl}$ for $j,l\neq i$, while $z$ does not depend on $(t,v)$ and $w$ does not depend on $z$; so the Jacobian determinant of $\pi $ is $\pm t^{r-1}$. The fiber over a point $(z,0)$ is obtained by gluing the coordinate charts $v\in \mathbb C^{r-1}$, that is, it is $\mathbb P^{r-1}$. If $r=1$, there is one chart, and the coordinates and the map are unchanged.

For the second part, choose coordinates $(z,w)$ near a point of $C$ with $W=\{ w_1=\cdots =w_ s=0\} $ and $C=\{ w_1=\cdots =w_ s=z_1=\cdots =z_ k=0\} $, where $k\geq 1$ is the codimension of $C$ in $W$. Such coordinates exist: take coordinates $(x,w)$ with $W=\{ w=0\} $, then holomorphic coordinates $z=\phi (x)$ on $W$ near the point in which $C$ is a coordinate plane, and use $(\phi (x),w)$, whose Jacobian matrix is block triangular and invertible, so that these are holomorphic coordinates near the point by proposition 7.1.2(1). The center is now generated by $z_1,\ldots ,z_ k,w_1,\ldots ,w_ s$. In a chart whose pivot is $z_ i$, we have $z_ i=t$, $z_ l=tu_ l$ for $l\neq i$, $l\leq k$, and $w_ j=tv_ j$; here $\pi ^{-1}(W\setminus C)=\{ v=0,\ t\neq 0\} $, so $W'=\{ v=0\} $ in this chart, and

\[ \mathcal I_ W\mathcal O_{M'}=(tv_1,\ldots ,tv_ s)=(t)(v_1,\ldots ,v_ s)=\mathcal O_{M'}(-E)\mathcal I_{W'}. \]

In a chart whose pivot is $w_ i$, we have $w_ i=t$, so $\pi ^{-1}(W)$ lies in $\{ t=0\} =E$. Moreover, $W'$ does not meet this chart, because the points of $\pi ^{-1}(W\setminus C)$, and therefore those of its closure, have projective coordinates $\xi $ whose components corresponding to $w_1,\ldots ,w_ s$ vanish. The pulled-back ideal contains $t$ and is therefore $(t)=\mathcal O_{M'}(-E)$, which proves (A.9) in this chart as well. In the charts of the first kind, $W'$ has the coordinates $(t,u)$ together with the remaining $z$-coordinates, and $\pi |_{W'}$ is given by the formulas of the blowup of $W$ along $C$; so $W'=\operatorname {Bl}_ CW$. Finally, if $C$ is disjoint from $W$, then $\pi $ is an isomorphism over the neighborhood $M\setminus C$ of $W$. $\square$

Theorem A.5.4 (Resolution of singularities). Every reduced complex space $X$ has a proper bimeromorphic map $\pi \colon M\to X$ from a complex manifold $M$ that is a composite of blowups with smooth centers and an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. The divisor formed by the inverse images in $M$ of the blowup centers has simple normal crossings. The sequence of blowups is finite when $X$ is compact, and finite over each relatively compact open subset of $X$ in general.

Theorem A.5.5 (Principalization). Let $M$ be a complex manifold, let $\mathfrak a\subset \mathcal O_ M$ be a coherent ideal that is not identically zero on any connected component of $M$, and let $D$ be a simple normal crossings divisor on $M$, possibly empty. Then there is a sequence of blowups $\sigma _ i\colon M_ i\to M_{i-1}$, $i\geq 1$, with $M_0=M$ and smooth centers $C_ i\subset M_{i-1}$, finite over each relatively compact open subset of $M$, with the following properties. Put $\mathfrak a_0=\mathfrak a$ and $E_ i=\sigma _ i^{-1}(C_ i)$, and, for $i\geq 0$, call the union of the strict transforms of $D,E_1,\ldots ,E_ i$ in $M_ i$ the accumulated divisor on $M_ i$. Each center $C_ i$ is contained in $V(\mathfrak a_{i-1})$ and has normal crossings with the accumulated divisor on $M_{i-1}$; the ideal

A.10
\begin{equation} \mathfrak a_ i=\mathcal O_{M_ i}(E_ i)\, \mathfrak a_{i-1}\mathcal O_{M_ i} \end{equation}

is holomorphic; and over each relatively compact open subset of $M$ the last of these ideals is the unit ideal. The accumulated divisors have simple normal crossings, and the composite is an isomorphism off $V(\mathfrak a)$.

In the notation of [BM08], theorem A.5.5 is the theorem for marked ideals with mark $1$ and $N=M$. The cosupport of $\mathfrak a_ i$ is $V(\mathfrak a_ i)$, and the transform (A.10) divides by a single exceptional factor at each step, even where $\mathfrak a_{i-1}$ vanishes to higher order along the center. If $M$ is compact, the sequence is finite, say with $t$ steps, and iterating (A.10) gives

\[ \mathfrak a\mathcal O_{M_ t}=\mathcal O_{M_ t}(-H), \qquad H=\sum _{i=1}^ t(\sigma _{i+1}\circ \cdots \circ \sigma _ t)^*E_ i, \]

where the empty composition for $i=t$ is the identity, so that the last term is $E_ t$; the same holds over every relatively compact open subset of $M$.

The divisor $H$ is effective, and its support has simple normal crossings. Indeed, for a blowup $\sigma $ with center $C$ and exceptional divisor $E$, and a closed set $D$, we have $\sigma ^{-1}(D)\subset D'\cup E$, where $D'$ is the strict transform, the closure of $\sigma ^{-1}(D\setminus C)$. By induction, the inverse image in $M_ t$ of each $E_ i$ is contained in the accumulated divisor on $M_ t$. The support of $H$ is the union of these inverse images, the zero set of a local equation of $H$, which is not identically zero on any component of $M_ t$; so it is empty or pure of codimension one, by proposition A.4.6. By corollary A.2.14(4) and (3), each of its irreducible components is contained in, and hence equal to, an irreducible component of the accumulated divisor. Thus the support of $H$ is a union of some components of a divisor whose support has simple normal crossings.

Near a point, in coordinates in which the support of the accumulated divisor is $\{ z_1\cdots z_ k=0\} $, the germ of each of its components through the point is the germ of a connected submanifold of codimension one, hence irreducible by lemma A.2.9. It is contained in the union of the germs of the hyperplanes $\{ z_ l=0\} $, so it is contained in one of them and, by proposition A.2.7, equal to it. Hence the support of $H$ is near this point the union of some of these hyperplanes, and it has simple normal crossings.

Let $\mathfrak a$ be a coherent ideal on a complex space $X$, not identically zero on any branch of any germ of $X$. A log resolution of $\mathfrak a$ is a proper bimeromorphic map $\pi \colon Y\to X$ from a complex manifold $Y$ that is a composite of blowups with smooth centers, finite over each relatively compact open subset of $X$, such that $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_ Y=\mathcal O_ Y(-D)$ for an effective divisor $D$ on $Y$ whose support has simple normal crossings.

Log resolutions exist. Let $\rho \colon M\to X$ be a resolution given by theorem A.5.4. The zero set of the pulled-back ideal $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_ M$ is $\rho ^{-1}(V(\mathfrak a))$, which is nowhere dense, because $\rho $ is bimeromorphic and $V(\mathfrak a)$ is nowhere dense in $X$ (lemma A.3.1); so $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_ M$ is not identically zero on any component of $M$. We principalize it by theorem A.5.5, taking as the given simple normal crossings divisor the exceptional divisor $D_\rho $ of $\rho $, formed by the inverse images of its blowup centers, and obtain $\sigma \colon Y\to M$ with $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_ Y=\mathcal O_ Y(-D)$, $D=H$.

The argument given above for the support of $H$ applies also to $\sigma ^{-1}(D_\rho )$. This set lies in the accumulated divisor by the same induction, and it is the zero set of the pullback of a local equation of $D_\rho $. This pullback is not identically zero on any component of $Y$: the map $\sigma $ is an isomorphism off the support of $D$, which is the nowhere dense set $\sigma ^{-1}(V(\mathfrak a\mathcal O_ M))$, so $\sigma $ is bimeromorphic and $\sigma ^{-1}(D_\rho )$ is nowhere dense by lemma A.3.1. Hence the support of $D$ together with $\sigma ^{-1}(D_\rho )$ is a union of irreducible components of the accumulated divisor, and it has simple normal crossings. The composite $\pi =\rho \circ \sigma $ is proper, and it is bimeromorphic: it is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus V(\mathfrak a)$, the set $\sigma ^{-1}(\rho ^{-1}(V(\mathfrak a)))$ is the support of $D$, a hypersurface, and $\sigma ^{-1}(\rho ^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}}))$ is nowhere dense, because $\rho ^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})$ is nowhere dense and $\sigma $ is an isomorphism off the support of $D$.

When $X$ is a manifold, the centers of a log resolution of $\mathfrak a$ are closed submanifolds of positive codimension at each of their points, because the ideal of a center must not vanish identically on any component (definition A.5.1). No resolution step is needed then: theorem A.5.5 with the empty divisor gives a log resolution that is an isomorphism off $V(\mathfrak a)$. These are the log resolutions used in chapter 12, chapter 13; in section 13.1, a log resolution of the ideal in the type of a function with analytic singularities is also called a log resolution of that function.

A.6 Proper images and relative principalization

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

We now show that the image of a proper holomorphic map is analytic (Remmert's theorem). It is proved together with the Remmert–Stein theorem, which says that an analytic set whose branches all have large dimension remains analytic after adding its limit points on an analytic set of smaller dimension. In this book the mapping theorem is applied mainly to images of exceptional sets of compositions of blowups. We then prove a relative principalization theorem: one sequence of blowups principalizes an ideal on a compact manifold and, at the same time, its restriction to a given submanifold, and the ideal of the submanifold transforms in a controlled way.

Theorem A.6.1 (Remmert–Stein). Let $X$ be a complex space, let $p\geq 0$ be an integer, and let $A\subset X$ be an analytic subset of dimension at most $p$. Let $Z$ be an analytic subset of $X\setminus A$ all of whose local branches have dimension greater than $p$. Then the closure of $Z$ in $X$ is analytic.

Theorem A.6.2 (Remmert). The image of a proper holomorphic map between complex spaces is analytic.

Proof. We prove the two theorems together by induction on dimension. Let $R_ m$ denote the assertion of theorem A.6.1 for sets $Z$ of dimension at most $m$, and $P_ m$ the assertion of theorem A.6.2 for maps whose source has dimension at most $m$. Both assertions are local on $X$. A proper map has compact inverse image over a compact neighborhood of a point of the target, and near this compact set its source has finite dimension; so, locally on the target, every proper map falls under some $P_ m$. The assertion $P_0$ holds because a source of dimension zero is discrete, so the image of a proper map from it is closed and locally finite, hence analytic. The assertion $R_0$ holds trivially: a set $Z$ of dimension zero has no branches of dimension greater than $p\geq 0$, so $Z=\varnothing $. We show that $P_{m-1}$ implies $R_ m$, and that $R_ m$ and $P_{m-1}$ imply $P_ m$.

Step 1: $P_{m-1}$ implies $R_ m$; reductions. Since the assertion is local, we may assume that $X$ is an analytic subset of a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb C^ N$; then $Z$ is an analytic subset of $\Omega \setminus A$, and its closure in $X$ is its closure in $\Omega $. We first reduce to the case where $A$ is a submanifold of dimension $p$, by induction on $p$. Let $A''$ be the union of $A_{\mathrm{sing}}$ and of the irreducible components of $A$ of dimension less than $p$; it is analytic of dimension at most $p-1$ (empty if $p=0$), and $A\setminus A''$ is a closed submanifold of $\Omega \setminus A''$ of dimension $p$. Suppose that the closure $Z_1$ of $Z$ in $\Omega \setminus A''$ is analytic. No branch $B$ of a germ of $Z_1$ is contained in $A$: since $Z$ is dense in $Z_1$, the branches not contained in $A$ already have union $Z_1$ near the base point, and the decomposition into branches is irredundant. Hence $B\setminus A$ is dense in $B$, and at its regular points outside the other branches $B$ coincides with $Z$, whose branches have dimension greater than $p$. Hence all branches of $Z_1$ have dimension greater than $p>p-1$, and by the induction hypothesis on $p$ the closure of $Z_1$ in $\Omega $, which is the closure of $Z$, is analytic.

Thus we may assume that $A$ is a $p$-dimensional submanifold. Fix a point of $A$ in the closure of $Z$; after a holomorphic change of coordinates it is the origin, and near it $A$ is a linear subspace $L$ of dimension $p$. Finally, the irreducible components of $Z$ are pure-dimensional by corollary A.2.14(1), and their dimensions exceed $p$, because each component contains regular points of $Z$ near which $Z$ coincides with it. The union of the components of a given dimension $s$ is analytic in $\Omega \setminus A$, by corollary A.2.13, and only the finitely many values $p<s\leq m$ occur; so we may assume that $Z$ is pure of dimension $s$, with $p<s\leq m$. We shall present $Z$ near the origin as a branched covering over an open subset of $\mathbb C^ s$ and write down equations for its closure.

Choice of a projection. We choose linear forms $\xi _1,\ldots ,\xi _ s$ on $\mathbb C^ N$ with the following properties: (i) for each $j<s$, the form $\xi _{j+1}$ does not vanish identically on any irreducible component of $Z\cap \{ \xi _1=\cdots =\xi _ j=0\} $; (ii) the restrictions of $\xi _1,\ldots ,\xi _ p$ to $L$ are linearly independent; (iii) at a point $z_\alpha $ chosen in each connected component $Z^\circ _\alpha $ of the manifold $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$, the restrictions of $\xi _1,\ldots ,\xi _ s$ to the tangent space of $Z$ are linearly independent.

We choose the forms one at a time. Suppose that $\xi _1,\ldots ,\xi _ j$, $j<s$, have been chosen and that $Z_ j=Z\cap \{ \xi _1=\cdots =\xi _ j=0\} $ is pure of dimension $s-j$, which holds for $j=0$. Since $\Omega $ has a countable basis, $Z_ j$ has countably many irreducible components and $Z_{\mathrm{reg}}$ has countably many connected components $Z^\circ _\alpha $, by corollary A.2.13. The three properties exclude, for $\xi _{j+1}$, countably many proper linear subspaces of the dual space of $\mathbb C^ N$: by (i), the forms vanishing on a given component of $Z_ j$, which has positive dimension $s-j$; by (ii), if $j<p$, the forms whose restriction to $L$ is a combination of those of $\xi _1,\ldots ,\xi _ j$; and by (iii), the forms whose restriction to the $s$-dimensional tangent space of $Z$ at $z_\alpha $ is a combination of those of $\xi _1,\ldots ,\xi _ j$. A countable union of proper linear subspaces has Lebesgue measure zero, so $\xi _{j+1}$ can be chosen. Then $Z_{j+1}$ is empty or pure of dimension $s-j-1$, by proposition A.4.6; here we use that a holomorphic function vanishing identically on a local branch of $Z_ j$ vanishes on the irreducible component of $Z_ j$ containing this branch. Indeed, the branch contains regular points of $Z_ j$ outside the other branches, by corollary A.2.4, and hence a nonempty open subset of the regular locus of this component, so corollary A.2.13 applies.

Replacing $\xi _ j$, $j>p$, by $\xi _ j-\sum _{i\leq p}a_{ji}\xi _ i$ with suitable constants, we may assume that $\xi _{p+1},\ldots ,\xi _ s$ vanish on $L$; this triangular change does not affect the zero sets $\{ \xi _1=\cdots =\xi _ j=0\} $ or the independence at the points $z_\alpha $. Complete $\xi _1,\ldots ,\xi _ s$ by linear forms vanishing on $L$ to linear coordinates $z=(z',z'')$ with $z'=(\xi _1,\ldots ,\xi _ s)\in \mathbb C^ s$. Then $L=\mathbb C^ p\times \{ 0\} $, and we let $\pi (z)=z'$.

A proper projection. By (i), the set $Z\cap \pi ^{-1}(0)$ is discrete in $\Omega \setminus A$, and by (ii), $A\cap \pi ^{-1}(0)=\{ 0\} $ near $0$. Choose a ball $B''$ centered at $0$ in the $z''$-space whose boundary sphere does not meet $Z\cap \pi ^{-1}(0)$; this excludes countably many radii. The set $Z\cup A$ is closed near $0$ and does not meet the compact set $\{ 0\} \times \partial B''$, so there is a ball $B'\subset \mathbb C^ s$ centered at $0$ with $(Z\cup A)\cap (\overline{B'}\times \partial B'')=\varnothing $. Let $L'=B'\cap (\mathbb C^ p\times \{ 0\} )$, so that $A\cap (B'\times B'')$ lies over $L'$. The projection of $Z\cap (B'\times B'')$ over $B'\setminus L'$ is proper, because over a compact set $K\subset B'\setminus L'$ it is the compact set $(Z\cup A)\cap (K\times \overline{B''})$, and its fibers are compact analytic subsets of $B''$, hence finite by corollary A.4.3.

The exceptional sets in the base. Let $S_1$ be the set of singular points of $Z$ over $B'\setminus L'$. It is analytic of dimension at most $s-1\leq m-1$, and its projection is proper, so $S_1'=\pi (S_1)$ is analytic in $B'\setminus L'$ by $P_{m-1}$. Over $B'\setminus (L'\cup S_1')$ the set $Z$ is a manifold of dimension $s$; let $S_2$ be the set of its points where $\pi |_ Z$ has rank less than $s$. It is analytic, given locally by a Jacobian determinant. Each connected component of this manifold is an open subset of some $Z^\circ _\alpha $, and on the connected manifold $Z^\circ _\alpha $ the set where the rank of $\pi $ is less than $s$ is a proper analytic subset, since the rank is $s$ at $z_\alpha $ by (iii); so it is nowhere dense, and $S_2$ has dimension at most $s-1$. By $P_{m-1}$, $S_2'=\pi (S_2)$ is analytic in $B'\setminus (L'\cup S_1')$.

By lemma A.2.12(3), the sets $S_1'$ and $S_2'$ have dimension at most $s-1$, so they are nowhere dense in the open subsets of $\mathbb C^ s$ in which they are analytic. Applying lemma A.1.7 first to $L'$, then to $S_1'$ and then to $S_2'$, we see that

\[ G=B'\setminus (L'\cup S_1'\cup S_2') \]

is connected; applying it in the opposite order, we see that every bounded holomorphic function on $G$ extends holomorphically to $B'$.

The set $Z\cap \pi ^{-1}(G)$ is dense in $Z\cap (B'\times B'')$. Indeed, on each irreducible component $Z_\alpha =\overline{Z^\circ _\alpha }$, the functions $\xi _{p+1},\ldots ,\xi _ s$ do not all vanish identically, because $\pi |_ Z$ is open near $z_\alpha $ and $s>p$; so $Z_\alpha \cap \pi ^{-1}(L')$ is a proper analytic subset of $Z_\alpha $, and it is nowhere dense by corollary A.2.14(2). Moreover, the points at which $\pi |_ Z$ has rank $s$ are dense in $Z$, by the previous paragraph, and near such points $\pi $ is open; hence $\pi ^{-1}(S_1')$ and $\pi ^{-1}(S_2')$, inverse images of nowhere dense sets, are nowhere dense in $Z$ over $B'\setminus L'$ and over $B'\setminus (L'\cup S_1')$, respectively.

Over $G$, the projection $Z\cap \pi ^{-1}(G)\to G$ is proper and, by proposition 7.1.2(1) applied in charts of the $s$-dimensional manifold $Z$ over $G$, a local biholomorphism, so its image is open and closed in the connected set $G$; it is nonempty, because $Z\cap (B'\times B'')$ is nonempty and $Z\cap \pi ^{-1}(G)$ is dense in it. Hence it is a covering of $G$ with a fixed number $q\geq 1$ of sheets. Indeed, let $z'\in G$ and let $y_1,\ldots ,y_ l$ be the points of the finite fiber over $z'$; choose disjoint neighborhoods $V_ i$ of the $y_ i$ mapped biholomorphically onto neighborhoods of $z'$, and, by properness, a connected neighborhood $N$ of $z'$ in $G$, contained in all these images, such that every point of $Z$ over $N$ lies in $\bigcup _ iV_ i$. Then the part of $Z$ over $N$ is the disjoint union of $l$ graphs over $N$, so the number of points in the fibers is locally constant, hence constant on $G$.

Equations for the closure. For a linear form $\lambda $ on the $z''$-space and $z'\in G$ put

\[ P_\lambda (z',T)=\prod _{z\in Z\cap \pi ^{-1}(z')} (T-\lambda (z'')). \]

The coefficient of $T^{q-k}$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $k$ in the coefficients of $\lambda $, whose coefficients are symmetric polynomials in the coordinates of the $q$ points of the fiber; these are bounded holomorphic functions on $G$, and they extend holomorphically to $B'$. Thus $P_\lambda (z',\lambda (z''))=\sum _\beta g_\beta (z)\lambda ^\beta $, where $\lambda ^\beta $ runs over the monomials in the coefficients of $\lambda $ and the $g_\beta $ are holomorphic on $B'\times B''$. Let $W=\{ g_\beta =0\text{ for all }\beta \} $, the set of points where $P_\lambda (z',\lambda (z''))=0$ for every $\lambda $; it is analytic. Over $G$, the set $W$ is $Z$: a point outside a finite fiber can be separated from all its points by a linear form. Every fiber of $W$ over $B'$ has at most $q$ points, because a linear form taking distinct values at $q+1$ points of one fiber would give $q+1$ distinct roots of a polynomial of degree $q$.

Choose a smaller ball $B''_1\subset B''$ whose boundary does not meet the finite set $W\cap \pi ^{-1}(0)$, and then a ball $B'_1\subset B'$ centered at $0$ with $W\cap (\overline{B'_1}\times \partial B''_1)=\varnothing $. Then $W_1=W\cap (B'_1\times B''_1)$ is proper with finite fibers over $B'_1$. Every branch of $W_1$ has dimension at most $s$: at a regular point of a branch of dimension $e$, the projection restricted to the branch has finite fibers, so by lemma A.2.11, applied near a point where its rank is maximal, its maximal rank is $e$, and $e\leq s$.

Let $W_ s$ be the union of the irreducible components of $W_1$ of dimension $s$, and $W_{<s}$ the union of the others. We claim that the closure of $Z$ in $B'_1\times B''_1$ is $W_ s$, which is analytic; this proves $R_ m$. Each component of $W_ s$ is pure of dimension $s$, by corollary A.2.14(1), and the image under $\pi $ of every neighborhood of each of its points in it contains a neighborhood of the image point, by lemma A.4.5; so its points over the dense set $G$ are dense in it, and they belong to $Z$. Conversely, let $y\in Z\cap (B'_1\times B''_1)$ lie over $G$. The germ $(W,y)=(Z,y)$ is a manifold germ of dimension $s$, hence irreducible by lemma A.2.9, so it equals $(W_ s,y)$ or $(W_{<s},y)$, and the latter has smaller dimension. Hence $y\in W_ s$, and since $Z\cap \pi ^{-1}(G)$ is dense in $Z$ and $W_ s$ is closed, the closure of $Z$ in $B'_1\times B''_1$ is contained in $W_ s$.

Step 2: $R_ m$ and $P_{m-1}$ imply $P_ m$. Let $F\colon Y\to X$ be proper, with $\dim Y\leq m$. Let $\rho \colon M\to Y$ be a resolution given by theorem A.5.4. It is proper, and it is surjective, because its image is closed and contains the dense set $Y_{\mathrm{reg}}$; so replacing $F$ by $F\circ \rho $ does not change the image. Moreover $\dim M\leq m$: the set $\rho ^{-1}(Y_{\mathrm{sing}})$ is nowhere dense, because $\rho $ is bimeromorphic (lemma A.3.1), so every connected component of $M$ contains a nonempty open set mapped isomorphically onto an open subset of $Y_{\mathrm{reg}}$. Fix a point of $X$, a relatively compact open neighborhood $U'$ of it and an open neighborhood $U''\Subset X$ of $\overline{U'}$ embedded in a domain of some $\mathbb C^ N$. The compact set $(F\circ \rho )^{-1}(\overline{U'})$ meets only finitely many connected components $M_1,\ldots ,M_ k$ of the manifold $(F\circ \rho )^{-1}(U'')$, and the restriction $F_ j\colon M_ j\to U''$ of $F\circ \rho $ is proper. It suffices to show that each $F_ j(M_ j)$ is analytic in $U''$.

Let $r$ be the maximal rank of the differential of $F_ j$ on the connected manifold $M_ j$. If $r=0$, the components of $F_ j$ have zero differential and are constant, so the image is a point. Let $r\geq 1$. The critical set $C=\{ \operatorname {rank}\mathrm dF_ j<r\} $ is an analytic subset of $M_ j$, defined by minors, and it is not all of $M_ j$; so it has dimension at most $m-1$, and $F_ j(C)$ is analytic by $P_{m-1}$. It has dimension at most $r-1$. Indeed, by corollary A.2.10(2), $C$ is a finite union of complex submanifolds of open subsets of $M_ j$, and the restriction of $F_ j$ to each of them has rank at most $r-1$; so $F_ j(C)$ is $\mathcal H^{2r}$-null by lemma A.2.12(1), and $\dim F_ j(C)\leq r-1$ by lemma A.2.12(2).

If $F_ j(C)=F_ j(M_ j)$, we are done. Otherwise $F_ j^{-1}(F_ j(C))$ is an analytic subset of the connected manifold $M_ j$ different from $M_ j$, and on its complement $M'_ j$ the rank of $F_ j$ is constantly $r$. The restriction $M'_ j\to U''\setminus F_ j(C)$ is proper, and a proper map of constant rank has analytic image. Indeed, at a point $w$ of the image, cover the compact fiber by finitely many open sets $V_ l$ such that, by lemma A.2.11 applied in charts of $M'_ j$, each $F_ j(V_ l)$ is a closed submanifold of dimension $r$ of a neighborhood of $w$. Corollary A.6.3. The composite of two proper bimeromorphic holomorphic maps $g\colon Z\to Y$ and $f\colon Y\to X$ between complex spaces is proper and bimeromorphic.

A.7 Connected fibers and resolution models

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

A proper bimeromorphic map can contract analytic sets, so its fibers may have positive dimension. Over a unibranch space they are nevertheless connected (theorem A.7.1). Consequently a finite bimeromorphic map onto a normal space is an isomorphism, and a proper bimeromorphic map between manifolds is an isomorphism outside a set of codimension at least two. We then introduce smooth models and prime divisors over a complex space, and show that every prime divisor over a compact space is realized after finitely many blowups with smooth centers.

Theorem A.7.1. Let $f\colon Y\to X$ be a proper bimeromorphic holomorphic map between complex spaces, and let $x\in X$ be a point at which the germ $(X,x)$ is irreducible. Then the fiber $f^{-1}(x)$ is connected. In particular, if $X$ is unibranch, every fiber of $f$ is connected.

Proof. Choose a nowhere dense analytic subset $A\subset X$ such that $f^{-1}(A)$ is nowhere dense and $f$ is an isomorphism over $X\setminus A$. Suppose that the compact fiber over $x$ is disconnected, and write it as the union of two disjoint nonempty compact sets. Since $Y$ is Hausdorff, they have disjoint open neighborhoods $V_1$ and $V_2$. Since $f$ is proper, hence closed, $U=X\setminus f(Y\setminus (V_1\cup V_2))$ is an open neighborhood of $x$ with $f^{-1}(U)\subset V_1\cup V_2$. As $(X,x)$ is irreducible, corollary A.3.9 allows us to shrink $U$ so that $U\setminus A$ is connected. Each set $V_ i\cap f^{-1}(U)$ is open and nonempty, so it meets the dense set $Y\setminus f^{-1}(A)$. Therefore $V_1$ and $V_2$ split $f^{-1}(U\setminus A)$ into two disjoint nonempty open sets. This contradicts the connectedness of $U\setminus A$, which is isomorphic to $f^{-1}(U\setminus A)$. $\square$

Corollary A.7.2. A finite bimeromorphic holomorphic map to a normal complex space is an isomorphism. Let $\phi \colon S\to X$ be a holomorphic map from a normal complex space to a complex space such that $\phi ^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})$ is nowhere dense in $S$. Then $\phi $ factors uniquely through the normalization $\nu \colon \widetilde X\to X$: there is a unique holomorphic map $\psi \colon S\to \widetilde X$ with $\nu \circ \psi =\phi $.

Proof. Let $f\colon Y\to X$ be finite and bimeromorphic, with $X$ normal, and let $A\subset X$ be a nowhere dense analytic subset such that $f$ is an isomorphism over $X\setminus A$ and $f^{-1}(A)$ is nowhere dense. Normal spaces are unibranch, so the finite fibers of $f$ are connected by theorem A.7.1, hence consist of at most one point. The image of $f$ is closed, because $f$ is proper, and contains the dense set $X\setminus A$; so $f$ is a continuous closed bijection, that is, a homeomorphism. Let $x\in X$ and $y=f^{-1}(x)$. Choose a neighborhood $V$ of $y$, relatively compact in the domain of a local model of $Y$, so that the coordinate functions of the model are bounded on $V$. Composed with $f^{-1}$, they are bounded holomorphic functions on $f(V)\setminus A$, where $f^{-1}$ is holomorphic, and $f(V)$ is an open neighborhood of $x$. By lemma A.3.4 they extend holomorphically to $f(V)$, and the extensions are the continuous functions $(\text{coordinates})\circ f^{-1}$. Hence $f^{-1}$ is holomorphic.

For the second assertion, over the dense open set $S\setminus \phi ^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})$ there is a unique lift $\psi _0=\nu ^{-1}\circ \phi $, because $\nu $ is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. We extend it by the argument used for the uniqueness in the proof of theorem A.3.6. Let $s\in S$ and $x=\phi (s)$, choose disjoint neighborhoods $W_ l$ of the finitely many points of $\nu ^{-1}(x)$, relatively compact in domains $W'_ l$ of local models of $\widetilde X$, and a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ with $\nu ^{-1}(U)\subset \bigcup _ lW_ l$, which exists by properness. Since $S$ is normal, hence unibranch, corollary A.3.9 gives a neighborhood $V$ of $s$ with $\phi (V)\subset U$ such that $V\setminus \phi ^{-1}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})$ is connected. Then $\psi _0$ maps this set into a single $W_ l$. The coordinate functions of $W'_ l$ composed with $\psi _0$ are bounded and extend holomorphically to $V$, by lemma A.3.4; the extended map takes values in $\overline{W_ l}\subset W'_ l$, and it satisfies the equations of the local model, which hold on a dense subset of $V$. This gives a holomorphic lift on $V$, with $\nu \circ \psi =\phi $ by density. Lifts agree on the dense set where $\psi _0$ is defined, so these local lifts glue, and the lift is unique. $\square$

Proposition A.7.3. A proper bimeromorphic holomorphic map $f\colon M'\to M$ between complex manifolds is an isomorphism outside an analytic subset $S$ of $M$ of codimension at least two. In particular, every irreducible hypersurface $D$ in $M$ has a well-defined strict transform in $M'$, namely the closure $D'$ of $f^{-1}(D\setminus S)$, which does not depend on the choice of $S$; it is an irreducible hypersurface, $f(D')=D$, and $f$ maps $D'$ isomorphically onto $D$ over the dense open subset $D\setminus S$ of $D$.

Proof. We take for $S$ the image of the critical set of $f$; the main point is that a component of $S$ of codimension one would contain a point over which $f$ is finite, hence an isomorphism by corollary A.7.2.

Step 1: the set $S$. We may work over each connected component of $M$, of dimension $n$ say; every connected component of its inverse image has dimension $n$ as well, because it contains a nonempty open set mapped isomorphically onto an open subset of $M$, by lemma A.3.1. The map $f$ is surjective, since its image is closed and dense. The critical set of $f$, where the Jacobian determinant vanishes in local coordinates, is analytic, and its image $S$ is analytic by theorem A.6.2. Over $M\setminus S$ the map $f$ is proper and, by proposition 7.1.2(1), a local biholomorphism, so its fibers are discrete and finite. They are connected by theorem A.7.1, and nonempty; so they are single points, and $f$ is a bijective local biholomorphism, hence an isomorphism, over $M\setminus S$. Since $f$ is an isomorphism over a dense open subset of $M$, it has no critical points over this set, so $S$ does not meet it and is nowhere dense.

Step 2: $S$ has codimension at least two. Suppose that $S$ has an irreducible component $D_0$ of codimension one. Choose a point of $(D_0)_{\mathrm{reg}}$ outside the other components of $S$, and a coordinate polydisc $U$ centered at it in which $D_0\cap U=S\cap U=\{ h=0\} $ for one of the coordinate functions $h$; then $D_0\cap U$ is a connected manifold. Let $A\subset M$ be a nowhere dense analytic subset such that $f^{-1}(A)$ is nowhere dense and $f$ is an isomorphism over $M\setminus A$. The function $h\circ f$ does not vanish identically on any connected component $V$ of $f^{-1}(U)$: otherwise $f$ would map the nonempty open set $V\setminus f^{-1}(A)$ isomorphically onto an open subset of $M$ contained in the hypersurface $D_0$, which is impossible. By proposition A.4.6, $E=f^{-1}(D_0\cap U)$ is pure of dimension $n-1$.

Let $K\subset D_0\cap U$ be a compact neighborhood of the chosen point in $D_0$. Only finitely many irreducible components $E_1,\ldots ,E_ k$ of $E$ meet the compact set $f^{-1}(K)$, and by theorem A.6.2 each image $f(E_ j)$ is an analytic subset of $D_0\cap U$. If its dimension is $n-1$, then $f(E_ j)$ contains an open subset of the connected manifold $D_0\cap U$ and therefore equals it, since proper analytic subsets of connected manifolds are nowhere dense (lemma A.1.6); we then call $E_ j$ dominating. Otherwise $f(E_ j)$ has dimension at most $n-2$.

For a dominating $E_ j$, the maximal rank of $f$ restricted to the connected manifold $(E_ j)_{\mathrm{reg}}$, as a map into the coordinate hyperplane $D_0\cap U$, is $n-1$. Otherwise $f((E_ j)_{\mathrm{reg}})$ would be $\mathcal H^{2n-2}$-null by lemma A.2.12(1); so would $f((E_ j)_{\mathrm{sing}})$, by the same lemma, since $(E_ j)_{\mathrm{sing}}$ has dimension at most $n-2$ by corollary A.2.10(1) and is therefore, by (2) of the same corollary, a finite union of complex submanifolds of dimension at most $n-2$; and $f(E_ j)$ would have dimension at most $n-2$ by lemma A.2.12(2).

Let $R_ j$ be the union of $(E_ j)_{\mathrm{sing}}$ and of the regular points where this rank is less than $n-1$. It is analytic. Indeed, near a point of $E_ j$, let $e$ be a local equation of the irreducible hypersurface $E_ j$ as in lemma A.2.16, and let $f'$ be the map formed by the $n-1$ coordinates of $f$ in the coordinate hyperplane $D_0\cap U$. At a regular point $p$ of $E_ j$ we have $\mathrm de(p)\neq 0$ and $T_ pE_ j=\ker \mathrm de(p)$, so the rank of $f'|_{E_ j}$ at $p$ is $n-1$ exactly when the Jacobian determinant of $(e,f')$ does not vanish at $p$. Hence $R_ j$ is, near this point, the union of the analytic set $(E_ j)_{\mathrm{sing}}$ and of the zero set in $E_ j$ of this Jacobian determinant. It is different from $E_ j$, because the rank $n-1$ is attained on $(E_ j)_{\mathrm{reg}}$; so it is nowhere dense in the irreducible set $E_ j$ and has dimension at most $n-2$, by corollary A.2.14(2), and $f(R_ j)$ is analytic of dimension at most $n-2$, by theorem A.6.2 and lemma A.2.12(3).

Choose a point $a$ in the interior of $K$ outside the finitely many sets $f(E_ j)$, $E_ j$ not dominating, and $f(R_ j)$, $E_ j$ dominating. Let $p\in f^{-1}(a)$. Since $f^{-1}(a)\subset E\cap f^{-1}(K)$, the point $p$ lies on some of the components $E_1,\ldots ,E_ k$. Each of them that contains $p$ is dominating, and $p\notin R_ j$, so $f|_{E_ j}$ is a local biholomorphism at $p$ onto an open subset of $D_0$, by proposition 7.1.2(1) in charts, and $p$ is isolated in $f^{-1}(a)\cap E_ j$. The components of $E$ not containing $p$ form a locally finite family of closed sets, so near $p$ the set $f^{-1}(a)$ lies in the union of the components containing $p$, and $p$ is isolated in $f^{-1}(a)$. Thus $f^{-1}(a)$ is discrete, and being compact, it is finite.

Let $p_1,\ldots ,p_ l$ be the points of $f^{-1}(a)$, and choose disjoint coordinate balls $B_1,\ldots ,B_ l$ around them. By properness there is a neighborhood $U_0$ of $a$ with $f^{-1}(U_0)\subset \bigcup _ iB_ i$. The fibers of $f$ over $U_0$ are compact analytic subsets of these balls, hence finite by corollary A.4.3. Thus $f$ is finite and bimeromorphic over the manifold $U_0$, which is normal, and it is an isomorphism over $U_0$ by corollary A.7.2. In particular $f$ has no critical points over $U_0$, which contradicts $a\in S$. Therefore $S$ has codimension at least two.

Step 3: strict transforms. Let $D$ be an irreducible hypersurface. Then $S\cap D$ has dimension at most $n-2$, so $D\setminus S$ is dense in $D$ by corollary A.2.14(2). The closure $D'$ of $f^{-1}(D\setminus S)=f^{-1}(D)\setminus f^{-1}(S)$ is analytic by lemma A.2.5, and $f$ is an isomorphism from $D'\setminus f^{-1}(S)=f^{-1}(D\setminus S)$ onto $D\setminus S$. The set $f(D')$ is closed, because $f$ is proper, it contains the dense subset $D\setminus S$ of $D$, and it is contained in the closed set $D$; so $f(D')=D$.

The set $Q=f^{-1}(D_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus S)$ is a connected manifold of dimension $n-1$, because $D_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus S$ is connected by lemma A.1.7; it is open in $D'$ and dense in $D'$, since $D_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus S$ is open and dense in $D\setminus S$. Hence $Q$ consists of regular points of $D'$ and lies in a single connected component of $(D')_{\mathrm{reg}}$, whose closure, an irreducible component of $D'$ by corollary A.2.13, contains $\overline Q=D'$; so $D'$ is irreducible, of dimension $n-1$. Finally, if $S_1$ is another analytic subset of codimension at least two off which $f$ is an isomorphism, then $D\setminus (S\cup S_1)$ is dense in $D\setminus S$ and in $D\setminus S_1$, by corollary A.2.14(2), so $f^{-1}(D\setminus (S\cup S_1))$ is dense in both $f^{-1}(D\setminus S)$ and $f^{-1}(D\setminus S_1)$, and the two closures agree. $\square$

A smooth model of a complex space $X$ is a proper bimeromorphic holomorphic map $M\to X$ with $M$ a complex manifold; resolutions (theorem A.5.4) are smooth models. Two smooth models $f_1\colon M_1\to X$ and $f_2\colon M_2\to X$ have a common smooth model, that is, a smooth model $M_3$ with proper bimeromorphic maps $M_3\to M_1$ and $M_3\to M_2$ commuting with the maps to $X$.

To construct it, let $E_1,E_2\subset X$ be nowhere dense analytic subsets as in the definition of bimeromorphic maps for $f_1$ and $f_2$, and put $U=X\setminus (E_1\cup E_2)$. Both $f_ i$ are isomorphisms over $U$, and $f_ i^{-1}(U)$ is dense in $M_ i$ by lemma A.3.1. Let $\Gamma \subset M_1\times _ XM_2=\{ (p_1,p_2):f_1(p_1)=f_2(p_2)\} $ be the closure of the graph of $f_2^{-1}\circ f_1$ over $U$. The fiber product is analytic in $M_1\times M_2$, since the equality of the two images is expressed by coordinate equations in local embeddings of $X$. Over $U$ the fiber product is the graph, so $\Gamma $ is the closure of the complement in the fiber product of the analytic set of points lying over $E_1\cup E_2$, and it is analytic by lemma A.2.5.

The projections of $\Gamma $ are proper: over a compact subset of $M_1$, the second coordinates lie in the compact inverse image under $f_2$ of its image in $X$, and $\Gamma $ is closed. Their images are closed and contain the dense sets $f_ i^{-1}(U)$, so they are surjective; they are isomorphisms over $f_ i^{-1}(U)$, and the inverse images of the complements $f_ i^{-1}(E_1\cup E_2)$ are nowhere dense in $\Gamma $, because the graph over $U$ is dense in $\Gamma $; hence they are bimeromorphic. Resolving $\Gamma $ by theorem A.5.4 and composing with the projections, we obtain the common smooth model; the composites are proper and bimeromorphic by corollary A.6.3.

When $X$ is compact and the two smooth models are themselves obtained from $X$ by finite sequences of blowups with smooth centers, there is also a common refinement of the same kind. Let $X=X_0\leftarrow X_1\leftarrow \cdots \leftarrow X_ k=M_1$ be the first sequence, where $X_ j$ is the blowup of $X_{j-1}$ along the ideal of a smooth center $C_ j\subset X_{j-1}$. We start from a resolution of $X$, which is such a sequence because $X$ is compact, and lift it through the first sequence one center at a time.

Suppose that the current smooth model $N$, obtained from $X$ by a finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers, has been constructed with a map to $X_{j-1}$ over $X$. The maps $N\to X$ and $X_{j-1}\to X$ are proper and bimeromorphic by corollary A.6.3; let $U\subset X$ be the complement of a nowhere dense analytic subset over which both are isomorphisms. The pullback of the ideal of $C_ j$ to $N$ is not identically zero on any component of $N$. Indeed, its zero set is the inverse image of $C_ j$, which is nowhere dense in $X_{j-1}$ because the ideal of $C_ j$ is not identically zero on any branch of $X_{j-1}$ (definition A.5.1). Over $U$ the map $N\to X_{j-1}$ is an isomorphism, so this inverse image meets the dense open part of $N$ over $U$ in a nowhere dense set, and its part over $X\setminus U$ is nowhere dense by lemma A.3.1. Principalizing it by theorem A.5.5, we obtain a sequence of blowups $N'\to N$ with smooth centers, finite because $N$ is compact, on which the pulled-back ideal is invertible, and proposition A.5.2 lifts $N'\to X_{j-1}$ to a map $N'\to X_ j$.

Repeating this for the finitely many centers of the first sequence and then of the second, we obtain a finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers over $X$ that dominates both models. All three models are proper and bimeromorphic over $X$, by proposition A.5.2, corollary A.6.3; by lemma A.3.1 there is a complement $U\subset X$ of a nowhere dense analytic subset over which all three are isomorphisms. The maps from the new model to the two given ones commute with the maps to $X$, so they are isomorphisms over the parts lying over $U$. The complements of these parts are nowhere dense analytic subsets of the three models, again by lemma A.3.1; so the two maps are bimeromorphic, and they are proper because the source is compact. (For general proper bimeromorphic models we only have the common smooth model constructed above.)

Definition A.7.4. Let $X$ be a complex space. A prime divisor over $X$ is represented by a pair consisting of a smooth model $M\to X$ and an irreducible hypersurface $F\subset M$; two pairs are identified if the strict transforms of their hypersurfaces, in the sense of proposition A.7.3, agree on a common smooth model. The prime divisor is exceptional if the image of $F$ in $X$ has codimension at least two.

Lemma A.7.5. Let $X$ be a complex space.

  1. The identification of pairs in definition A.7.4 is an equivalence relation, and the image in $X$ of the hypersurface $F$ of a representative $(M,F)$ depends only on the prime divisor.

  2. Let $(M,F)$ represent a prime divisor over $X$, and let $g\colon M'\to M$ be the composite of the principalization of $\mathcal I_ F$ given by theorem A.5.5 with the empty divisor. Then $g$ is proper and bimeromorphic, the strict transform $F'$ of $F$ is a smooth irreducible hypersurface of $M'$, and $(M',F')$ represents the same prime divisor.

In particular, every prime divisor over $X$ has a representative $(M,F)$ with $F$ smooth.

Proof. We begin with two remarks used in the proof of (1). First, two maps from a smooth model $M_3$ to a smooth model $M_1$ that commute with the maps to $X$ agree. Indeed, let $U\subset X$ be the complement of a nowhere dense analytic subset over which both models are isomorphisms; the two maps agree over $U$, and the part of $M_3$ over $U$ is dense, by lemma A.3.1.

Second, strict transforms are compatible with composition. Let $g\colon P\to N$ and $h\colon N\to M$ be proper bimeromorphic maps between complex manifolds, so that $h\circ g$ is proper and bimeromorphic by corollary A.6.3, and let $D\subset M$ be an irreducible hypersurface, with strict transform $D^ N$ in $N$. We claim that the strict transform of $D^ N$ under $g$ is the strict transform $D^ P$ of $D$ under $h\circ g$. Let $S_ h,S_{hg}\subset M$ and $S_ g\subset N$ be analytic subsets of codimension at least two as in proposition A.7.3 for $h$, $h\circ g$ and $g$, and let $T=S_ h\cup S_{hg}\cup h(S_ g)$, which is analytic of codimension at least two by theorem A.6.2 and lemma A.2.12(3).

The set $O=D^ N\cap h^{-1}(M\setminus T)=h^{-1}(D\setminus T)$ is open in $D^ N$, nonempty, and does not meet $S_ g$; its complement $D^ N\cap h^{-1}(T)$ is an analytic subset of the irreducible set $D^ N$ different from $D^ N$, hence nowhere dense by corollary A.2.14(2). So $O$ is dense in $D^ N\setminus S_ g$, and since $g$ is an isomorphism over $N\setminus S_ g$, the strict transform of $D^ N$ under $g$ is the closure of $g^{-1}(O)=(h\circ g)^{-1}(D\setminus T)$. By the independence of the choice of $S_{hg}$ in proposition A.7.3, applied with $T$, this closure is also $D^ P$.

Now let $(M_ i,F_ i)$, $i=1,2,3$, be pairs such that $(M_1,F_1)$ and $(M_2,F_2)$ are identified on a common smooth model $N_{12}$, and $(M_2,F_2)$ and $(M_3,F_3)$ on $N_{23}$. Let $P$ be a common smooth model of $N_{12}$ and $N_{23}$, and let $p_ i\colon P\to M_ i$ be the composite maps; by the first remark, the two composite maps $P\to M_2$, through $N_{12}$ and through $N_{23}$, coincide. By the second remark, the strict transforms of $F_1$ and $F_2$ under $p_1$ and $p_2$ are the strict transforms in $P$ of their strict transforms in $N_{12}$, which agree; similarly for $F_2$ and $F_3$. Hence $F_1$ and $F_3$ have the same strict transform in $P$, and the identification is transitive; it is reflexive, with $M_1$ itself as common smooth model, and symmetric by definition. Since $p_ i$ maps the strict transform of $F_ i$ onto $F_ i$ (proposition A.7.3), the images of $F_1$ and $F_3$ in $X$ both equal the image of their common strict transform in $P$; so the image of $F$ in $X$ depends only on the prime divisor.

We now prove (2). Let $(M,F)$ represent a prime divisor. The ideal $\mathcal I_ F$ is coherent by theorem A.2.8, and it is not identically zero on any component of $M$, because $F$ is nowhere dense. Let $g\colon M'\to M$ be as in (2), so that $\mathcal I_ F\mathcal O_{M'}=\mathcal O_{M'}(-H)$ with $H$ an effective divisor whose support has simple normal crossings; $g$ is proper, it is an isomorphism off $g^{-1}(F)$, and $g^{-1}(F)$, the support of $H$, is nowhere dense, so $g$ is bimeromorphic. The strict transform $F'$ of $F$ is an irreducible hypersurface contained in the support of $H$, so by corollary A.2.14(4) and (3) it is one of its irreducible components, which are smooth. The pair $(M',F')$ represents the same prime divisor, as we see on the common smooth model $M'$, which is a smooth model of $X$ by corollary A.6.3. $\square$

Theorem A.7.6. Let $X$ be a compact complex space. Every prime divisor over $X$ is represented on a smooth model obtained from $X$ by a finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers. Its representative may also be chosen smooth.

Proof. After reducing to the case of a manifold, we blow up the image of the divisor in $X$ as long as it has codimension at least two; each step lowers the order of vanishing along the divisor of the Jacobian determinant of the model.

Step 1: reduction to a manifold. Let $\widehat X\to X$ be a resolution given by theorem A.5.4, a finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers, since $X$ is compact. Let the prime divisor be represented by $(M_0,F_0)$, and let $M$ be a common smooth model of $M_0$ and $\widehat X$, with $E\subset M$ the strict transform of $F_0$ (proposition A.7.3); then $(M,E)$ represents the same divisor, as we see on the common smooth model $M$. A finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers over $\widehat X$, composed with $\widehat X\to X$, is such a sequence over $X$, and smooth models of $\widehat X$ are smooth models of $X$, by corollary A.6.3. Hence we may assume that $X$ is a compact complex manifold, and we are given a proper bimeromorphic map $f\colon M\to X$ from a compact manifold and an irreducible hypersurface $E\subset M$.

Since $E$ is irreducible, hence connected, it lies in a connected component $M_ E$ of $M$, and $f(M_ E)$ lies in a connected component $X_ E$ of $X$. These components have the same dimension $n$, because $M_ E$ contains a nonempty open set mapped isomorphically onto an open subset of $X$ (lemma A.3.1); moreover $n\geq 1$ and $\dim E=n-1$, since $E$ is a nonempty hypersurface. If $n=1$, the center $f(E)$ considered below is a point of $X_ E$, hence a hypersurface.

Step 2: the order of the Jacobian. The Jacobian determinant $J_ f$ of $f$ in local coordinates is holomorphic and determined up to multiplication by holomorphic units. It does not vanish identically near any point, because $f$ is an isomorphism over a dense open subset of $X$ whose inverse image is dense in $M$ (lemma A.3.1). Its order of vanishing $\operatorname {ord}_ EJ_ f$ along $E$ is the largest $k$ such that $J_ f$ is divisible by the $k$th power of a local equation of $E$ near points of $E_{\mathrm{reg}}$; definition 12.6.3 uses the same notion of order along a hypersurface. It is a finite nonnegative integer $k$, since a function divisible by all powers of a local coordinate $y$ has vanishing Taylor coefficients in $y$ and vanishes near the point. It does not depend on the point of $E_{\mathrm{reg}}$, which is a connected manifold by corollary A.2.14: for each integer $j$, the set of points of $E_{\mathrm{reg}}$ at which the order is at least $j$ is open, and it is closed by the identity theorem on $E_{\mathrm{reg}}$, since a holomorphic function vanishing on $E$ near a regular point is divisible by a local equation of $E$.

For composable maps the chain rule gives

A.14
\begin{equation} \operatorname {ord}_ E J_{g\circ h} =\operatorname {ord}_ E J_ h+ \operatorname {ord}_ E(J_ g\circ h), \end{equation}

because orders of vanishing along $E$ add under multiplication. Replacing $M$ by a common smooth model $h\colon M'\to M$ and $E$ by its strict transform $E'$ does not change $k$: by proposition A.7.3, $h$ is an isomorphism near generic points of $E'$, so $\operatorname {ord}_{E'}J_ h=0$ and $\operatorname {ord}_{E'}(J_ f\circ h)=k$.

Step 3: one principalization lowers the order. The center $Z=f(E)$ is an analytic subset of $X$ by theorem A.6.2, of dimension at most $\dim E=n-1$ by lemma A.2.12(3). It is irreducible: if $Z=Z_1\cup Z_2$ with analytic subsets $Z_1,Z_2$, then the irreducible set $E$ is the union of its analytic subsets $E\cap f^{-1}(Z_1)$ and $E\cap f^{-1}(Z_2)$, so it lies in one of them, and $Z$ equals $Z_1$ or $Z_2$. The same argument shows that images of irreducible analytic sets under proper holomorphic maps are irreducible. Suppose first that $Z$ is a hypersurface, and let $S\subset X$ be as in proposition A.7.3 for $f$. The strict transform $Z^ M$ of $Z$ in $M$ is an irreducible hypersurface containing $f^{-1}(Z\setminus S)$. The set $E\cap f^{-1}(Z\setminus S)$ is open in $E$, and it is nonempty, because $f(E)=Z$ is not contained in $S$, which has codimension at least two. So $E\cap Z^ M$ is an analytic subset of $E$ with interior points in $E$, and $E\subset Z^ M$ by corollary A.2.14(2); then $E=Z^ M$ by corollary A.2.14(3). Hence $(X,Z)$ represents the given divisor, as we see on the common smooth model $M$.

Suppose now that $Z$ has codimension at least two. Its ideal $\mathcal I_ Z$ is coherent by theorem A.2.8, and it is not identically zero on any component of $X$, since $Z$ is nowhere dense. Let $\sigma =\sigma _1\circ \cdots \circ \sigma _ t\colon X'\to X$ be the finite sequence of blowups with centers $C_ i\subset X_{i-1}$ and transformed ideals $\mathfrak a_ i$, $\mathfrak a_0=\mathcal I_ Z$, given by theorem A.5.5; it is proper and bimeromorphic by corollary A.6.3, so $X'$ is a smooth model of $X$. Let $h\colon M'\to M$ and $f'\colon M'\to X'$ be the maps from a common smooth model of $M$ and $X'$, so that $f\circ h=\sigma \circ f'$, let $E'$ be the strict transform of $E$, let $\rho _ i\colon M'\to X_ i$ be the induced maps, and let $Z_ i=\rho _ i(E')$; thus $Z_0=Z$.

Let $r\geq 2$ be the codimension of $Z$, which is pure by corollary A.2.14(1). We claim that for some $i$ the center $C_ i$ contains $Z_{i-1}$, and that the connected component of $C_ i$ containing $Z_{i-1}$ has codimension $r$. The sets $Z_ j$ are irreducible, as images of the irreducible set $E'$, and $\sigma _{j+1}(Z_{j+1})=Z_ j$. Put $\tau _ j=\sigma _1\circ \cdots \circ \sigma _ j$. Suppose that $Z_{l-1}\not\subset C_ l$ for all $l\leq j$. We show by induction on $j$ that there are a nowhere dense analytic subset $A_ j\subset Z$ and an open set $N_ j\subset X\setminus A_ j$ containing $Z^\circ _ j=Z\setminus A_ j$ such that $\tau _ j$ is an isomorphism over $N_ j$ and, on $\tau _ j^{-1}(N_ j)$,

\[ Z_ j=\tau _ j^{-1}(Z^\circ _ j),\qquad \mathfrak a_ j=\mathcal I_ Z\mathcal O_{X_ j}. \]

For $j=0$ take $A_0=\varnothing $ and $N_0=X$.

Assume the assertion for some $j<t$, and suppose that $Z_ j\not\subset C_{j+1}$. On $\tau _ j^{-1}(N_ j)$ we have $C_{j+1}\subset V(\mathfrak a_ j)=\tau _ j^{-1}(Z^\circ _ j)\subset Z_ j$. By corollary A.2.14(2), $C_{j+1}\cap Z_ j$ is nowhere dense in $Z_ j$. Its image $\tau _ j(C_{j+1}\cap Z_ j)\subset Z$ is analytic by theorem A.6.2, and it is nowhere dense in $Z$: its part in $Z^\circ _ j$ corresponds under the isomorphism $\tau _ j$ to the nowhere dense part of $C_{j+1}\cap Z_ j$ in the open subset $\tau _ j^{-1}(Z^\circ _ j)$ of $Z_ j$, and $A_ j$ is nowhere dense. Let $A_{j+1}=A_ j\cup \tau _ j(C_{j+1}\cap Z_ j)$ and $N_{j+1}=N_ j\setminus A_{j+1}$. Then $\tau _ j^{-1}(N_{j+1})$ does not meet $C_{j+1}$, so $\sigma _{j+1}$ is an isomorphism over it and does not change the ideal there: $\mathfrak a_{j+1}=\mathcal O_{X_{j+1}}(E_{j+1})\mathfrak a_ j\mathcal O_{X_{j+1}}=\mathcal I_ Z\mathcal O_{X_{j+1}}$ on $\tau _{j+1}^{-1}(N_{j+1})$. Moreover $Z_{j+1}\cap \tau _{j+1}^{-1}(N_{j+1})=\sigma _{j+1}^{-1}(Z_ j\cap \tau _ j^{-1}(N_{j+1}))=\tau _{j+1}^{-1}(Z^\circ _{j+1})$, because $\sigma _{j+1}(Z_{j+1})=Z_ j$ and $\sigma _{j+1}$ is injective there. This completes the induction.

Since $A_ j$ is nowhere dense in $Z$, the set $Z^\circ _ j$ is nonempty, and so is $\tau _ j^{-1}(Z^\circ _ j)$. If $Z_{l-1}\not\subset C_ l$ held for all $l\leq t$, the induction would reach $j=t$, and $\mathfrak a_ t$ would be the ideal of the nonempty set $\tau _ t^{-1}(Z^\circ _ t)$ on $\tau _ t^{-1}(N_ t)$, whereas $\mathfrak a_ t$ is the unit ideal. So there is a least index $i$ with $Z_{i-1}\subset C_ i$, and the assertion holds for $j=i-1$. On $\tau _{i-1}^{-1}(N_{i-1})$ we have $Z_{i-1}\subset C_ i\subset V(\mathfrak a_{i-1})=\tau _{i-1}^{-1}(Z^\circ _{i-1})\subset Z_{i-1}$, so $C_ i$ coincides there with a copy of the open subset $Z^\circ _{i-1}$ of $Z$, which is pure of codimension $r$. The set $Z_{i-1}$ is connected, being the closure of the connected manifold $(Z_{i-1})_{\mathrm{reg}}$, so it lies in one connected component of the manifold $C_ i$, and this component has codimension $r$.

By (A.8) and proposition A.5.3, near points over this component the Jacobian determinant of $\sigma _ i$ is, in any local coordinates, a unit times the $(r-1)$st power of a local equation of the exceptional divisor $E_ i=\sigma _ i^{-1}(C_ i)$. Since $Z_ i=\rho _ i(E')$ lies over $Z_{i-1}\subset C_ i$, the function $J_{\sigma _ i}\circ \rho _ i$ vanishes on $E'$; it does not vanish identically near any point, because $J_{\sigma _ i}$ vanishes only on $E_ i$, and $\rho _ i^{-1}(E_ i)$ is nowhere dense, $\rho _ i$ being proper and bimeromorphic as the composite of $f'$ with $\sigma _{i+1}\circ \cdots \circ \sigma _ t$ (corollary A.6.3). Hence it has positive order along $E'$. The Jacobians of the other blowups composed with the corresponding maps $\rho _ j$ are holomorphic, so they have nonnegative order, and by the chain rule $J_\sigma \circ f'=\prod _ j(J_{\sigma _ j}\circ \rho _ j)$. Thus

\[ \operatorname {ord}_{E'}(J_\sigma \circ f')\geq 1. \]

Applying (A.14) to $f\circ h=\sigma \circ f'$ and using Step 2, we obtain

\[ 0\leq \operatorname {ord}_{E'}J_{f'} =k-\operatorname {ord}_{E'}(J_\sigma \circ f')\leq k-1. \]

Step 4: conclusion. The pair $(M',E')$ represents the given prime divisor, as we see on the common smooth model $M'$, and $X'$ is a compact manifold. We repeat Step 3 with $X'$, $f'$ and $E'$ in place of $X$, $f$ and $E$. Each repetition in which the center has codimension at least two lowers the nonnegative integer $k$ by at least one, so after at most $k$ repetitions the center is a hypersurface on the current model, which is obtained from $X$ by a finite sequence of blowups with smooth centers, and it represents the given prime divisor. Finally, by lemma A.7.5(2), principalizing the ideal of this hypersurface by theorem A.5.5 gives a further sequence of blowups with smooth centers, finite because the model is compact, on which the strict transform of the hypersurface is smooth and represents the same prime divisor. $\square$

A.8 Plurisubharmonic functions on reduced spaces

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

At a singular point of a complex space there is no intrinsic complex Hessian, so we define plurisubharmonic functions on a complex space as local restrictions of plurisubharmonic functions on ambient domains. Their compositions with holomorphic discs are then subharmonic, and together with the curves of section A.4 this gives a maximum principle on compact spaces and local upper bounds near analytic subsets of codimension at least two.

Definition A.8.1. Let $X$ be a complex space. A function $u\colon X\to [-\infty ,+\infty )$ is locally plurisubharmonic if every point of $X$ has a local model, which identifies a neighborhood of the point with an analytic subset of a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb C^ N$, and a ball $V\subset \Omega $ around the point such that $u$ is the restriction to $X\cap V$ of a function on $V$ that is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $. We write $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$ if, in addition, $u$ is not identically $-\infty $ on any irreducible component of $X$, the components being defined as in corollary A.2.13. Locally plurisubharmonic functions are allowed to be identically $-\infty $ on some components; we use them in this generality when only local properties are needed.

The following lemma shows that this definition does not depend on the local models, and that it agrees with the definition of section 8.2 on complex manifolds.

Lemma A.8.2. Let $X$ be a complex space.

  1. Let $u$ be locally plurisubharmonic on $X$. For every local model of a neighborhood of a point of $X$, there is a ball $V$ around the point in the ambient domain such that $u$ is the restriction to $X\cap V$ of a function on $V$ that is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $.

  2. If $u$ is locally plurisubharmonic on $X$ and $F\colon Y\to X$ is a holomorphic map of complex spaces, then $u\circ F$ is locally plurisubharmonic on $Y$. In particular, if $Y$ is a one-dimensional complex manifold, such as a disc, then $u\circ F$ is subharmonic, in the sense of definition 3.1.1, in every local coordinate of $Y$, and on the whole disc if $Y$ is a disc.

  3. If $X$ is a complex manifold, then a function on $X$ is locally plurisubharmonic if and only if, on each connected component of $X$, it is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $. Moreover, $\mathrm{PSH}(X)$ agrees with the set defined in section 8.2.

Proof. We prove (1) and the first assertion of (2) together. Let $u$ be locally plurisubharmonic on $X$, let $F\colon Y\to X$ be holomorphic, and let $y\in Y$. Choose a local model of a neighborhood of $F(y)$, with ambient coordinates $w$ and a ball $V$ around $F(y)$ on which $u$ is the restriction of a function $v$ that is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $, and an arbitrary local model of a neighborhood of $y$, with ambient coordinates $z$. By the definition of holomorphic maps and of $\mathcal O_ Y$, the components of $F$ in the $w$-model extend holomorphically to a neighborhood of $y$ in the $z$-ambient space, and their joint extension $G$, being continuous with $G(y)=F(y)$, maps a smaller ball $V'$ around $y$ into $V$. By proposition 8.2.3, $v\circ G$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on $V'$, and its restriction to $Y\cap V'$ is $u\circ F$. Hence $u\circ F$ is locally plurisubharmonic, with respect to every local model of $Y$. For $Y=X$ and $F$ the identity, with two local models at the same point, this is (1).

If $Y$ is a one-dimensional complex manifold, its holomorphic charts are local models. By what we have just proved, $u\circ F$ is therefore, in local coordinates near each point of $Y$, plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $, that is, subharmonic in the sense of definition 3.1.1. Subharmonicity is a local property, so $u\circ F$ is subharmonic in every local coordinate, and on the whole disc if $Y$ is a disc.

(3) Holomorphic charts are local models. So by (1), every point of $X$ has a coordinate ball on which a locally plurisubharmonic function $u$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $, and by lemma 8.2.4, $u$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on each connected component of $X$. Conversely, a function with this property is locally plurisubharmonic, with the charts as local models, since its expressions in charts with connected domains are plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $. The irreducible components of the manifold $X$ are its connected components, by corollary A.2.13. Hence $\mathrm{PSH}(X)$, in the sense of definition A.8.1, consists of the functions that are plurisubharmonic on each connected component of $X$, which are the plurisubharmonic functions in the sense of section 8.2, since plurisubharmonicity is a local property there. $\square$

Proposition A.8.3. A locally plurisubharmonic function on a compact connected complex space is constant, possibly equal to $-\infty $.

Proof. Let $u$ be locally plurisubharmonic on the compact connected space $X$. It is upper semicontinuous, so it attains its maximum $M\in [-\infty ,\infty )$. If $M=-\infty $, then $u\equiv -\infty $. Suppose that $M$ is finite, and let $x$ be a point with $u(x)=M$. We show that $u=M$ near $x$. If $x$ is an isolated point, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, let $X_1$ be a branch of $(X,x)$ of positive dimension, and take a parametrization of $(X_1,x)$, with its connected covering locus $X_{1,\delta }$, dense in the representative $X_1$. By proposition A.4.2, applied to the subgerm consisting of the singular locus of $X$ and the points of $X_1$ outside $X_{1,\delta }$, there is a nonconstant holomorphic curve $\gamma $ with $\gamma (0)=x$ and $\gamma (t)\in X_{1,\delta }\cap X_{\mathrm{reg}}$ for $t\neq 0$. The function $u\circ \gamma $ is subharmonic by lemma A.8.2(2), and it attains its finite maximum $M$ at $0$; by the maximum principle (proposition 3.1.4) it equals $M$ near $0$. Thus $u$ attains the value $M$ at points of $X_{1,\delta }$.

By lemma A.8.2(2) and (3), applied to the inclusion of the connected manifold $X_{1,\delta }$, the restriction of $u$ to $X_{1,\delta }$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $, hence plurisubharmonic, since it takes the value $M$. On a coordinate ball around a point where $u=M$, it is subharmonic by theorem 8.2.1, and it attains its maximum $M$ at the center, so it equals $M$ on this ball by proposition 3.1.4. Thus the set where $u=M$ is open in $X_{1,\delta }$; it is closed by upper semicontinuity and nonempty, so $u=M$ on $X_{1,\delta }$. Since $X_{1,\delta }$ is dense in $X_1$, upper semicontinuity gives $u\geq M$, hence $u=M$, on all of $X_1$. Doing this for each branch, we find that $u=M$ on a neighborhood of $x$.

Hence the set $\{ u=M\} $ is open. It is also closed, because $\{ u<M\} $ is open for an upper semicontinuous function bounded above by $M$. Since $X$ is connected, $u\equiv M$. $\square$

Proposition A.8.4. Let $A$ be an analytic subset of a complex space $X$ such that, at every point of $A$, the intersection of $A$ with each irreducible branch of $X$ has codimension at least two in that branch. Then every locally plurisubharmonic function on $X\setminus A$ is locally bounded above near $A$.

Proof. Let $u$ be locally plurisubharmonic on $X\setminus A$, and let $x\in A$. We bound $u$ over a circle that avoids the image of $A$ under a finite projection, and carry the bound inward along one-dimensional slices by the maximum principle. It suffices to bound $u$ from above near $x$ on each branch of $(X,x)$, and the restriction of $u$ to a branch is locally plurisubharmonic by lemma A.8.2(2); so we may work on the representative $X_1$ of a branch on a parametrization polydisc. Let $X_2,\ldots ,X_ p$ be representatives of the other branches.

The hypothesis is inherited by $X_1$ near $x$. Indeed, let $y\in X_1$ be close to $x$, and let $Y$ be a branch of $(X_1,y)$. It has dimension $\dim X_1$, by proposition A.2.7, and it lies in a branch $Y'$ of $(X,y)$, which lies in some $(X_ j,y)$. If $j=1$, then $Y'=Y$, because $Y$ is a branch of $(X_1,y)$. If $j\neq 1$, then $Y\subset (X_1\cap X_ j,y)$, which is impossible for $y$ close to $x$, because $\dim (X_1\cap X_ j,y)\leq \dim (X_1\cap X_ j,x)<\dim X_1$, by upper semicontinuity and proposition A.2.7. Hence every branch of $(X_1,y)$ is a branch of $(X,y)$. The dimension $d$ of $X_1$ is at least two, since $A\cap X_1$ contains $x$ and has codimension at least two in $X_1$. Now write $X$ for $X_1$, and let $\pi \colon X\to \Delta '\subset \mathbb C^ d$ be the projection of the local parametrization (theorem A.2.3); it is proper with finite fibers, and $\pi ^{-1}(0)=\{ x\} $.

Step 1: a cylinder avoiding $\pi (A)$. The image $\pi (A)$ is analytic by theorem A.6.2, and its dimension is at most $d-2$ by lemma A.2.12(3). In particular there is a holomorphic function $g\not\equiv 0$ near $0$ vanishing on $\pi (A)$. Choose a complex line through $0$ on which the first nonzero homogeneous term of $g$ does not vanish, and write the base coordinates as $(\zeta ,z')$, with $\zeta $ in the direction of this line. Then $\pi (A)$ meets the line only at $0$ near $0$, and by compactness there are $r>0$ and $\varepsilon >0$ such that the cylinder

\[ \{ |\zeta |=r,\ |z'|\leq \varepsilon \} \]

is compactly contained in $\Delta '$ and disjoint from $\pi (A)$. Its inverse image is compact and disjoint from $A$, so $u$ has an upper bound $M<\infty $ there.

Step 2: slicing by curves. The projection to the $z'$-ball of $\pi (A)\cap \{ |\zeta |<r,\ |z'|<\varepsilon \} $ is proper, because the closure of this set does not meet $\{ |\zeta |=r\} $. By theorem A.6.2, its image $S$ is analytic in the ball $\{ |z'|<\varepsilon \} $, and it has dimension at most $d-2$ by lemma A.2.12(3), so it is nowhere dense in this $(d-1)$-dimensional ball. Fix $z'$ with $|z'|<\varepsilon $ and $z'\notin S$. Then the compact set $\pi ^{-1}(\{ |\zeta |\leq r\} \times \{ z'\} )$ is disjoint from $A$, and so is $C=\pi ^{-1}(\{ |\zeta |<r'\} \times \{ z'\} )$ for some $r'>r$.

The set $C$ is a pure one-dimensional complex space, or empty. Indeed, it is cut out of $X$ by the $d-1$ coordinate equations $z'_ k=\mathrm{const}$. At each stage, every branch of the current set at every point is mapped finitely, hence openly by lemma A.4.5, to the space of the remaining base coordinates; so the next coordinate equation is not identically satisfied on it, and by proposition A.4.6 the dimension drops by exactly one. Let $\nu \colon \widetilde C\to C$ be the normalization (theorem A.3.6). By proposition A.4.1, $\widetilde C$ is a one-dimensional complex manifold, and $\zeta \circ \pi \circ \nu \colon \widetilde C\to \{ |\zeta |<r'\} $ is proper with finite fibers, hence open on each connected component by lemma A.4.5.

By lemma A.8.2(2), the function $v=u\circ \nu $ is subharmonic on the one-dimensional manifold $\widetilde C$. Let $Q$ be a connected component of the open set $\{ |\zeta \circ \pi \circ \nu |<r\} \subset \widetilde C$; its closure is compact, by properness. A point of $\overline Q$ over the open disc $\{ |\zeta |<r\} $ has a connected neighborhood in $\{ |\zeta \circ \pi \circ \nu |<r\} $ meeting $Q$, and therefore lies in $Q$; so the boundary of $Q$ lies over the circle $\{ |\zeta |=r\} $, where $v\leq M$ by Step 1. This boundary is nonempty: otherwise $Q$ would be a connected component of $\widetilde C$, and its image would be open and, by properness, closed in the disc $\{ |\zeta |<r'\} $, hence equal to this disc, although it lies in $\{ |\zeta |<r\} $.

The upper semicontinuous function $v$ attains its maximum on the compact set $\overline Q$. If the maximum is finite and attained at a point of $Q$, the maximum principle (proposition 3.1.4), applied on coordinate discs, shows that the set where $v$ attains it is open in $Q$; this set is also closed, so $v$ is constant on $Q$, and by upper semicontinuity at a boundary point the constant is at most $M$. Otherwise the maximum is $-\infty $ or is attained on the boundary of $Q$, so again it is at most $M$. Hence $v\leq M$ on $Q$, and since $\nu $ is surjective, $u\leq M$ on $\pi ^{-1}(\{ |\zeta |<r\} \times \{ z'\} )$.

Step 3: the remaining slices. We have shown that $u\leq M$ on the part of $X\cap \pi ^{-1}(\{ |\zeta |<r,\ |z'|<\varepsilon \} )$ lying over $z'\notin S$. Let $y\notin A$ be a point of this set lying over some $z'\in S$, and let $Y_1$ be a branch of $(X,y)$. The set $T=\pi ^{-1}(\{ z'\in S\} )$ does not contain the germ $Y_1$: by purity, $Y_1$ has dimension $d$, so $\pi |_{Y_1}$ is open by lemma A.4.5, while the image of $T$ is nowhere dense. Since $A$ is closed and $y\notin A$, proposition A.4.2 gives a holomorphic curve $\gamma $ in $Y_1$ with $\gamma (0)=y$ and $\gamma (t)\notin A\cup T$ for $t\neq 0$. Then $u\circ \gamma $ is subharmonic near $0$, by lemma A.8.2(2), and at most $M$ on the punctured disc, so $u(y)\leq M$ by the submean inequality on small circles. Thus $u\leq M$ on $X\setminus A$ near $x$. $\square$
