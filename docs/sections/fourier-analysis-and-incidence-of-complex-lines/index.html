2.2 Fourier analysis and incidence of complex lines

Definitions, results, and proofs on this subject.

6 Fourier analysis and incidence of complex lines

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

This chapter develops the Fourier analysis on Euclidean space that the complex theory of 2 requires, and applies it to functions of several complex variables. Let $D\subset \mathbb C^ n$ be open, with $n\geq 2$, and let $u$ be a function on $D$ whose restriction to every complex line is subharmonic. On each line $u$ is upper semicontinuous, and hence bounded above on compact subsets of that line, but the hypothesis gives no control of these bounds as the line varies. We prove that a Borel function with this property is nevertheless locally bounded above on $D$ (corollary 6.5.4). In chapter 8 this is the step that makes upper semicontinuity automatic for functions that are subharmonic on complex lines (theorem 8.1.3).

The proof is by contradiction. Suppose that $u(x_ j)\to \infty $ along a sequence $x_ j\to p$. By the submean inequality, $u\geq u(x_ j)$ on a subset of positive length of every sufficiently small circle centered at $x_ j$ in a complex line through $x_ j$. Choosing one such point on each circle, we obtain a probability measure on an annulus about $x_ j$, carried by the set where $u\geq u(x_ j)$, whose Riesz energy of order $2n-2$ is bounded independently of $j$ (theorem 6.4.2).

By Plancherel's theorem, energy of this order is a constant multiple of the average, over all complex hyperplanes $H$ through the origin, of the squared $L^2$ norms of the densities of the measures projected onto $H$ (theorem 6.5.2). By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, a projected measure with a density of controlled $L^2$ norm cannot be concentrated on a set of small measure. An incidence argument then produces a single complex line that meets infinitely many of the sets $\{ u\geq u(x_ j)\} $ near $p$ (theorem 6.5.3), which contradicts the upper bound of $u$ on that line. The Fourier analysis is developed in section 6.1, and the invariant measures used to average over directions and over hyperplanes are constructed in section 6.3.

The same Fourier methods give $L^ p$ estimates for second derivatives. In section 6.2 we show that, for $1<p<\infty $, the $L^ p$ norm of the Laplacian of a test function bounds the $L^ p$ norm of its full real Hessian. Together with Morrey's inequality, these estimates supply the real regularity of functions with bounded Laplacian that is used in chapter 16 to compute Monge–Ampère measures of envelopes below nonsmooth obstacles.

Historical remarks and references for this chapter are collected in section B.6.

6.1 Fourier transform and energy

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

Let $d\geq 1$. We develop the Fourier transform on $\mathbb R^ d$ as far as Plancherel's theorem, and use it to express the Riesz energy of a measure as a weighted $L^2$ norm of its Fourier transform (theorem 6.1.4). For $f\in L^1(\mathbb R^ d)$ and a finite positive Borel measure $\mu $ on $\mathbb R^ d$ we use the Fourier transforms

\[ \widehat f(\xi )=\int _{\mathbb R^ d}f(x)\mathrm e^{-2\pi \mathrm i x\cdot \xi }\, \mathrm d x, \qquad \widehat\mu (\xi )=\int \mathrm e^{-2\pi \mathrm i x\cdot \xi }\, \mathrm d\mu (x). \]

Both functions are bounded, by $\| f\| _1$ and by $\mu (\mathbb R^ d)$, respectively, and they are continuous by dominated convergence.

The Schwartz space $\mathcal S(\mathbb R^ d)$ consists of the smooth complex functions $f$ on $\mathbb R^ d$ such that $x^\alpha \partial ^\beta f$ is bounded for all multi-indices $\alpha ,\beta $. By the Leibniz rule, $\mathcal S(\mathbb R^ d)$ is stable under differentiation and under multiplication by polynomials, and $C_ c^\infty (\mathbb R^ d)\subset \mathcal S(\mathbb R^ d)$. Every derivative $\partial ^\beta f$ of a Schwartz function is integrable: $(1+|x|^2)^ d\partial ^\beta f$ is a linear combination of the bounded functions $x^\alpha \partial ^\beta f$, so $|\partial ^\beta f|\leq C(1+|x|^2)^{-d}$, and $(1+|x|^2)^{-d}$ is integrable by polar coordinates (2.1), because $r^{d-1}(1+r^2)^{-d}\leq \min \{ r^{d-1},r^{-d-1}\} $.

Lemma 6.1.1. The Fourier transform maps $\mathcal S(\mathbb R^ d)$ into itself, and for $f\in \mathcal S(\mathbb R^ d)$ and $1\leq j\leq d$,

6.1
\begin{equation} \widehat{\partial _ jf}(\xi )=2\pi \mathrm i\xi _ j\widehat f(\xi ),\qquad \partial _{\xi _ j}\widehat f=\widehat{(-2\pi \mathrm i x_ jf)}. \end{equation}

Moreover, for $t>0$,

\[ \widehat{\mathrm e^{-\pi t|x|^2}}(\xi )=t^{-d/2}\mathrm e^{-\pi |\xi |^2/t}. \]

Proof. Let $f\in \mathcal S$. We first prove the second rule in (6.1). By the mean value theorem, the difference quotients of $\xi \mapsto \mathrm e^{-2\pi \mathrm i x\cdot \xi }$ in the direction of $\xi _ j$ are bounded by $2\pi |x_ j|$, and $x_ jf$ is integrable. Hence dominated convergence allows us to differentiate under the integral sign: $\partial _{\xi _ j}\widehat f$ exists and is the transform of $-2\pi \mathrm i x_ jf$, which is continuous. Since $x_ jf\in \mathcal S$, induction shows that $\widehat f$ is smooth and that $\partial ^\beta \widehat f$ is the transform of $(-2\pi \mathrm i x)^\beta f$.

For the first rule, Fubini's theorem allows us to integrate in $x_ j$ before the other coordinates. For almost every value of the other coordinates, $f$ and $\partial _ jf$ are integrable on the corresponding line, and an integration by parts over $[-a,a]$ followed by $a\to \infty $ gives

\[ \int _{\mathbb R}\partial _ jf(x)\, \mathrm e^{-2\pi \mathrm i x\cdot \xi }\, \mathrm d x_ j=2\pi \mathrm i\xi _ j\int _{\mathbb R}f(x)\, \mathrm e^{-2\pi \mathrm i x\cdot \xi }\, \mathrm d x_ j, \]

because the boundary terms are bounded by $|f(x)|$ at $x_ j=\pm a$, and $f(x)\to 0$ as $|x|\to \infty $. Integrating over the other coordinates proves the first rule. Combining the two rules, we find that $\xi ^\alpha \partial ^\beta \widehat f$ is a constant multiple of the transform of $\partial ^\alpha (x^\beta f)$. This function belongs to $\mathcal S$ and is therefore integrable, so $\xi ^\alpha \partial ^\beta \widehat f$ is bounded by a multiple of its $L^1$ norm. Hence $\widehat f\in \mathcal S$.

For the Gaussian, consider first $d=1$, put $g(x)=\mathrm e^{-\pi x^2}$, which belongs to $\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$, and set $F=\widehat g$. Since $-2\pi \mathrm i xg=\mathrm i g'$, the two rules give $F'=\mathrm i\, \widehat{g'}=\mathrm i(2\pi \mathrm i\xi )F=-2\pi \xi F$, so $F(\xi )\mathrm e^{\pi \xi ^2}$ is constant. Moreover $F(0)=1$: squaring the positive integral $F(0)$ and using polar coordinates in the plane, where $s_1=2\pi $, gives $F(0)^2=2\pi \int _0^\infty r\mathrm e^{-\pi r^2}\, \mathrm d r=1$. Hence $F(\xi )=\mathrm e^{-\pi \xi ^2}$. In dimension $d$, Fubini's theorem writes the transform of $\mathrm e^{-\pi |x|^2}$ as a product of one-dimensional transforms, which proves the formula for $t=1$. For general $t$, the linear substitution $x=y/\sqrt t$ gives

\[ \int \mathrm e^{-\pi t|x|^2}\mathrm e^{-2\pi \mathrm i x\cdot \xi }\, \mathrm d x=t^{-d/2}\int \mathrm e^{-\pi |y|^2}\mathrm e^{-2\pi \mathrm i y\cdot \xi /\sqrt t}\, \mathrm d y=t^{-d/2}\mathrm e^{-\pi |\xi |^2/t}. \]
$\square$

Theorem 6.1.2 (Plancherel). Every $f\in \mathcal S(\mathbb R^ d)$ satisfies the inversion formula

\[ f(x)=\int \widehat f(\xi )\mathrm e^{2\pi \mathrm i x\cdot \xi }\, \mathrm d\xi ,\qquad x\in \mathbb R^ d. \]

The Fourier transform on $\mathcal S(\mathbb R^ d)$ extends uniquely to a bounded operator on $L^2(\mathbb R^ d)$. This extension is unitary, its inverse is given on Schwartz functions by the integral with the exponential $\mathrm e^{2\pi \mathrm i x\cdot \xi }$, and on $L^1\cap L^2$ it agrees almost everywhere with the integral transform.

Proof. Let $f\in \mathcal S$ and $\varepsilon >0$. Insert the factor $\mathrm e^{-\pi \varepsilon ^2|\xi |^2}$ into the inversion integral. By Fubini's theorem and lemma 6.1.1 with $t=\varepsilon ^2$,

\[ \int \widehat f(\xi )\mathrm e^{2\pi \mathrm i x\cdot \xi }\mathrm e^{-\pi \varepsilon ^2|\xi |^2}\, \mathrm d\xi =f*g_\varepsilon (x), \qquad g_\varepsilon (y)=\varepsilon ^{-d}\mathrm e^{-\pi |y|^2/\varepsilon ^2}. \]

The functions $g_\varepsilon $ are positive with integral one, and the substitution $y=\varepsilon y'$ shows that their mass outside a fixed ball $B(0,\delta )$ tends to zero as $\varepsilon \to 0$. Since $f$ is bounded, and uniformly continuous because its gradient is bounded, splitting the convolution integral into the regions $|y|<\delta $ and $|y|\geq \delta $ shows that $f*g_\varepsilon \to f$ uniformly. On the left-hand side, $\widehat f\in \mathcal S\subset L^1$ by lemma 6.1.1, so dominated convergence lets $\varepsilon \to 0$ under the frequency integral. This proves the inversion formula.

Write $\mathcal F$ for the Fourier transform on $\mathcal S$, $\mathcal F^*$ for the inverse integral transform $\mathcal F^*g(x)=\int g(\xi )\mathrm e^{2\pi \mathrm i x\cdot \xi }\, \mathrm d\xi $, and $Rf(x)=f(-x)$. The substitution $x\mapsto -x$ shows that $\mathcal F^*=R\mathcal F=\mathcal FR$ on $\mathcal S$. In particular $\mathcal F^*$ maps $\mathcal S$ into itself, and both compositions $\mathcal F^*\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal F\mathcal F^*$ equal $R\mathcal F\mathcal F$, which is the identity on $\mathcal S$ by the inversion formula. For $f,g\in \mathcal S$, applying inversion to $g$ and Fubini's theorem, we obtain Parseval's identity

\[ \int f\overline g\, \mathrm d x=\int \widehat f\, \overline{\widehat g}\, \mathrm d\xi ; \]

the interchange of integrals is justified because the double integral of the absolute value is at most $\| f\| _1\| \widehat g\| _1$.

By lemma 1.4.1(5), applied to Lebesgue measure, $C_ c^\infty (\mathbb R^ d)$, and hence $\mathcal S$, is dense in $L^2$. By Parseval's identity, $\mathcal F$ maps sequences in $\mathcal S$ that are Cauchy in $L^2$ to Cauchy sequences, and $L^2$ is complete; therefore $\mathcal F$ extends uniquely from $\mathcal S$ to a bounded operator on $L^2$, and this extension is an isometry. Since $\mathcal F^*=R\mathcal F$, the inverse integral transform extends in the same way. Both compositions of the two extensions are continuous and equal to the identity on the dense subspace $\mathcal S$, hence everywhere. Thus the extension is unitary, with the stated inverse.

Finally let $f\in L^1\cap L^2$. We choose $f_ j\in C_ c^\infty $ converging to $f$ both in $L^1$ and in $L^2$: first replace $f$ by $f\mathbf1_{B(0,R)}$ with $R$ large, and then mollify; by lemma 1.4.1(2) the mollifications converge in both norms, since their supports lie in a fixed compact set. The integral transforms of the $f_ j$ converge uniformly to that of $f$, because the difference is bounded by $\| f_ j-f\| _1$, while by isometry they converge in $L^2$ to the $L^2$ transform of $f$. A subsequence of an $L^2$ convergent sequence converges almost everywhere: choose it so that the squared $L^2$ errors are summable, and apply Tonelli's theorem to the sum of the squared errors. Hence the two transforms of $f$ agree almost everywhere. $\square$

We continue to write $\widehat f$ for the $L^2$ transform of $f\in L^2(\mathbb R^ d)$, and we call its inverse the inverse $L^2$ transform.

Proposition 6.1.3. Let $\mu $ be a finite positive Borel measure on $\mathbb R^ d$ such that $\widehat\mu \in L^2(\mathbb R^ d)$. Then $\mu =g\, \mathrm d x$ for a nonnegative function $g\in L^1\cap L^2$, and $\| g\| _2=\| \widehat\mu \| _2$.

Proof. Let $g\in L^2$ be the inverse $L^2$ transform of $\widehat\mu $, so that $\widehat g=\widehat\mu $. For $\phi \in \mathcal S$ we have

\[ \int \phi \, \mathrm d\mu =\int \widehat\phi (\xi )\widehat\mu (-\xi )\, \mathrm d\xi =\int \phi g\, \mathrm d x. \]

The first equality follows from the inversion formula for $\phi $ and Fubini's theorem. For the second, we apply Parseval's identity to $\phi $ and $\overline g$; it extends from $\mathcal S$ to $L^2$ because the $L^2$ transform is unitary. We also use that the transform of $\overline g$ at $\xi $ is $\overline{\widehat g(-\xi )}$; this holds for Schwartz functions by definition, and it extends to $L^2$ because the maps $g\mapsto \widehat{\overline g}$ and $g\mapsto \overline{\widehat g(-\, \cdot \, )}$ are continuous from $L^2$ to $L^2$.

Consequently, for every test function $\phi $ the number $\int \phi g\, \mathrm d x=\int \phi \, \mathrm d\mu $ is real when $\phi $ is real and nonnegative when $\phi \geq 0$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on compact sets, $g$ is locally integrable, so it defines a positive distribution. By proposition 1.4.3(3) its mollifications $g*\rho _\varepsilon $ are nonnegative, and they converge to $g$ in $L^1$ on compact sets by lemma 1.4.1(2). Hence $g$ is real and nonnegative almost everywhere. The measures $\mu $ and $g\, \mathrm d x$ are Radon by proposition 1.1.2, and they define the same distribution; by proposition 1.4.3(4), $\mu =g\, \mathrm d x$. In particular $\int g\, \mathrm d x=\mu (\mathbb R^ d)<\infty $, and $\| g\| _2=\| \widehat\mu \| _2$ because the $L^2$ transform is unitary. $\square$

For $0<s<d$, the Riesz $s$-energy of a finite positive Borel measure $\mu $ on $\mathbb R^ d$ is

\[ I_ s(\mu )=\iint |x-y|^{-s}\, \mathrm d\mu (x)\, \mathrm d\mu (y)\in [0,\infty ]. \]

The kernel $|x-y|^{-s}$ carries no normalizing constant. For comparison, if $d\geq 3$ and $\mu $ has compact support, the energy $I(\mu )$ of section 4.1, defined with the normalized kernel $\Gamma _ d$, satisfies $(d-2)s_{d-1}I(\mu )=I_{d-2}(\mu )$, where $s_{d-1}$ is the area of the unit sphere $S^{d-1}$ (section 2.1).

Theorem 6.1.4. Let $\mu $ be a finite positive Borel measure on $\mathbb R^ d$, and let $0<s<d$. Then

\[ I_ s(\mu )=b_{d,s}\int _{\mathbb R^ d}|\widehat\mu (\xi )|^2|\xi |^{s-d}\, \mathrm d\xi , \qquad b_{d,s}=\pi ^{s-d/2}\frac{\Gamma ((d-s)/2)}{\Gamma (s/2)}, \]

where $\Gamma (a)=\int _0^\infty v^{a-1}\mathrm e^{-v}\, \mathrm d v$, $a>0$, is Euler's Gamma function. Both sides may be infinite.

Proof. For $z\in \mathbb R^ d$, the substitution $v=\pi t|z|^2$ gives

\[ |z|^{-s}=\frac{\pi ^{s/2}}{\Gamma (s/2)}\int _0^\infty t^{s/2-1}\mathrm e^{-\pi t|z|^2}\, \mathrm d t, \]

and both sides are $+\infty $ when $z=0$. By lemma 6.1.1 with $t$ replaced by $1/t$, we have $\mathrm e^{-\pi t|z|^2}=t^{-d/2}\int \mathrm e^{-\pi |\xi |^2/t}\mathrm e^{-2\pi \mathrm i z\cdot \xi }\, \mathrm d\xi $. Taking $z=x-y$ and integrating with respect to $\mu \times \mu $, we obtain

\[ \iint \mathrm e^{-\pi t|x-y|^2}\, \mathrm d\mu (x)\, \mathrm d\mu (y) =t^{-d/2}\int \mathrm e^{-\pi |\xi |^2/t}|\widehat\mu (\xi )|^2\, \mathrm d\xi ; \]

Fubini's theorem applies here because the Gaussian in $\xi $ is integrable and $\mu \times \mu $ is finite. Now insert the integral formula for $|x-y|^{-s}$ into $I_ s(\mu )$. All integrands are nonnegative, so Tonelli's theorem allows us to integrate first in $(x,y)$, then use the last identity, and finally integrate in $t$ before $\xi $, even when the integrals are infinite:

\[ I_ s(\mu )=\frac{\pi ^{s/2}}{\Gamma (s/2)}\int |\widehat\mu (\xi )|^2\int _0^\infty t^{(s-d)/2-1}\mathrm e^{-\pi |\xi |^2/t}\, \mathrm d t\, \mathrm d\xi . \]

For $\xi \neq 0$, the substitution $v=\pi |\xi |^2/t$ evaluates the inner integral as

\[ \int _0^\infty t^{(s-d)/2-1}\mathrm e^{-\pi |\xi |^2/t}\, \mathrm d t =\pi ^{(s-d)/2}|\xi |^{s-d}\Gamma ((d-s)/2), \]

and the point $\xi =0$ is a Lebesgue null set. Multiplying the constants gives the formula. $\square$

Example 6.1.5. The area of the unit sphere $S^{d-1}\subset \mathbb R^ d$ is

6.2
\begin{equation} s_{d-1}=\frac{2\pi ^{d/2}}{\Gamma (d/2)}. \end{equation}

For $0<s<d$, the probability measure $\mathrm d\mu =\mathrm e^{-\pi |x|^2}\, \mathrm d x$ on $\mathbb R^ d$ satisfies

\[ I_ s(\mu )=\left(\frac\pi 2\right)^{s/2}\frac{\Gamma ((d-s)/2)}{\Gamma (d/2)}. \]

Proof. By lemma 6.1.1 with $\xi =0$ and $t=1$, the Gaussian $\mathrm e^{-\pi |x|^2}$ has integral one. Integrating in polar coordinates and substituting $v=\pi r^2$, we obtain

\[ 1=\int \mathrm e^{-\pi |x|^2}\, \mathrm d x=s_{d-1}\int _0^\infty \mathrm e^{-\pi r^2}r^{d-1}\, \mathrm d r=\frac{s_{d-1}\Gamma (d/2)}{2\pi ^{d/2}}, \]

which proves (6.2). By lemma 6.1.1, $\widehat\mu (\xi )=\mathrm e^{-\pi |\xi |^2}$. Hence theorem 6.1.4 and polar coordinates give

\[ I_ s(\mu )=b_{d,s}\int \mathrm e^{-2\pi |\xi |^2}|\xi |^{s-d}\, \mathrm d\xi =b_{d,s}s_{d-1}\int _0^\infty \mathrm e^{-2\pi r^2}r^{s-1}\, \mathrm d r. \]

The substitution $v=2\pi r^2$ evaluates the radial integral as $\Gamma (s/2)/(2(2\pi )^{s/2})$. Inserting the values of $b_{d,s}$ and $s_{d-1}$ proves the formula. $\square$

6.2 Second derivatives and singular integrals

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

Let $d\geq 1$. For a function $v$ on $\mathbb R^ d$ we write $D^2v=(\partial _ a\partial _ bv)_{a,b=1}^ d$ for its real Hessian. In $L^ p$ norms of vector-valued and matrix-valued functions, vectors carry the Euclidean norm and matrices the Hilbert–Schmidt norm $|M|=(\sum _{a,b}|M_{ab}|^2)^{1/2}$. Constants denoted by $C_ d$ and $C_{d,p}$ depend only on the indicated parameters and may change from line to line.

The Laplacian $\Delta v$ is the trace of $D^2v$. If $d\geq 2$, a bound for $\Delta v$ does not bound $D^2v$ pointwise: a test function equal to $x_1^2-x_2^2$ near the origin has vanishing Laplacian and nonzero Hessian there. We show that $\Delta v$ does bound $D^2v$ in $L^ p$ for $1<p<\infty $ (theorem 6.2.3). On the Fourier side, $\partial _ a\partial _ bv$ is obtained from $-\Delta v$ by multiplication with the bounded function $-\xi _ a\xi _ b/|\xi |^2$, so the case $p=2$ follows from Plancherel's theorem. For the other exponents we represent the corresponding operator, away from the support of a function, by a kernel that is smooth off the diagonal. We then use the Calderón–Zygmund decomposition: on each of its cubes the function minus its average has mean zero, and this cancellation makes the image of that part integrable far from the cube. The section ends with Sobolev spaces, on open subsets of $\mathbb R^ d$ and on compact manifolds, and with Morrey's inequality, which turns $L^ p$ bounds for second derivatives into Hölder continuity of first derivatives.

Lemma 6.2.1. Let $1\leq a,b\leq d$, and define an operator $T_{ab}$ on $L^2(\mathbb R^ d)$ by

\[ \widehat{T_{ab}f}(\xi )=-\frac{\xi _ a\xi _ b}{|\xi |^2}\widehat f(\xi ), \qquad \xi \neq 0, \]

the multiplier being zero at the origin. Then $T_{ab}$ is self-adjoint and has norm at most one. Moreover:

  1. There is a smooth real function $K_{ab}$ on $\mathbb R^ d\setminus \{ 0\} $ such that

    \[ |K_{ab}(z)|\leq C_ d|z|^{-d},\qquad |\nabla K_{ab}(z)|\leq C_ d|z|^{-d-1}. \]

  2. If $f\in L^2(\mathbb R^ d)$ vanishes almost everywhere outside a compact set $F$, then $f$ is integrable, and for almost every $x\in \mathbb R^ d\setminus F$,

    \[ T_{ab}f(x)=\int K_{ab}(x-y)f(y)\, \mathrm d y. \]

  3. For $v\in C_ c^\infty (\mathbb R^ d)$ we have $\partial _ a\partial _ bv=T_{ab}(-\Delta v)$.

Proof. The multiplier is real and bounded by one in absolute value, so Plancherel's theorem shows that $T_{ab}$ is self-adjoint with norm at most one. For $v\in C_ c^\infty $, the first rule in (6.1) (lemma 6.1.1), applied twice, gives $\widehat{-\Delta v}(\xi )=4\pi ^2|\xi |^2\widehat v(\xi )$ and $\widehat{\partial _ a\partial _ bv}(\xi )=-4\pi ^2\xi _ a\xi _ b\widehat v(\xi )$. Multiplying the first identity by the multiplier of $T_{ab}$ gives the second, which proves (3).

To construct the kernel we use the heat kernel $H_ t(z)=(4\pi t)^{-d/2}\mathrm e^{-|z|^2/(4t)}$, $t>0$. By lemma 6.1.1, $\widehat H_ t(\xi )=\mathrm e^{-4\pi ^2t|\xi |^2}$, and hence $\widehat{\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t}(\xi )=-4\pi ^2\xi _ a\xi _ b\mathrm e^{-4\pi ^2t|\xi |^2}$. For $0<\varepsilon <R<\infty $ put

\[ k_{\varepsilon ,R}=\int _\varepsilon ^ R\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t\, \mathrm d t. \]

Since $\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t(z)=\bigl (z_ az_ b/(4t^2)-\delta _{ab}/(2t)\bigr )H_ t(z)$, where $\delta _{ab}$ is the Kronecker symbol, we have

\[ |\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t(z)|\leq C_ dt^{-d/2-1}(1+|z|^2/t)\mathrm e^{-|z|^2/(4t)}. \]

Hence $\sup _ z|\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t(z)|\leq C_ dt^{-d/2-1}$, and the substitution $z=\sqrt t\, w$ gives $\int |\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t(z)|\, \mathrm d z\leq C_ dt^{-1}$. Therefore the kernel $k_{\varepsilon ,R}$ is bounded and integrable, and Fubini's theorem, applied with the integrable majorant $|\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t(z)|$ on $\mathbb R^ d\times [\varepsilon ,R]$, gives its Fourier transform:

\[ \widehat{k_{\varepsilon ,R}}(\xi )=-\frac{\xi _ a\xi _ b}{|\xi |^2} \bigl (\mathrm e^{-4\pi ^2\varepsilon |\xi |^2} -\mathrm e^{-4\pi ^2R|\xi |^2}\bigr ),\qquad \xi \neq 0. \]

Let $f\in L^1\cap L^2$. Then $k_{\varepsilon ,R}*f$ is bounded and integrable, hence in $L^2$, and by Fubini's theorem and the last assertion of theorem 6.1.2 its Fourier transform is $\widehat{k_{\varepsilon ,R}}\widehat f$. As $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ and $R\uparrow \infty $, these multipliers are bounded by one and converge to $-\xi _ a\xi _ b/|\xi |^2$ for $\xi \neq 0$. By dominated convergence on the Fourier side and Plancherel's theorem, $k_{\varepsilon ,R}*f\to T_{ab}f$ in $L^2$.

For $z\neq 0$ define

\[ K_{ab}(z)=\int _0^\infty \partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t(z)\, \mathrm d t. \]

Since $H_ t(z)=t^{-d/2}H_1(z/\sqrt t)$, every derivative satisfies

\[ \partial ^\gamma H_ t(z)=t^{-(d+|\gamma |)/2}(\partial ^\gamma H_1)(z/\sqrt t), \]

and induction on $|\gamma |$ shows that $\partial ^\gamma H_1(w)=P_\gamma (w)\mathrm e^{-|w|^2/4}$ for a polynomial $P_\gamma $, of degree $m_\gamma $ say. Fix $0<c\leq c'$, and let $c\leq |z|\leq c'$ and $|\gamma |\geq 2$. Since $|P_\gamma (w)|\leq C_\gamma (1+|w|)^{m_\gamma }$ and $\mathrm e^{-|z|^2/(4t)}\leq \mathrm e^{-c^2/(4t)}$, we obtain $|\partial ^\gamma H_ t(z)|\leq g_\gamma (t)$, where

\[ g_\gamma (t)=\begin{cases} C_\gamma t^{-(d+|\gamma |)/2}(1+c't^{-1/2})^{m_\gamma }\mathrm e^{-c^2/(4t)}, & 0<t\leq 1,\\ C_\gamma (1+c')^{m_\gamma }t^{-(d+|\gamma |)/2}, & t\geq 1. \end{cases} \]

This function is integrable on $(0,\infty )$ and does not depend on $z$: near $t=0$ the factor $\mathrm e^{-c^2/(4t)}$ dominates every power of $t^{-1}$, and for $t\geq 1$ the exponent satisfies $(d+|\gamma |)/2\geq 3/2>1$. By the mean value theorem and dominated convergence, we may therefore differentiate under the integral sign any number of times. Hence $K_{ab}$ is smooth on $\mathbb R^ d\setminus \{ 0\} $, and its derivatives are the integrals of the corresponding derivatives of $\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t$. Moreover, the integrals $\int _0^\varepsilon $ and $\int _ R^\infty $ of $|\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t(z)|$ tend to zero as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ and $R\uparrow \infty $, uniformly for $c\leq |z|\leq c'$.

Differentiating the identity $H_{r^2s}(rz)=r^{-d}H_ s(z)$ twice in $z$ and substituting $t=r^2s$, we find that $K_{ab}(rz)=r^{-d}K_{ab}(z)$ for $r>0$. Thus $K_{ab}$ is homogeneous of degree $-d$ and $\nabla K_{ab}$ is homogeneous of degree $-d-1$, and the bounds in (1) follow from the boundedness of the continuous functions $K_{ab}$ and $\nabla K_{ab}$ on the unit sphere. The same scaling shows that the function $z\mapsto \int _0^\infty |\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t(z)|\, \mathrm d t$ is homogeneous of degree $-d$; it is bounded on the unit sphere by $\int _0^\infty g_\gamma (t)\, \mathrm d t$, where $\partial ^\gamma =\partial _ a\partial _ b$ and $c=c'=1$, so it is at most $C_ d|z|^{-d}$.

To prove (2), let $f$ and $F$ be as there. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on $F$, the function $f$ is integrable, so $f\in L^1\cap L^2$. Let $L$ be a compact set disjoint from $F$. For $x\in L$,

\[ k_{\varepsilon ,R}*f(x)=\int f(y)\int _\varepsilon ^ R\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t(x-y)\, \mathrm d t\, \mathrm d y, \]

where the outer integral may be taken over $F$. The differences $x-y$, with $x\in L$ and $y\in F$, form a compact set not containing the origin, on which $c\leq |x-y|\leq c'$ for some $0<c\leq c'$. By the uniform estimates just proved, the inner integral converges to $K_{ab}(x-y)$ uniformly on this set, and it is bounded by $\int _0^\infty |\partial _ a\partial _ bH_ t(x-y)|\, \mathrm d t\leq C_ dc^{-d}$. Hence, since $f$ is integrable, $k_{\varepsilon ,R}*f$ converges uniformly on $L$ to $\int K_{ab}(x-y)f(y)\, \mathrm d y$. Since it also converges to $T_{ab}f$ in $L^2$, a subsequence converges almost everywhere to $T_{ab}f$, and the two limits agree almost everywhere on $L$. Covering the open set $\mathbb R^ d\setminus F$ by countably many such compact sets proves (2). For $d=1$ the kernel vanishes, in accordance with $T_{11}=-1$: a direct differentiation gives $\partial _ z^2H_ t=\bigl (z^2/(4t^2)-1/(2t)\bigr )H_ t=\partial _ tH_ t$, so $K_{11}(z)=\lim _{t\uparrow \infty }H_ t(z)-\lim _{t\downarrow 0}H_ t(z)=0$ for $z\neq 0$. $\square$

Lemma 6.2.2. Let $T_{ab}$ be the operator of lemma 6.2.1, let $f\in L^1(\mathbb R^ d)\cap L^2(\mathbb R^ d)$ and let $\lambda >0$. Then

\[ |\{ x:|T_{ab}f(x)|>\lambda \} |\leq \frac{C_ d}{\lambda }\| f\| _1. \]

Proof. We use the Calderón–Zygmund decomposition of $f$ at height $\lambda $ into a bounded part $g$, handled by the $L^2$ bound for $T_{ab}$, and parts $b_ Q$ of mean zero on disjoint cubes $Q$, handled by lemma 6.2.1. The dyadic cubes are the cubes $2^ k(m+[0,1)^ d)$ with $k\in \mathbb Z$ and $m\in \mathbb Z^ d$. Two dyadic cubes are either disjoint or nested, and each dyadic cube $Q$ lies in a unique dyadic cube of twice its side length, its parent, which has volume $2^ d|Q|$. Call a dyadic cube $Q$ selected if the average of $|f|$ over $Q$ exceeds $\lambda $, while the average over every strictly larger dyadic cube containing $Q$ does not. Every dyadic cube on which the average of $|f|$ exceeds $\lambda $ is contained in a selected cube, because the averages over its successive ancestors are at most $\| f\| _1$ divided by their volumes and therefore tend to zero. The selected cubes are pairwise disjoint. For each of them, the average over its parent is at most $\lambda $, and hence

\[ \lambda <\frac1{|Q|}\int _ Q|f|\leq 2^ d\lambda ,\qquad \sum _ Q|Q|\leq \frac1\lambda \sum _ Q\int _ Q|f|\leq \frac1\lambda \| f\| _1. \]

Moreover, $|f|\leq \lambda $ almost everywhere outside the union of the selected cubes. Indeed, let $x$ be a point outside this union at which the conclusion of theorem 1.7.3 holds. Every dyadic cube containing $x$ has average of $|f|$ at most $\lambda $, since otherwise it would lie in a selected cube. The dyadic cube of side $2^ k$ containing $x$ lies in the cube $x+(-2^ k,2^ k)^ d$, whose volume is $2^ d$ times larger. Hence the average of $|f-f(x)|$ over it tends to zero as $k\to -\infty $, so its average of $|f|$ tends to $|f(x)|$, and therefore $|f(x)|\leq \lambda $.

For a selected cube $Q$, let $f_ Q=|Q|^{-1}\int _ Qf$ be the average of $f$. Define $g=f$ outside the selected cubes and $g=f_ Q$ on each selected cube $Q$, and put $b_ Q=(f-f_ Q)\mathbf1_ Q$. Each $b_ Q$ has integral zero, and

\[ \| g\| _1\leq \| f\| _1,\quad \| g\| _\infty \leq 2^ d\lambda , \quad \| g\| _2^2\leq 2^ d\lambda \| f\| _1, \qquad \sum _ Q\| b_ Q\| _1\leq 2\| f\| _1. \]

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $|Q||f_ Q|^2\leq \int _ Q|f|^2$, so $\| b_ Q\| _2\leq 2\| f\mathbf1_ Q\| _2$. Since the $b_ Q$ have disjoint supports, the series $\sum _ Qb_ Q$ converges to $f-g$ in $L^2$.

Let $c_ Q$ be the center of $Q$, and let $Q^*$ be the cube with center $c_ Q$ and $4\sqrt d$ times the side length of $Q$. If $x\notin Q^*$ and $y\in Q$, then $|x-c_ Q|\geq 2\operatorname {diam}Q$ and $|y-c_ Q|\leq \operatorname {diam}Q/2$, so every point of the segment from $x-c_ Q$ to $x-y$ has norm at least $|x-c_ Q|/2$. The function $b_ Q\in L^2$ vanishes outside the compact set $\overline Q$, which does not meet $\mathbb R^ d\setminus Q^*$, and it has integral zero. Therefore parts (1) and (2) of lemma 6.2.1 and the mean value theorem give, for almost every $x\notin Q^*$,

\[ \begin{aligned} |T_{ab}b_ Q(x)| & =\left|\int _ Q\bigl (K_{ab}(x-y)-K_{ab}(x-c_ Q)\bigr )b_ Q(y)\, \mathrm d y\right|\\ & \leq C_ d\frac{\operatorname {diam}Q}{|x-c_ Q|^{d+1}}\| b_ Q\| _1. \end{aligned} \]

Integrating in polar coordinates about $c_ Q$ over the region $|x-c_ Q|\geq 2\operatorname {diam}Q$, which contains $\mathbb R^ d\setminus Q^*$, we obtain

\[ \int _{\mathbb R^ d\setminus Q^*}|T_{ab}b_ Q|\leq C_ d\| b_ Q\| _1. \]

Put $B^*=\bigcup _ QQ^*$. Then $|B^*|\leq (4\sqrt d)^ d\sum _ Q|Q|\leq C_ d\lambda ^{-1}\| f\| _1$. Enumerate the selected cubes, and let $S_ N$ be the partial sums of $\sum _ Qb_ Q$. Since $S_ N\to f-g$ in $L^2$, also $T_{ab}S_ N\to T_{ab}(f-g)$ in $L^2$, and a subsequence converges almost everywhere. Moreover $\int _{\mathbb R^ d\setminus B^*}|T_{ab}S_ N|\leq \sum _ Q\int _{\mathbb R^ d\setminus Q^*}|T_{ab}b_ Q|\leq C_ d\| f\| _1$, so Fatou's lemma gives

\[ \int _{\mathbb R^ d\setminus B^*}|T_{ab}(f-g)|\leq C_ d\| f\| _1. \]

Finally, $T_{ab}f=T_{ab}g+T_{ab}(f-g)$, and the set where $|T_{ab}f|>\lambda $ is contained in the union of $B^*$, the set where $|T_{ab}g|>\lambda /2$, and the set of $x\notin B^*$ with $|T_{ab}(f-g)(x)|>\lambda /2$. By Chebyshev's inequality and the $L^2$ bound for $T_{ab}$, the second set has measure at most $4\lambda ^{-2}\| g\| _2^2\leq 2^{d+2}\lambda ^{-1}\| f\| _1$, and the third has measure at most $2\lambda ^{-1}\int _{\mathbb R^ d\setminus B^*}|T_{ab}(f-g)|\leq C_ d\lambda ^{-1}\| f\| _1$. Together with the bound for $|B^*|$, this proves the lemma. $\square$

Theorem 6.2.3. Let $1<p<\infty $, and let $T_{ab}$ be the operators of lemma 6.2.1. There is a constant $C_{d,p}$ such that $\| T_{ab}f\| _ p\leq C_{d,p}\| f\| _ p$ for all $a,b$ and all $f\in L^ p\cap L^2$; consequently each $T_{ab}$ extends uniquely to a bounded operator on $L^ p(\mathbb R^ d)$. In particular

6.7
\begin{equation} \| D^2v\| _ p\leq C_{d,p}\| \Delta v\| _ p, \qquad v\in C_ c^\infty (\mathbb R^ d). \end{equation}

Proof. Assume first that $1<p<2$, and let $f\in L^ p\cap L^2$. Fix $\lambda >0$ and split $f=f_1+f_2$ with $f_1=f\mathbf1_{\{ |f|>\lambda \} }$ and $f_2=f\mathbf1_{\{ |f|\leq \lambda \} }$. Since $|f_1|\leq \lambda ^{1-p}|f|^ p$, we have $f_1\in L^1\cap L^2$, and $f_2\in L^2$. Apply lemma 6.2.2 to $f_1$, and Chebyshev's inequality with the $L^2$ bound to $f_2$, both with threshold $\lambda /2$. This gives

\[ |\{ |T_{ab}f|>\lambda \} | \leq \frac{C_ d}{\lambda }\int _{\{ |f|>\lambda \} }|f| +\frac{C_ d}{\lambda ^2}\int _{\{ |f|\leq \lambda \} }|f|^2. \]

For a measurable function $h$, Tonelli's theorem applied to $|h(x)|^ p=\int _0^{|h(x)|}p\lambda ^{p-1}\, \mathrm d\lambda $ expresses $\| h\| _ p^ p$ as the integral of $p\lambda ^{p-1}$ times the distribution function $\lambda \mapsto |\{ |h|>\lambda \} |$, also when the norm is infinite. We apply this to $h=T_{ab}f$: multiply the last inequality by $p\lambda ^{p-1}$ and integrate in $\lambda $. By Tonelli's theorem again, the first term on the right contributes $C_ d\int |f|\int _0^{|f|}p\lambda ^{p-2}\, \mathrm d\lambda \, \mathrm d x=C_ dp\| f\| _ p^ p/(p-1)$, and the second contributes $C_ d\int |f|^2\int _{|f|}^\infty p\lambda ^{p-3}\, \mathrm d\lambda \, \mathrm d x=C_ dp\| f\| _ p^ p/(2-p)$; the inner integrals converge precisely because $p>1$ and $p<2$. Therefore

\[ \| T_{ab}f\| _ p^ p \leq C_ dp\left(\frac1{p-1}+\frac1{2-p}\right)\| f\| _ p^ p. \]

For $p=2$ the bound is Plancherel's theorem.

Now let $2<p<\infty $, put $p'=p/(p-1)\in (1,2)$, let $f\in L^ p\cap L^2$, and write $h=T_{ab}f\in L^2$. For integers $N\geq 1$, put $E_ N=B(0,N)\cap \{ |h|\leq N\} $ and $g_ N=\mathbf1_{E_ N}h|h|^{p-2}$. Then $|g_ N|\leq N^{p-1}\mathbf1_{E_ N}$, so $g_ N\in L^2\cap L^{p'}$, and $|g_ N|^{p'}=\mathbf1_{E_ N}|h|^ p$. Since $T_{ab}$ is self-adjoint on $L^2$, the case $p'$ already proved and Hölder's inequality give

\[ \int _{E_ N}|h|^ p=\int h\overline{g_ N} =\int f\overline{T_{ab}g_ N} \leq C_{d,p'}\| f\| _ p\left(\int _{E_ N}|h|^ p\right)^{1/p'}. \]

The integral on the left is finite. If it is nonzero, dividing by its $(1/p')$th power gives $(\int _{E_ N}|h|^ p)^{1/p}\leq C_{d,p'}\| f\| _ p$; if it is zero, this bound is trivial. The sets $E_ N$ increase to $\{ |h|<\infty \} $, which has full measure since $h\in L^2$, so monotone convergence gives $\| T_{ab}f\| _ p\leq C_{d,p'}\| f\| _ p$.

By lemma 1.4.1(5), $C_ c^\infty \subset L^ p\cap L^2$ is dense in $L^ p$, and $L^ p$ is complete, so $T_{ab}$ has a unique bounded extension from $L^ p\cap L^2$ to $L^ p$. Finally, for $v\in C_ c^\infty $, lemma 6.2.1(3) gives $\| \partial _ a\partial _ bv\| _ p=\| T_{ab}\Delta v\| _ p\leq C_{d,p}\| \Delta v\| _ p$, and summing over $a$ and $b$ proves (6.7). $\square$

For an integer $k\geq 0$, $1\leq p<\infty $ and an open set $U\subset \mathbb R^ d$, the Sobolev space $W^{k,p}(U)$ consists of the functions in $L^ p(U)$ whose distributional derivatives of order at most $k$ belong to $L^ p(U)$, with the norm $\| v\| _{W^{k,p}(U)}=\sum _{|\alpha |\leq k}\| \partial ^\alpha v\| _{L^ p(U)}$. We write $W^{k,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$ for the space of functions whose restrictions to every open set $V\Subset U$ belong to $W^{k,p}(V)$. If $v\in W^{k,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$, $V\Subset U$ is open and $|\alpha |\leq k$, then $\partial ^\alpha (v*\rho _\varepsilon )=(\partial ^\alpha v)*\rho _\varepsilon \to \partial ^\alpha v$ in $L^ p(V)$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$, by proposition 1.4.3(1) and lemma 1.4.1(2).

Lemma 6.2.4. Let $k\geq 0$ and $1\leq p<\infty $, let $U,V\subset \mathbb R^ d$ be open, and let $v\in W^{k,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$.

  1. If $\chi \in C^\infty (U)$, then $\chi v\in W^{k,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$, and $\partial ^\alpha (\chi v)=\sum _{\beta \leq \alpha }\binom \alpha \beta \partial ^{\alpha -\beta }\chi \, \partial ^\beta v$ for $|\alpha |\leq k$. If moreover $\operatorname{Supp}\chi $ is a compact subset of $U$, then $\chi v$, extended by zero, belongs to $W^{k,p}(\mathbb R^ d)$, its derivatives are given by the same formula extended by zero, and $\| \chi v\| _{W^{k,p}(\mathbb R^ d)}\leq C\| v\| _{W^{k,p}(W)}$ for every open set $W$ with $\operatorname{Supp}\chi \subset W\subset U$, where $C$ depends only on $d$, $k$ and $\chi $.

  2. If $\Phi \colon V\to U$ is a $C^\infty $ diffeomorphism, then $v\circ \Phi \in W^{k,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(V)$. If moreover $v$ vanishes almost everywhere outside a compact set $K\subset U$, then $v\circ \Phi $ vanishes almost everywhere outside the compact set $\Phi ^{-1}(K)$, and $\| v\circ \Phi \| _{W^{k,p}(V)}\leq C\| v\| _{W^{k,p}(U)}$, where $C$ depends only on $k$, $p$, $\Phi $ and $K$.

Proof. (1) For $k=0$ the first assertion holds because $\chi $ is bounded on compact sets. Let $k\geq 1$ and $1\leq j\leq d$. For a test function $\phi $ on $U$, the function $\chi \phi $ is a test function with $\partial _ j(\chi \phi )=\chi \partial _ j\phi +\phi \partial _ j\chi $, so

\[ \int \chi v\, \partial _ j\phi \, \mathrm d x=\int v\, \partial _ j(\chi \phi )\, \mathrm d x-\int v\, \partial _ j\chi \, \phi \, \mathrm d x=-\int \bigl (\chi \partial _ jv+v\partial _ j\chi \bigr )\phi \, \mathrm d x. \]

Thus $\partial _ j(\chi v)=\chi \partial _ jv+(\partial _ j\chi )v$. The functions $\partial _ jv$ and $v$ belong to $W^{k-1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$, so by induction on $k$ both terms belong to $W^{k-1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$; hence $\chi v\in W^{k,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$, and iterating the formula for first derivatives gives the Leibniz formula, exactly as for smooth functions.

Now let $\operatorname{Supp}\chi $ be a compact subset of $U$, and let $W$ be as in the statement. By the Leibniz formula, $\chi v$ and its derivatives of order at most $k$ vanish almost everywhere outside $\operatorname{Supp}\chi $, and there they are bounded by $C\sum _{|\beta |\leq k}|\partial ^\beta v|$, where $C$ depends on $d$, $k$ and the derivatives of $\chi $ of order at most $k$. Hence their $L^ p$ norms are at most $C\| v\| _{W^{k,p}(W)}$, which is finite when $W\Subset U$. To identify the derivatives of the extension by zero, let $\phi $ be a test function on $\mathbb R^ d$, and choose a smooth cutoff function $\zeta $ for $\operatorname{Supp}\chi $ in $U$ which equals $1$ on a neighborhood $N$ of $\operatorname{Supp}\chi $ (lemma 1.4.1(4)). Then $\zeta \phi $ is a test function on $U$, and $\partial ^\alpha (\zeta \phi )=\partial ^\alpha \phi $ on $N$. Since $\chi v$ and $\partial ^\alpha (\chi v)$ vanish almost everywhere outside $\operatorname{Supp}\chi $,

\[ \begin{split} \int \chi v\, \partial ^\alpha \phi \, \mathrm d x& =\int \chi v\, \partial ^\alpha (\zeta \phi )\, \mathrm d x\\ & =(-1)^{|\alpha |}\int \partial ^\alpha (\chi v)\, \zeta \phi \, \mathrm d x=(-1)^{|\alpha |}\int \partial ^\alpha (\chi v)\, \phi \, \mathrm d x. \end{split} \]

This proves (1).

(2) The composite $v\circ \Phi $ is defined almost everywhere: if $Z\subset U$ is a Borel null set, the change of variables formula for the diffeomorphism $\Phi ^{-1}$ gives $|\Phi ^{-1}(Z)|=\int _ Z|\det D\Phi ^{-1}(x)|\, \mathrm d x=0$. For a smooth function $w$ on $U$ and $1\leq |\alpha |\leq k$, the classical chain rule gives

\[ \partial ^\alpha (w\circ \Phi )=\sum _{1\leq |\beta |\leq |\alpha |}c_{\alpha \beta }\, (\partial ^\beta w)\circ \Phi \qquad \text{on }V, \]

with smooth functions $c_{\alpha \beta }$ on $V$ that depend only on $\Phi $: for $|\alpha |=1$ this is $\partial _ j(w\circ \Phi )=\sum _ i\partial _ j\Phi _ i\, (\partial _ iw)\circ \Phi $, and differentiating the formula once more, with the product rule and the chain rule applied to each $(\partial ^\beta w)\circ \Phi $, gives the induction step.

Assume first that $v$ vanishes almost everywhere outside the compact set $K$. By (1), applied with a smooth cutoff function equal to $1$ near $K$, the extension of $v$ by zero belongs to $W^{k,p}(\mathbb R^ d)$. Its derivatives of order at most $k$ vanish almost everywhere outside $K$, because $v$ vanishes almost everywhere on the open set $\mathbb R^ d\setminus K$ and hence so do its distributional derivatives. Choose $\delta >0$ such that the compact set $K'=\{ x:\operatorname {dist}(x,K)\leq \delta \} $ lies in $U$. For $\varepsilon <\delta $, the mollification $v_\varepsilon =v*\rho _\varepsilon $ is smooth and vanishes outside $K'$, and $\partial ^\beta v_\varepsilon =(\partial ^\beta v)*\rho _\varepsilon \to \partial ^\beta v$ in $L^ p(\mathbb R^ d)$ for $|\beta |\leq k$, by the remark preceding the lemma, applied on $\mathbb R^ d$ to a bounded open set containing $K'$, outside of which both sides vanish.

Let $J$ be the maximum of $|\det D\Phi ^{-1}|$ on $K'$, and let $M\geq 1$ bound the functions $|c_{\alpha \beta }|$ on the compact set $\Phi ^{-1}(K')$. By the change of variables formula,

\[ \int _ V|g\circ \Phi |^ p\, \mathrm d y=\int _ U|g(x)|^ p|\det D\Phi ^{-1}(x)|\, \mathrm d x\leq J\| g\| _ p^ p \]

for every measurable function $g$ on $U$ that vanishes outside $K'$. The smooth functions $w_\varepsilon =v_\varepsilon \circ \Phi $ vanish outside $\Phi ^{-1}(K')$. Applying this inequality to $v_\varepsilon -v$ and its derivatives, we find that $w_\varepsilon \to v\circ \Phi $ and, for $1\leq |\alpha |\leq k$,

\[ \partial ^\alpha w_\varepsilon \to G_\alpha =\sum _{1\leq |\beta |\leq |\alpha |}c_{\alpha \beta }\, (\partial ^\beta v)\circ \Phi \qquad \text{in }L^ p(V), \]

with $\| G_\alpha \| _ p\leq MJ^{1/p}\sum _{1\leq |\beta |\leq |\alpha |}\| \partial ^\beta v\| _ p$. For a test function $\phi $ on $V$, we obtain

\[ \begin{split} \int (v\circ \Phi )\, \partial ^\alpha \phi \, \mathrm d y& =\lim _{\varepsilon \to 0}\int w_\varepsilon \, \partial ^\alpha \phi \, \mathrm d y\\ & =(-1)^{|\alpha |}\lim _{\varepsilon \to 0}\int \partial ^\alpha w_\varepsilon \, \phi \, \mathrm d y=(-1)^{|\alpha |}\int G_\alpha \phi \, \mathrm d y. \end{split} \]

Hence $\partial ^\alpha (v\circ \Phi )=G_\alpha \in L^ p(V)$. These functions, and $v\circ \Phi $, vanish almost everywhere outside $\Phi ^{-1}(K)$, and summing the bounds over $|\alpha |\leq k$ gives $\| v\circ \Phi \| _{W^{k,p}(V)}\leq C\| v\| _{W^{k,p}(U)}$, with $C$ depending only on $k$, $p$, $J$ and $M$, that is, on $k$, $p$, $\Phi $ and $K$ once $\delta $ has been fixed in terms of $K$ and $U$.

In general, let $V'\Subset V$ be open. Choose a smooth cutoff function $\chi $ for the compact set $\Phi (\overline{V'})$ in $U$ (lemma 1.4.1(4)). By (1), $\chi v\in W^{k,p}(U)$, and it vanishes outside $\operatorname{Supp}\chi $; by the case just treated, $(\chi v)\circ \Phi \in W^{k,p}(V)$. Since $(\chi v)\circ \Phi =v\circ \Phi $ on $V'$, we obtain $v\circ \Phi \in W^{k,p}(V')$. Hence $v\circ \Phi \in W^{k,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(V)$. $\square$

Lemma 6.2.5. Let $1\leq p<\infty $ and $\varepsilon >0$. There is a constant $C_{d,\varepsilon }$, depending only on $d$ and $\varepsilon $, such that

\[ \| \nabla v\| _ p\leq \varepsilon \| D^2v\| _ p+C_{d,\varepsilon }\| v\| _ p, \qquad v\in C_ c^\infty (\mathbb R^ d). \]

Proof. Let $e_ a$ be the $a$th coordinate vector and $h>0$. Taylor's formula with integral remainder gives

\[ h\partial _ av(x)=v(x+he_ a)-v(x) -\int _0^ h(h-t)\partial _ a^2v(x+te_ a)\, \mathrm d t. \]

The $L^ p$ norm is invariant under translation. Moreover, $\omega (t)\, \mathrm d t=2h^{-2}(h-t)\, \mathrm d t$ is a probability measure on $[0,h]$, so Hölder's inequality gives $|\int _0^ hg\, \omega \, \mathrm d t|^ p\leq \int _0^ h|g|^ p\omega \, \mathrm d t$ for every measurable function $g$ on $[0,h]$. Applying this to $g(t)=\partial _ a^2v(x+te_ a)$, integrating in $x$ and using Tonelli's theorem, we obtain

\[ \Bigl \| \int _0^ h(h-t)\partial _ a^2v(\cdot +te_ a)\, \mathrm d t\Bigr \| _ p\leq \frac{h^2}2\Bigl (\int _0^ h\| \partial _ a^2v(\cdot +te_ a)\| _ p^ p\, \omega (t)\, \mathrm d t\Bigr )^{1/p}=\frac{h^2}2\| \partial _ a^2v\| _ p. \]

Hence $h\| \partial _ av\| _ p\leq 2\| v\| _ p+(h^2/2)\| \partial _ a^2v\| _ p$. Since $|\nabla v|\leq \sum _ a|\partial _ av|$ and $|\partial _ a^2v|\leq |D^2v|$, summing over $a$ yields $\| \nabla v\| _ p\leq 2dh^{-1}\| v\| _ p+(dh/2)\| D^2v\| _ p$. The choice $h=2\varepsilon /d$ proves the lemma with $C_{d,\varepsilon }=d^2/\varepsilon $. $\square$

For a compact set $F\subset \mathbb R^ d$ and $0<\alpha \leq 1$, the Hölder norm of a function $v$ on $F$ is

\[ \| v\| _{C^{0,\alpha }(F)}=\sup _ F|v|+\sup _{x,y\in F,\ x\neq y}\frac{|v(x)-v(y)|}{|x-y|^\alpha }. \]

A continuously differentiable function on an open set $U$ is of class $C^{1,\alpha }$ on a compact set $F\subset U$ if its first derivatives have finite $C^{0,\alpha }(F)$ norm, and it belongs to $C^{1,\alpha }_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$ if it is of class $C^{1,\alpha }$ on every compact subset of $U$.

Lemma 6.2.6 (Morrey's inequality). Let $d<p<\infty $, put $\alpha =1-d/p$, and let $B'\Subset B$ be Euclidean balls in $\mathbb R^ d$. Every $v\in W^{1,p}(B)$ has a representative that is continuous on $B$ and satisfies

\[ \| v\| _{C^{0,\alpha }(\overline{B'})}\leq C\| v\| _{W^{1,p}(B)}, \]

where $C$ depends only on $d,p,B',B$. Consequently, for an open set $U\subset \mathbb R^ d$, every function in $W^{2,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$ has a representative in $C^{1,\alpha }_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$.

Proof. We prove the estimate for smooth $v$, pass to general $v$ by mollification, and then apply the result to first derivatives. Assume first that $v$ is smooth on $B$. Let $Q\subset B$ be a ball of radius $r$, let $v_ Q=|Q|^{-1}\int _ Qv$ be the average of $v$ over $Q$, and let $x\in Q$. We claim that

\[ |v(x)-v_ Q|\leq C_ d\int _ Q|\nabla v(z)|\, |x-z|^{1-d}\, \mathrm d z. \]

For $z\in Q$, write $z=x+s\theta $ with $s=|z-x|$ and $\theta \in S^{d-1}$; then $|v(z)-v(x)|\leq \int _0^ s|\nabla v(x+t\theta )|\, \mathrm d t$, and $|v(x)-v_ Q|$ is at most the average over $z\in Q$ of these segment integrals. Since $Q$ is convex and open and contains $x$, for each $\theta $ the radii $s>0$ with $x+s\theta \in Q$ form an interval $0<s<R(\theta )$, and $R(\theta )\leq 2r$. In polar coordinates about $x$ ((2.1) on $B(x,2r)\supset Q$), the average of the segment integrals is

\[ \frac1{|Q|}\int _{S^{d-1}}\int _0^{R(\theta )}s^{d-1}\int _0^ s|\nabla v(x+t\theta )|\, \mathrm d t\, \mathrm d s\, \mathrm d S(\theta ). \]

Interchanging the integrals in $s$ and $t$ produces the factor $|Q|^{-1}\int _ t^{R(\theta )}s^{d-1}\, \mathrm d s$, which is at most $(2r)^ d/(d|Q|)$ and hence bounded by a constant depending only on $d$. The remaining integral $\int _{S^{d-1}}\int _0^{R(\theta )}|\nabla v(x+t\theta )|\, \mathrm d t\, \mathrm d S(\theta )$ is the integral on the right side of the claim, written in polar coordinates. This proves the claim.

By Hölder's inequality with $p'=p/(p-1)$, the right side of the claim is at most $C_{d,p}r^{1-d/p}\| \nabla v\| _{L^ p(Q)}$, because

\[ \int _ Q|x-z|^{(1-d)p'}\, \mathrm d z\leq \int _{B(x,2r)}|x-z|^{(1-d)p'}\, \mathrm d z=C_{d,p}r^{(1-d)p'+d}, \]

and this integral is finite exactly when $(1-d)p'>-d$, that is, when $p>d$. (For $d=1$ the exponent $(1-d)p'$ is zero.)

Let $\delta >0$ be the distance from $B'$ to $\mathbb R^ d\setminus B$. If $x,y\in B'$ and $0<|x-y|<\delta /2$, the ball $Q=B(x,2|x-y|)$ lies in $B$ and contains $x$ and $y$. Applying the preceding bound at both points gives

\[ |v(x)-v(y)|\leq C_{d,p}|x-y|^{1-d/p}\| \nabla v\| _{L^ p(B)}. \]

For $x\in B'$, the same bound on the ball $Q=B(x,\delta /2)\subset B$, together with the estimate $|v_ Q|\leq |Q|^{-1/p}\| v\| _{L^ p(B)}$ from Hölder's inequality, gives $\sup _{B'}|v|\leq C\| v\| _{W^{1,p}(B)}$ with $C$ depending on $d,p,\delta $. If $|x-y|\geq \delta /2$, then $|v(x)-v(y)|/|x-y|^\alpha \leq 2(\delta /2)^{-\alpha }\sup _{B'}|v|$. These bounds prove the estimate for smooth $v$ on $B'$, and hence on $\overline{B'}$, since $v$ is continuous on $B\supset \overline{B'}$.

For general $v\in W^{1,p}(B)$, choose a ball $B''$, depending only on $B'$ and $B$, with $B'\Subset B''\Subset B$, and let $v_\varepsilon =v*\rho _\varepsilon $, which is smooth on $B''$ for small $\varepsilon $. By the remark preceding lemma 6.2.4, $\partial _ av_\varepsilon =(\partial _ av)_\varepsilon $, and $v_\varepsilon \to v$ and $\partial _ av_\varepsilon \to \partial _ av$ in $L^ p(B'')$. The estimate for smooth functions, applied on $B'\Subset B''$ to the differences of the $v_\varepsilon $, shows that they form a Cauchy family in the norm of $C^{0,\alpha }(\overline{B'})$. Their uniform limit is continuous on $\overline{B'}$, agrees almost everywhere with $v$, and satisfies the asserted estimate, because $\| v\| _{W^{1,p}(B'')}\leq \| v\| _{W^{1,p}(B)}$. Two continuous functions that agree almost everywhere on an open set agree everywhere on it. Hence the continuous representatives obtained from different balls $B'\Subset B$ agree on their overlaps and define a single continuous representative on $B$.

Finally, let $v\in W^{2,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$. On every ball $B\Subset U$, both $v$ and its first derivatives $\partial _ av$ belong to $W^{1,p}(B)$. By the preceding paragraph, on each smaller ball $B'\Subset B$ the mollifications $v_\varepsilon $ and $\partial _ av_\varepsilon =(\partial _ av)_\varepsilon $ converge uniformly to the continuous representatives $\tilde v$ of $v$ and $\tilde w_ a$ of $\partial _ av$. Passing to the limit in

\[ v_\varepsilon (x+he_ a)-v_\varepsilon (x)=\int _0^ h\partial _ av_\varepsilon (x+te_ a)\, \mathrm d t \]

shows that $\tilde v$ has the continuous partial derivatives $\partial _ a\tilde v=\tilde w_ a$. Hence $\tilde v$ is continuously differentiable, and by the first part its first derivatives are Hölder continuous with exponent $\alpha $ on each $\overline{B'}$. Every compact subset of $U$ is covered by finitely many such balls $B'$; pairs of points closer than a Lebesgue number of this cover lie in a common ball, and for the remaining pairs the supremum bound suffices. Hence $\tilde v\in C^{1,\alpha }_{\mathrm{loc}}(U)$. $\square$

6.3 Unitarily invariant measures

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

The incidence argument outlined at the beginning of the chapter averages over complex directions and over complex hyperplanes, so we need invariant probability measures on complex projective space and on the Grassmannian of hyperplanes. We construct them from the Gaussian measure on matrices, compute the measure of small balls in projective space, and introduce orthonormal frames on hyperplanes that depend on the hyperplane in a Borel way.

We identify $\mathbb C^ n$ with $\mathbb R^{2n}$ and write $\langle z,w\rangle =\sum _ jz_ j\overline{w_ j}$ for the Hermitian inner product, so that the Euclidean inner product is $\operatorname{Re}\langle z,w\rangle $. The unitary group $\mathrm U(n)$ consists of the complex $n\times n$ matrices $U$ with $U^*U=1$, where $U^*$ is the conjugate transpose; it is compact, being closed and bounded in the space of matrices.

Proposition 6.3.1. There is a Borel probability measure $\eta $ on $\mathrm U(n)$ that is invariant under left and right multiplication by elements of $\mathrm U(n)$. Let $Y$ be a nonempty metric space on which $\mathrm U(n)$ acts continuously and transitively. Then $Y$ is compact, and it carries a unique $\mathrm U(n)$-invariant Borel probability measure; for every $y_0\in Y$, this measure is the image of $\eta $ under the map $g\mapsto gy_0$. In particular, the unit sphere of $\mathbb C^ n$ and the complex Grassmannians described below carry unique unitarily invariant probability measures.

Proof. Identify the space of complex $n\times n$ matrices with $\mathbb R^{2n^2}$ through the real and imaginary parts of the entries, and let $\gamma $ be the measure on it with density $\mathrm e^{-\pi \operatorname {tr}(X^*X)}$ with respect to Lebesgue measure. Since $\operatorname {tr}(X^*X)=\sum _{j,k}|X_{jk}|^2$, this density is the Gaussian $\mathrm e^{-\pi |x|^2}$ on $\mathbb R^{2n^2}$, so $\gamma $ is a probability measure by lemma 6.1.1 (with $\xi =0$ and $t=1$). The singular matrices form a $\gamma $-null set: they are the zero set of the real polynomial $|\det X|^2$, which is not identically zero since $\det 1=1$, and the zero set of such a polynomial is Lebesgue null.

To prove the last fact, let $P$ be a real polynomial in $m\geq 1$ variables that is not identically zero; we argue by induction on $m$. If $m=1$, the zero set is finite. If $m\geq 2$, write $P=\sum _ kc_ k(x')x_ m^ k$ as a polynomial in the last variable, with coefficients $c_ k$ that are polynomials in $x'=(x_1,\ldots ,x_{m-1})$. Some $c_ k$ is not identically zero, so by induction the common zero set $Z'$ of the coefficients is null in $\mathbb R^{m-1}$. For $x'\notin Z'$, the polynomial $P(x',\cdot )$ is not identically zero and has finitely many zeros. By Tonelli's theorem applied to the indicator function of the closed set $\{ P=0\} $, this set is null.

For invertible $X$ define

\[ Q(X)=X(X^*X)^{-1/2}, \]

where $(X^*X)^{1/2}$ is the positive square root of the positive definite matrix $X^*X$. By the spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices, this square root exists and is unique. It depends continuously on the matrix. Indeed, let $A_ k\to A$ be positive definite matrices. The square roots $A_ k^{1/2}$ are bounded, since $\operatorname {tr}(A_ k^{1/2}A_ k^{1/2})=\operatorname {tr}A_ k$. Every convergent subsequence of them has a positive semidefinite limit $S$ with $S^2=A$; then $S$ is invertible, hence positive definite, and $S=A^{1/2}$ by uniqueness. A bounded sequence of matrices all of whose convergent subsequences have the same limit converges to it, so $A_ k^{1/2}\to A^{1/2}$. Since inversion is continuous on invertible matrices, $Q$ is continuous on the open set of invertible matrices, and $Q(X)^*Q(X)=(X^*X)^{-1/2}X^*X(X^*X)^{-1/2}=1$. Let $\eta $ be the image under $Q$ of the restriction of $\gamma $ to the invertible matrices; it is a Borel probability measure on $\mathrm U(n)$.

Fix $U,V\in \mathrm U(n)$. The map $X\mapsto UXV$ is real linear and preserves $\operatorname {tr}(X^*X)$, so it is orthogonal on $\mathbb R^{2n^2}$ and preserves $\gamma $. By uniqueness of positive square roots, $(V^*X^*XV)^{1/2}=V^*(X^*X)^{1/2}V$, and therefore $Q(UXV)=UQ(X)V$. Consequently the image of $\eta $ under $g\mapsto UgV$ is the image of $\gamma $ under $X\mapsto Q(UXV)$, which is $\eta $ because $X\mapsto UXV$ preserves $\gamma $. This proves the invariance of $\eta $ on both sides.

Now let $Y$ be as in the statement and fix $y_0\in Y$. By transitivity, $Y$ is the image of the compact space $\mathrm U(n)$ under the continuous map $g\mapsto gy_0$, so $Y$ is compact, and hence separable. Let $\sigma $ be the image of $\eta $ under $g\mapsto gy_0$. For $h\in \mathrm U(n)$, the image of $\sigma $ under $h$ is the image of $\eta $ under $g\mapsto hgy_0$, which is $\sigma $ by left invariance; thus $\sigma $ is invariant. Let $f$ be continuous on $Y$. Every $y\in Y$ has the form $hy_0$, by transitivity, and right invariance gives

\[ \int f(gy)\, \mathrm d\eta (g)=\int f(ghy_0)\, \mathrm d\eta (g)=\int f\, \mathrm d\sigma . \]

Let $\tau $ be any invariant Borel probability measure on $Y$. We integrate the last identity against $\tau $ and apply Fubini's theorem to the bounded continuous function $(g,y)\mapsto f(gy)$ on $\mathrm U(n)\times Y$, which is product-measurable by lemma 1.1.8 because $\mathrm U(n)$ and $Y$ are separable metric spaces. We obtain

\[ \int f\, \mathrm d\sigma =\int _{\mathrm U(n)}\int _ Yf(gy)\, \mathrm d\tau (y)\, \mathrm d\eta (g)=\int f\, \mathrm d\tau . \]

Thus $\sigma $ and $\tau $ agree on continuous functions. For a nonempty closed set $F\subset Y$, the continuous functions $\max \{ 0,1-j\operatorname {dist}(y,F)\} $ decrease to $\mathbf1_ F$, so dominated convergence gives $\sigma (F)=\tau (F)$. The closed sets are stable under finite intersections and generate the Borel $\sigma $-algebra, so lemma 1.1.6(2) gives $\sigma =\tau $. In particular $\sigma $ does not depend on $y_0$.

Finally, $\mathrm U(n)$ acts continuously on the unit sphere of $\mathbb C^ n$, and transitively: every unit vector is the first column of a unitary matrix, obtained by completing it to an orthonormal basis with the Gram–Schmidt process. For the Grassmannians, these properties are verified below, where they are introduced. $\square$

For an integer $0\leq k\leq n$, we realize the complex Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb C}(k,n)$ of $k$-dimensional complex subspaces of $\mathbb C^ n$ as the set of Hermitian matrices $P$ with $P^2=P$ and $\operatorname {tr}P=k$. By the spectral theorem, such a matrix has the eigenvalues $0$ and $1$ only, so it is the orthogonal projection onto its image, which has dimension $\operatorname {tr}P=k$; conversely, the orthogonal projection onto a $k$-dimensional subspace is such a matrix. The set $\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb C}(k,n)$ is closed and bounded, since $\operatorname {tr}(P^*P)=\operatorname {tr}P=k$, and hence compact. The unitary group acts continuously by $P\mapsto UPU^*$, which replaces the image of $P$ by its image under $U$. The action is transitive: given two $k$-dimensional subspaces, choose orthonormal bases of $\mathbb C^ n$ whose first $k$ vectors span them; the unitary matrix carrying one basis to the other maps one subspace onto the other.

Complex projective space $\mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ is the case $k=1$. For $\xi \in \mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ we write $P_\xi $ for the corresponding rank-one projection, and we denote the invariant probability measure on $\mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ by $\sigma $. The Hopf map $q\colon S^{2n-1}\to \mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ sends a unit vector $e$ to the complex line it spans, that is, to the projection $ee^*$.

For $\xi ,\xi '\in \mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ with unit representatives $e,e'$, set

\[ d(\xi ,\xi ')=\arccos |\langle e,e'\rangle |\in [0,\pi /2]; \]

this does not depend on the choice of representatives. We claim that $d$ is a metric inducing the topology of $\mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$. Let $\delta (e,e')=\arccos \operatorname{Re}\langle e,e'\rangle \in [0,\pi ]$ be the angle between two unit vectors, that is, their great-circle distance on $S^{2n-1}$. Since the maximum of $\operatorname{Re}\langle \mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }e,e'\rangle $ over $\theta $ is $|\langle e,e'\rangle |$ and $\arccos $ is decreasing, the number $d(\xi ,\xi ')$ is the minimum of $\delta $ over the unit representatives of $\xi $ and $\xi '$.

The angle $\delta $ satisfies the triangle inequality. Indeed, let $e,e',e''$ be unit vectors, put $a=\delta (e,e')$ and $b=\delta (e',e'')$, and write $e=\cos a\, e'+\sin a\, u$ and $e''=\cos b\, e'+\sin b\, w$ with unit vectors $u,w$ orthogonal to $e'$ for the Euclidean inner product. Then $\operatorname{Re}\langle e,e''\rangle =\cos a\cos b+\sin a\sin b\operatorname{Re}\langle u,w\rangle \geq \cos (a+b)$ by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Hence $\delta (e,e'')\leq a+b$ when $a+b\leq \pi $, and this inequality is trivial when $a+b>\pi $. To deduce the triangle inequality for $d$, fix a unit representative $e'$ of $\xi '$, and choose the representatives $e$ of $\xi $ and $e''$ of $\xi ''$ such that $\delta (e,e')=d(\xi ,\xi ')$ and $\delta (e',e'')=d(\xi ',\xi '')$. Then

\[ d(\xi ,\xi '')\leq \delta (e,e'')\leq d(\xi ,\xi ')+d(\xi ',\xi ''). \]

The function $d$ is symmetric, and $d(\xi ,\xi ')=0$ forces equality in the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\langle e,e'\rangle |\leq 1$, hence $\xi =\xi '$. Finally, $\cos ^2d(\xi ,\xi ')=|\langle e,e'\rangle |^2=\operatorname {tr}(P_\xi P_{\xi '})$ depends continuously on the two projections. Therefore the identity map from $\mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$, with its compact matrix topology, to the metric space $(\mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C),d)$ is a continuous bijection onto a Hausdorff space, hence a homeomorphism. Since $q$ is a continuous surjection from a compact space onto a Hausdorff space, this topology is also the quotient topology of the Hopf map.

Lemma 6.3.2. Let $n\geq 1$, $0<a\leq \pi /2$ and $\xi _0\in \mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$. Then

\[ \sigma \{ \xi :d(\xi ,\xi _0)<a\} =(\sin a)^{2n-2}. \]

In particular, this ball has $\sigma $-measure at most $a^{2n-2}$.

Proof. If $n=1$, then $\mathbb P^0(\mathbb C)$ is a single point, and both sides equal $1$; so let $n\geq 2$. Let $\gamma $ be the probability measure on $\mathbb C^ n=\mathbb R^{2n}$ with density $\mathrm e^{-\pi |z|^2}$. Unitary maps are orthogonal on $\mathbb R^{2n}$, so they preserve Lebesgue measure and $|z|$; hence $\gamma $ is unitarily invariant. Moreover $\gamma (\{ 0\} )=0$. The map $z\mapsto \mathbb Cz$ from $\mathbb C^ n\setminus \{ 0\} $ to $\mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$, that is, $z\mapsto zz^*/|z|^2$, is continuous and commutes with the action of $\mathrm U(n)$. Hence the image of $\gamma $ under this map is a unitarily invariant Borel probability measure, and by the uniqueness in proposition 6.3.1 it is $\sigma $.

The metric $d$ is unitarily invariant, because unitary matrices preserve $\langle \cdot ,\cdot \rangle $. Since $\sigma $ is invariant and the unitary group acts transitively, we may therefore take $\xi _0=\mathbb Ce_1$, the first coordinate line. Write $z=(z_1,z')$ with $z'\in \mathbb C^{n-1}$. For the line $\xi =\mathbb Cz$, the condition $d(\xi ,\xi _0)<a$ means $|z_1|>\cos a\, |z|$, because $\arccos $ is strictly decreasing; that is, $|z_1|^2\sin ^2a>\cos ^2a\, |z'|^2$, or $|z_1|^2>\cot ^2a\, |z'|^2$, where $\cot (\pi /2)=0$. Thus the preimage of the ball under $z\mapsto \mathbb Cz$ is the set of $z\neq 0$ with $|z_1|^2>\cot ^2a\, |z'|^2$. We compute its $\gamma $-measure by Fubini's theorem, integrating first in $z_1$. For $c\geq 0$, polar coordinates in $\mathbb C$ give

\[ \int _{\{ w\in \mathbb C:|w|^2>c\} }\mathrm e^{-\pi |w|^2}\, \mathrm d w=2\pi \int _{\sqrt c}^\infty r\mathrm e^{-\pi r^2}\, \mathrm d r=\mathrm e^{-\pi c}, \]

and we apply this with $c=\cot ^2a\, |z'|^2$. Using lemma 6.1.1 in $\mathbb C^{n-1}=\mathbb R^{2n-2}$ with $\xi =0$ and $t=1+\cot ^2a$, we obtain

\[ \sigma \{ \xi :d(\xi ,\xi _0)<a\} =\int _{\mathbb C^{n-1}}\mathrm e^{-\pi (1+\cot ^2a)|z'|^2}\, \mathrm d z'=(1+\cot ^2a)^{-(n-1)}=(\sin a)^{2n-2}. \]

The last assertion follows from $\sin a\leq a$. $\square$

We write $\mathcal H=\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb C}(n-1,n)$ for the space of complex hyperplanes through the origin in $\mathbb C^ n$, and $\nu $ for its invariant probability measure. For $H\in \mathcal H$, the orthogonal projection $P_ H$ onto $H$ is the matrix representing $H$, and the orthogonal complement $H^\perp $ is a complex line. Let $m_ H$ be the Lebesgue measure of $H$, that is, the image of Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb C^{n-1}=\mathbb R^{2n-2}$ under any complex linear isometry $\mathbb C^{n-1}\to H$.

To integrate functions that depend on $H$, we use orthonormal frames of $H$ that depend on $H$ in a Borel way. Let $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$ be the standard basis of $\mathbb C^ n$, and order the $(n-1)$-element subsets $I$ of $\{ 1,\ldots ,n\} $. For each $I$, let $W_ I$ be the set of hyperplanes $H$ for which the vectors $P_ He_ i$, $i\in I$, are linearly independent. Since $\langle P_ He_ i,P_ He_ j\rangle =\langle P_ He_ i,e_ j\rangle $, the Gram determinant of these vectors is the principal minor of $P_ H$ with indices in $I$, so $W_ I$ is open. These sets cover $\mathcal H$, because the $n$ vectors $P_ He_1,\ldots ,P_ He_ n$ span the $(n-1)$-dimensional space $H$, so that $n-1$ of them form a basis. Let $W_ I'$ be the Borel set of hyperplanes $H$ for which $I$ is the first index with $H\in W_ I$; these finitely many sets partition $\mathcal H$. For $H\in W_ I'$, the Gram–Schmidt process applied to $(P_ He_ i)_{i\in I}$ gives an orthonormal basis of $H$, and hence a complex linear isometry $T_ H\colon \mathbb C^{n-1}\to H$.

The Gram–Schmidt process depends continuously on linearly independent families, so the map $(H,w)\mapsto T_ Hw$ is continuous on each set $W_ I'\times \mathbb C^{n-1}$. Hence the preimage of an open subset of $\mathbb C^ n$ under this map is a finite union of relatively open subsets of these Borel sets, and $(H,w)\mapsto T_ Hw$ is a Borel map from $\mathcal H\times \mathbb C^{n-1}$ to $\mathbb C^ n$. A map into a product of two separable metric spaces whose components are Borel is Borel, because the preimage of a product of Borel sets is Borel and these products generate the Borel sets of the product (lemma 1.1.8). In particular, $(H,w)\mapsto (H,T_ Hw)$ is a Borel map into $\mathcal H\times \mathbb C^ n$. Consequently, for every nonnegative Borel function $f$ on the closed set $\{ (H,c)\in \mathcal H\times \mathbb C^ n:c\in H\} $, the function

\[ H\longmapsto \int _ Hf(H,c)\, \mathrm d m_ H(c)=\int _{\mathbb C^{n-1}}f(H,T_ Hw)\, \mathrm d w \]

is Borel, by Tonelli's theorem (proposition 1.1.7), applied with respect to $\nu $ and Lebesgue measure to the Borel function $(H,w)\mapsto f(H,T_ Hw)$, which is product-measurable by lemma 1.1.8, since $\mathcal H$ and $\mathbb C^{n-1}$ are separable metric spaces. The value of the integral does not depend on the frame, because a change of orthonormal frame preserves Lebesgue measure.

6.4 Circle selection and high-value sets

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

An affine complex line in $\mathbb C^ n$ is a set $L=a+\mathbb Cb$ with $a\in \mathbb C^ n$ and $b\in \mathbb C^ n\setminus \{ 0\} $. For an open set $D\subset \mathbb C^ n$, we identify the section $D\cap L$ with the open set $\{ \zeta \in \mathbb C:a+\zeta b\in D\} $, and we call a function on a connected component of $D\cap L$ subharmonic if its composite with $\zeta \mapsto a+\zeta b$ is subharmonic in the sense of definition 3.1.1.

In the plane, the spherical means of (3.1) are averages with respect to arc length: the surface measure $S$ on the unit circle (section 2.1) is the image of Lebesgue measure on $[0,2\pi )$ under $\theta \mapsto \mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }$. Indeed, by the change of variables formula for $(t,\theta )\mapsto t\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }$, whose Jacobian determinant is $t$, the cone over an arc $\{ \mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }:\theta _1\leq \theta <\theta _2\} $ with $0\leq \theta _1\leq \theta _2\leq 2\pi $ has area $(\theta _2-\theta _1)/2$, so the arc has $S$-measure $\theta _2-\theta _1$. These arcs are closed under finite intersections, and every open subset of the circle is a countable union of them, so they generate the Borel sets of the circle. Since the whole circle is such an arc, lemma 1.1.6(2) identifies the two finite measures. Hence

\[ M_ w(\zeta ,r)=\frac1{2\pi }\int _0^{2\pi }w(\zeta +r\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta })\, \mathrm d\theta \]

for an upper semicontinuous function $w$ on an open subset of $\mathbb C$ containing $\overline{B(\zeta ,r)}$.

Consequently, subharmonicity on $D\cap L$ does not depend on the parametrization. Two parametrizations differ by a map $\psi (\zeta )=\alpha \zeta +\beta $ with $\alpha \neq 0$, which is a homeomorphism and maps the circle of radius $r$ about $\zeta $ onto the circle of radius $|\alpha |r$ about $\psi (\zeta )$. Since $\psi (\zeta +r\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta })=\psi (\zeta )+|\alpha |r\mathrm e^{\mathrm i(\theta +\arg \alpha )}$ and Lebesgue measure on the circle is invariant under rotations, the arc-length formula gives $M_{w\circ \psi }(\zeta ,r)=M_ w(\psi (\zeta ),|\alpha |r)$.

In the proof of theorem 6.4.2 we must choose, measurably in the circle, a point of each circle at which a given function is large. The sets of admissible points will be closed and have positive length, and for such sets the arc-length median provides an explicit Borel choice.

Lemma 6.4.1. Let $T$ be a separable metric space, and let $F\subset T\times [0,2\pi ]$ be a Borel set whose sections $F_ t=\{ \theta :(t,\theta )\in F\} $ are closed and have positive Lebesgue measure for every $t\in T$. Then there is a Borel map $a\colon T\to [0,2\pi ]$ such that $a(t)\in F_ t$ for every $t\in T$.

Proof. Write $|\cdot |$ for Lebesgue measure on $[0,2\pi ]$, and put $\ell (t)=|F_ t|$ and $A_ t(b)=|F_ t\cap [0,b]|$ for $b\in [0,2\pi ]$. We first show that, for fixed $b$, the function $t\mapsto A_ t(b)$ is Borel; with $b=2\pi $, so is $\ell $. Since $T$ and $[0,2\pi ]$ are separable metric spaces, the Borel sets of $T\times [0,2\pi ]$ form the $\sigma $-algebra generated by the products of Borel sets (lemma 1.1.8). Hence the sections $G_ t$ of a Borel set $G$ are Borel, because the sets all of whose sections are Borel form a $\sigma $-algebra containing these products. The class of Borel sets $G\subset T\times [0,2\pi ]$ for which $t\mapsto |G_ t\cap [0,b]|$ is Borel contains the products $B_1\times B_2$ of Borel sets, for which this function is $\mathbf1_{B_1}(t)|B_2\cap [0,b]|$. It is closed under complements, since the complement of $G$ gives $b-|G_ t\cap [0,b]|$, and under countable disjoint unions, which give sums. Since the products of Borel sets are closed under finite intersections, lemma 1.1.6(1) shows that the class contains every Borel set.

For fixed $t$, the function $A_ t$ is nondecreasing and $1$-Lipschitz, with $A_ t(0)=0$ and $A_ t(2\pi )=\ell (t)>0$. Define

\[ a(t)=\inf \{ b\in \mathbb Q\cap [0,2\pi ]:A_ t(b)\geq \ell (t)/2\} . \]

Because $A_ t$ is continuous and nondecreasing and $0<\ell (t)/2<\ell (t)$, the set of all $b\in [0,2\pi ]$ with $A_ t(b)\geq \ell (t)/2$ is an interval $[b_ t,2\pi ]$ with $0<b_ t<2\pi $ and $A_ t(b_ t)=\ell (t)/2$. The rational numbers are dense in this interval, so $a(t)=b_ t$ and $A_ t(a(t))=\ell (t)/2$. The map $a$ is Borel, since for every $c\in (0,2\pi ]$

\[ \{ t:a(t)<c\} =\bigcup _{b\in \mathbb Q\cap [0,c)}\{ t:A_ t(b)\geq \ell (t)/2\} . \]

Finally, suppose that $a(t)\notin F_ t$. Since $F_ t$ is closed and $a(t)>0$, some interval $[a(t)-\varepsilon ,a(t)]$ with $\varepsilon >0$ misses $F_ t$, and $A_ t$ is constant on it. A rational number $b$ in this interval with $b<a(t)$ then satisfies $A_ t(b)=\ell (t)/2$, contradicting the definition of $a(t)$. Hence $a(t)\in F_ t$. $\square$

Theorem 6.4.2. Let $D\subset \mathbb C^ n$ be open, with $n\geq 2$, and let $x\in D$ and $R>0$ satisfy $\overline{B(x,R)}\subset D$. Let $u\colon D\to [-\infty ,\infty )$ be a Borel function such that, for every complex line $L$ through $x$, the restriction of $u$ to the connected component of $D\cap L$ containing $x$ is subharmonic, and put $A=u(x)$. Then there is a Borel probability measure $\mu $ carried by the Borel set

\[ E=\{ y\in \mathbb C^ n:R/2\leq |y-x|\leq R,\ u(y)\geq A\} \]

such that $I_{2n-2}(\mu )\leq C_ nR^{-(2n-2)}$, where the constant $C_ n$ depends only on $n$.

Proof. We obtain $\mu $ by choosing, in a Borel way, a point of $E$ on each circle of radius $r\in [R/2,R]$ centered at $x$ in a complex line through $x$, and then we estimate its energy. For $\xi \in \mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ we first choose a unit vector $e_0(\xi )\in \xi $ in a Borel way: if $j$ is the first index with $(P_\xi )_{jj}>0$, put $e_0(\xi )=P_\xi e_ j/\sqrt{(P_\xi )_{jj}}$. Since $|P_\xi e_ j|^2=\langle P_\xi e_ j,e_ j\rangle =(P_\xi )_{jj}$, this is a unit vector in $\xi $. The set of $\xi $ with a given first index $j$ is Borel, and $e_0$ is continuous on it.

Let $r\in [R/2,R]$ and $\xi \in \mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$. The circle $\theta \mapsto x+r\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }e_0(\xi )$ lies in the closed disc $\{ x+\zeta e_0(\xi ):|\zeta |\leq r\} $, which is connected, contains $x$ and is contained in $D$; hence it lies in the connected component containing $x$ of the section of $D$ by the line $x+\xi $. On this component $u$ is subharmonic; in particular it is upper semicontinuous and bounded above on the circle, and by theorem 3.1.3(2) and the arc-length formula for circle means at the beginning of this section, it satisfies the submean inequality

\[ A=u(x)\leq \frac1{2\pi }\int _0^{2\pi }u(x+r\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }e_0(\xi ))\, \mathrm d\theta . \]

Let

\[ F_{r,\xi }=\{ \theta \in [0,2\pi ]:u(x+r\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }e_0(\xi ))\geq A\} . \]

This set is closed by upper semicontinuity, and it has positive length. If $A=-\infty $, it is the whole interval $[0,2\pi ]$. If $A$ is finite and $F_{r,\xi }$ were a null set, then $A-u$ would be positive almost everywhere on the circle, so the circle mean of $u$ would be strictly less than $A$, contradicting the submean inequality.

The set of $(r,\xi ,\theta )$ with $\theta \in F_{r,\xi }$ is Borel, because $u$ is Borel and the map $(r,\xi ,\theta )\mapsto x+r\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }e_0(\xi )$ into $\overline{B(x,R)}$ is Borel, being continuous on each of the finitely many Borel sets on which $e_0$ is continuous. Since $[R/2,R]\times \mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ is a compact, hence separable, metric space, lemma 6.4.1 gives a Borel map $(r,\xi )\mapsto \alpha (r,\xi )$ with $\alpha (r,\xi )\in F_{r,\xi }$. Put $e(r,\xi )=\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\alpha (r,\xi )}e_0(\xi )$ and $\Psi (r,\xi )=x+re(r,\xi )$. Then $\Psi $ is Borel, hence product-measurable (lemma 1.1.8), and it takes its values in $E$. Let $\mu $ be the image under $\Psi $ of the product of normalized Lebesgue measure on $[R/2,R]$ and $\sigma $. It is a Borel probability measure carried by $E$.

It remains to estimate the energy; the estimate does not depend on the choice of the phases $\alpha (r,\xi )$. For $r,r'\in [R/2,R]$ and unit vectors $e,e'$ spanning $\xi ,\xi '$,

\[ |re-r'e'|^2=(r-r')^2+rr'|e-e'|^2 \geq (r-r')^2+\frac{R^2}{4}\bigl (2-2|\langle e,e'\rangle |\bigr ), \]

where the identity follows by expanding both sides with $|e|=|e'|=1$ and $|e-e'|^2=2-2\operatorname{Re}\langle e,e'\rangle $, and the inequality holds since $rr'\geq R^2/4$ and $\operatorname{Re}\langle e,e'\rangle \leq |\langle e,e'\rangle |$. With $a=d(\xi ,\xi ')\in [0,\pi /2]$, we have $2-2|\langle e,e'\rangle |=4\sin ^2(a/2)\geq 4a^2/\pi ^2$, because $\sin y\geq 2y/\pi $ for $0\leq y\leq \pi /2$ by concavity of the sine on this interval. Hence $|re-r'e'|^2\geq (r-r')^2+R^2a^2/\pi ^2$.

Write $r=Rt$ with $t\in [1/2,1]$. Equip $[1/2,1]\times \mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ with the metric $\rho $ defined by $\rho ((t,\xi ),(t',\xi '))^2=(t-t')^2+d(\xi ,\xi ')^2$, and with the product probability $m$ of normalized Lebesgue measure and $\sigma $, which is defined on all Borel sets of this separable metric space by lemma 1.1.8. Then $\mu $ is the image of $m$ under $(t,\xi )\mapsto \Psi (Rt,\xi )$, and the lower bound for $|re-r'e'|^2$, together with $\pi >1$, gives

\[ |\Psi (Rt,\xi )-\Psi (Rt',\xi ')|\geq \frac R\pi \, \rho ((t,\xi ),(t',\xi ')). \]

A $\rho $-ball of radius $s\leq 1$ is contained in the product of an interval of length $2s$, whose normalized measure is at most $4s$, and a ball of radius $s$ for the metric $d$. Since $s\leq 1<\pi /2$, lemma 6.3.2 shows that its $m$-measure is at most $4s^{2n-1}$. In particular $m$ has no atoms, so the diagonal, a closed and hence product-measurable set (lemma 1.1.8), is null for $m\times m$ by Tonelli's theorem. Fix $z\in [1/2,1]\times \mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$. Splitting the integral into the set where $\rho (z,z')\geq 1$ and the shells where $2^{-k-1}\leq \rho (z,z')<2^{-k}$, $k\geq 0$, we obtain

\[ \int \rho (z,z')^{-(2n-2)}\, \mathrm d m(z') \leq 1+\sum _{k=0}^\infty 2^{(k+1)(2n-2)}\cdot 4\cdot 2^{-k(2n-1)}=1+2^{2n+1}. \]

Since $\mu $ is the image of $m$, the product $\mu \times \mu $ is the image of $m\times m$ under the product map, because both measures agree on rectangles (proposition 1.1.7). Therefore the lower bound for $|\Psi (Rt,\xi )-\Psi (Rt',\xi ')|$, valid off the $m\times m$-null diagonal, and an integration in $z$ give

\[ I_{2n-2}(\mu )\leq \Bigl (\frac\pi R\Bigr )^{2n-2}\iint \rho (z,z')^{-(2n-2)}\, \mathrm d m(z')\, \mathrm d m(z)\leq \pi ^{2n-2}(1+2^{2n+1})R^{-(2n-2)}. \]

This is the assertion, with $C_ n=\pi ^{2n-2}(1+2^{2n+1})$. $\square$

6.5 Projection densities and incidence

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

We now show that a measure of finite Riesz $(2n-2)$-energy on $\mathbb C^ n$, $n\geq 2$, projects onto almost every complex hyperplane with a square-integrable density, and we deduce the incidence theorem. The first step expresses averages over hyperplanes as a weighted integral over $\mathbb C^ n$.

Lemma 6.5.1. Let $n\geq 2$. For every nonnegative Borel function $f$ on $\mathbb C^ n$,

\[ \int _{\mathcal H}\int _ H f(\xi )\, \mathrm d m_ H(\xi )\, \mathrm d\nu (H) =a_ n\int _{\mathbb C^ n}f(\xi )|\xi |^{-2}\, \mathrm d\xi , \qquad a_ n=\frac{s_{2n-3}}{s_{2n-1}}. \]

Proof. We write the inner integral in polar coordinates on $H$ and show that the normalized surface measures of the unit spheres of the hyperplanes $H$ average to the normalized surface measure of $S^{2n-1}$. For $H\in \mathcal H$, let $S_ H$ be the unit sphere of $H$, and let $\tau _ H$ be the image under $T_ H$ of the normalized surface measure $\mathrm dS/s_{2n-3}$ of the unit sphere $S^{2n-3}$ of $\mathbb C^{n-1}=\mathbb R^{2n-2}$. We regard $\tau _ H$ as a Borel probability measure on $S^{2n-1}$ carried by $S_ H$; it does not depend on the frame, since surface measure is invariant under orthogonal transformations (section 2.1). Polar coordinates (2.1) on all of $\mathbb C^{n-1}$, transported to $H$ by $T_ H$, give

\[ \int _ Hf\, \mathrm d m_ H=s_{2n-3}\int _0^\infty r^{2n-3}\int f(r\theta )\, \mathrm d\tau _ H(\theta )\, \mathrm d r. \]

Measurability. Let $h$ be a nonnegative Borel function on $(0,\infty )\times S^{2n-1}$. By the definition of $\tau _ H$,

6.12
\begin{equation} \int h(r,\theta )\, \mathrm d\tau _ H(\theta )=\frac1{s_{2n-3}}\int _{S^{2n-3}}h(r,T_ H\omega )\, \mathrm dS(\omega ). \end{equation}

The map $(H,r,\omega )\mapsto (r,T_ H\omega )$ is Borel by the frame construction of section 6.3, since its two components are Borel. Hence $(H,r,\omega )\mapsto h(r,T_ H\omega )$ is a nonnegative Borel function on the product of the separable metric spaces $\mathcal H\times (0,\infty )$ and $S^{2n-3}$, and by lemma 1.1.8 and Tonelli's theorem the right side of (6.12) is a Borel function of $(H,r)$. In particular, taking $h(r,\theta )=g(\theta )$, we see that $H\mapsto \int g\, \mathrm d\tau _ H$ is Borel for every nonnegative Borel function $g$ on $S^{2n-1}$; for instance, $H\mapsto \tau _ H(B)$ is Borel for every Borel set $B\subset S^{2n-1}$.

Averaging. For a Borel set $B\subset S^{2n-1}$ put $\tau (B)=\int \tau _ H(B)\, \mathrm d\nu (H)$. By monotone convergence, $\tau $ is countably additive, so it is a Borel probability measure on $S^{2n-1}$. For a unitary matrix $U$, the map $UT_ H$ is a complex linear isometry from $\mathbb C^{n-1}$ onto $UH$, so $U$ maps $\tau _ H$ to $\tau _{UH}$, that is, $\tau _ H(U^{-1}B)=\tau _{UH}(B)$. Since $\nu $ is invariant, it follows that $\tau (U^{-1}B)=\int \tau _{UH}(B)\, \mathrm d\nu (H)=\tau (B)$, so $\tau $ is unitarily invariant. The normalized surface measure $\mathrm dS/s_{2n-1}$ of $S^{2n-1}$ is also unitarily invariant, because unitary matrices are orthogonal transformations of $\mathbb R^{2n}$. By the uniqueness in proposition 6.3.1, $\tau =\mathrm dS/s_{2n-1}$. By the definition of $\tau $ for indicator functions, by linearity for simple functions, and by monotone convergence in general, $\int _{\mathcal H}\int g\, \mathrm d\tau _ H\, \mathrm d\nu (H)=\int g\, \mathrm d\tau $ for every nonnegative Borel function $g$ on $S^{2n-1}$.

Conclusion. Integrate the polar-coordinate identity over $H$ with respect to $\nu $. The function $(r,\theta )\mapsto f(r\theta )$ is Borel on $(0,\infty )\times S^{2n-1}$, so by the measurability step $(H,r)\mapsto r^{2n-3}\int f(r\theta )\, \mathrm d\tau _ H(\theta )$ is a nonnegative Borel function on $\mathcal H\times (0,\infty )$, which is product-measurable by lemma 1.1.8. Tonelli's theorem therefore allows us to integrate first over $H$, and the averaging identity with $g(\theta )=f(r\theta )$ shows that the left side of the lemma equals

\[ s_{2n-3}\int _0^\infty r^{2n-3}\int _{S^{2n-1}}f(r\theta )\, \frac{\mathrm d S(\theta )}{s_{2n-1}}\, \mathrm d r. \]

In polar coordinates $\xi =r\theta $ on all of $\mathbb C^ n=\mathbb R^{2n}$, (2.1) gives $|\xi |^{-2}\, \mathrm d\xi =r^{2n-3}\, \mathrm d r\, \mathrm dS(\theta )$, so this is the right side. All integrals may be infinite. $\square$

Theorem 6.5.2. Let $n\geq 2$, and let $\mu $ be a finite positive Borel measure on $\mathbb C^ n$ with $I_{2n-2}(\mu )<\infty $. Then for $\nu $-almost every $H\in \mathcal H$, the projected measure $(P_ H)_*\mu $ has a density $g_ H\in L^2(H,m_ H)$ with respect to $m_ H$, and

\[ \int _{\mathcal H}\| g_ H\| _2^2\, \mathrm d\nu (H)=\beta _ n I_{2n-2}(\mu ), \qquad \beta _ n=\frac{a_ n}{b_{2n,2n-2}}=\frac{\Gamma (n)}{\pi ^{n-1}}>0. \]

Proof. On $H$ we use the Fourier transform defined by the Euclidean inner product $\operatorname{Re}\langle \cdot ,\cdot \rangle $ and the measure $m_ H$; in an orthonormal frame it is the transform of section 6.1 on $\mathbb R^{2n-2}$. For $y\in \mathbb C^ n$ and $\xi \in H$ we have $\operatorname{Re}\langle P_ Hy,\xi \rangle =\operatorname{Re}\langle y,\xi \rangle $, because $y-P_ Hy$ is orthogonal to $H$. Hence the Fourier transform of $(P_ H)_*\mu $ at $\xi \in H$ is $\int \mathrm e^{-2\pi \mathrm i\operatorname{Re}\langle P_ Hy,\xi \rangle }\, \mathrm d\mu (y)=\widehat\mu (\xi )$; that is, it is the restriction of $\widehat\mu $ to $H$. Apply lemma 6.5.1 to the continuous function $|\widehat\mu |^2$, and then theorem 6.1.4 with $d=2n$ and $s=2n-2$, for which $|\xi |^{s-d}=|\xi |^{-2}$. We obtain

\[ \int _{\mathcal H}\int _ H|\widehat\mu (\xi )|^2\, \mathrm d m_ H(\xi )\, \mathrm d\nu (H) =a_ n\int _{\mathbb C^ n}|\widehat\mu (\xi )|^2|\xi |^{-2}\, \mathrm d\xi =\beta _ n I_{2n-2}(\mu )<\infty . \]

The inner integral is a Borel function of $H$, by the frame construction in section 6.3; hence it is finite for $\nu $-almost every $H$. For each such $H$, we apply proposition 6.1.3 on $\mathbb C^{n-1}=\mathbb R^{2n-2}$ to the image of $\mu $ under $T_ H^{-1}P_ H$. Since $T_ H$ preserves $\operatorname{Re}\langle \cdot ,\cdot \rangle $, the Fourier transform of this image is $w\mapsto \widehat\mu (T_ Hw)$, whose squared $L^2$ norm is $\int _ H|\widehat\mu |^2\, \mathrm d m_ H<\infty $. Hence the image has a density in $L^1\cap L^2$, and composing it with $T_ H^{-1}$ we obtain a density $g_ H$ of $(P_ H)_*\mu $ with respect to $m_ H$, with $\| g_ H\| _2^2=\int _ H|\widehat\mu |^2\, \mathrm d m_ H$. In particular, $H\mapsto \| g_ H\| _2^2$ is Borel, being equal to this Fourier integral, so no measurable choice of the densities themselves is needed. Integrating over $H$ proves the identity.

To compute $\beta _ n$, recall from (6.2) that $s_{k-1}=2\pi ^{k/2}/\Gamma (k/2)$ for every $k\geq 1$. Hence $a_ n=\Gamma (n)/(\pi \Gamma (n-1))$, while $b_{2n,2n-2}=\pi ^{n-2}/\Gamma (n-1)$, and therefore $\beta _ n=\Gamma (n)/\pi ^{n-1}$. $\square$

Theorem 6.5.3. Let $n\geq 2$, let $K\subset \mathbb C^ n$ be bounded, and let $E_ j\subset K$, $j\geq 1$, be Borel sets. Suppose that there are Borel probability measures $\mu _ j$ on $\mathbb C^ n$ such that $\mu _ j(E_ j)=1$ and $I_{2n-2}(\mu _ j)\leq C$ for a constant $C<\infty $ independent of $j$. Then some affine complex line meets $E_ j$ for infinitely many $j$.

Proof. We parametrize the affine complex lines that can meet $K$ by a compact set carrying a finite measure $\lambda $, and bound from below, independently of $j$, the $\lambda $-measure of a set of lines meeting $E_ j$. Choose $M>0$ with $K\subset B(0,M)$. Every affine complex line can be written as $c+H^\perp $ with $H\in \mathcal H$ and $c\in H$, and $c=P_ Hy$ for every point $y$ of the line. Indeed, if the line is $a+\mathbb Cb$, let $H$ be the hyperplane orthogonal to $b$, so that $H^\perp =\mathbb Cb$, and put $c=P_ Ha$; then $P_ H(a+\zeta b)=c$ for every $\zeta \in \mathbb C$. If the line meets $K$ at a point $y$, then $|c|=|P_ Hy|\leq |y|<M$. Hence the lines that can meet $K$ are parametrized by the set

\[ \mathcal L_ M=\{ (H,c):H\in \mathcal H,\ c\in H,\ |c|\leq M\} , \]

which is compact, being closed in $\mathcal H\times \overline{B(0,M)}$ because $c\in H$ means $P_ Hc=c$. We equip $\mathcal L_ M$ with the finite measure $\lambda $ defined by

\[ \int h\, \mathrm d\lambda =\int _{\mathcal H}\int _{H\cap \overline{B(0,M)}}h(H,c)\, \mathrm d m_ H(c)\, \mathrm d\nu (H) \]

for nonnegative Borel functions $h$. In the frames of section 6.3, $\lambda $ is the image of the product of $\nu $ and Lebesgue measure on the closed ball of radius $M$ in $\mathbb C^{n-1}$. Thus it is a Borel measure, and its total mass is the volume of a ball of radius $M$ in $\mathbb R^{2n-2}$.

By inner regularity (proposition 1.1.2), choose compact sets $F_ j\subset E_ j$ with $\mu _ j(F_ j)\geq 1/2$. The set

\[ J_ j=\{ (H,c):H\in \mathcal H,\ c\in P_ HF_ j\} \subset \mathcal L_ M \]

parametrizes the lines meeting $F_ j$. It is compact, as the image of $\mathcal H\times F_ j$ under the continuous map $(H,y)\mapsto (H,P_ Hy)$. Its section over $H$ is $P_ HF_ j$, so $\lambda (J_ j)=\int m_ H(P_ HF_ j)\, \mathrm d\nu (H)$. Let $g_{j,H}$ be the densities given by theorem 6.5.2 for $\mu _ j$. Since $F_ j\subset P_ H^{-1}(P_ HF_ j)$, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives, for $\nu $-almost every $H$,

\[ \frac12\leq \mu _ j(F_ j)\leq (P_ H)_*\mu _ j(P_ HF_ j)=\int _{P_ HF_ j}g_{j,H}\, \mathrm d m_ H \leq \| g_{j,H}\| _2\, m_ H(P_ HF_ j)^{1/2}. \]

Integrate this inequality over $H$ with respect to the probability measure $\nu $, and apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality once more. Squaring the result and using theorem 6.5.2, we obtain

\[ \frac14\leq \lambda (J_ j)\int _{\mathcal H}\| g_{j,H}\| _2^2\, \mathrm d\nu (H) \leq \lambda (J_ j)\beta _ n C. \]

In particular $C>0$, and $\lambda (J_ j)\geq \delta =1/(4\beta _ n C)$ for every $j$. The sets $\bigcup _{j\geq m}J_ j$ decrease in $m$ and have measure at least $\delta $. Since $\lambda $ is finite, continuity from above gives

\[ \lambda \left(\bigcap _ m\bigcup _{j\geq m}J_ j\right)\geq \delta >0, \]

so this set is nonempty. If $(H,c)$ belongs to it, then $c\in P_ HF_ j$ for infinitely many $j$. For each such $j$ there is $y\in F_ j$ with $c=P_ Hy$, so $y\in c+H^\perp $. Hence the line $c+H^\perp $ meets $F_ j$, and therefore $E_ j$, for infinitely many $j$. $\square$

Corollary 6.5.4. Let $D\subset \mathbb C^ n$ be open, with $n\geq 1$, and let $u\colon D\to [-\infty ,\infty )$ be a Borel function whose restriction to every connected component of every affine complex-line section of $D$ is subharmonic. Then $u$ is locally bounded above on $D$.

Proof. If $n=1$, the only affine complex line is $\mathbb C$ itself. Thus $u$ is subharmonic on every component of $D$, hence upper semicontinuous and locally bounded above.

Let $n\geq 2$, and suppose that $u$ is not bounded above on any neighborhood of some point $p\in D$. Then there are points $x_ j\to p$ with $A_ j=u(x_ j)\to \infty $. Choose $R>0$ with $\overline{B(p,2R)}\subset D$; discarding finitely many terms, we may assume that $|x_ j-p|<R$ for all $j$. Then $\overline{B(x_ j,R)}\subset B(p,2R)\subset D$, and theorem 6.4.2 gives Borel probability measures $\mu _ j$ carried by the sets

\[ E_ j=\{ y\in \mathbb C^ n:R/2\leq |y-x_ j|\leq R,\ u(y)\geq A_ j\} , \]

with $I_{2n-2}(\mu _ j)\leq C_ nR^{-(2n-2)}$ for every $j$. All the sets $E_ j$ lie in the bounded set $B(p,2R)$. By theorem 6.5.3, some affine complex line $L$ meets $E_ j$ for all $j$ in an infinite set $J$. Choose $y_ j\in L\cap E_ j$ for $j\in J$. Passing to a subsequence, we may assume that $y_ j\to y\in L\cap \overline{B(p,2R)}\subset D$. A small closed disc in $L$ centered at $y$ lies in $D$, so for large $j$ the points $y_ j$ lie in the same connected component of $D\cap L$ as $y$. On this component $u$ is subharmonic, hence upper semicontinuous, and therefore $\varlimsup _ ju(y_ j)\leq u(y)<\infty $. This contradicts $u(y_ j)\geq A_ j\to \infty $. $\square$

Borel measurability enters this argument through the construction of the measures in theorem 6.4.2. In the proof of theorem 8.1.3, measurability is first derived from the line hypothesis, and only then is corollary 6.5.4 applied.
