21.2 Historical and bibliographical notes

Definitions, results, and proofs on this subject.

B Historical and bibliographical notes

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

These notes give the sources of the results in this book. For each chapter we indicate where the main results were first proved or where they can be found in the literature, compare our statements, proofs and normalizations with those of the sources, and point to alternative presentations. The sections follow the order of the book: each concerns one chapter, except that the notes on chapter 17 occupy three sections, and the last section concerns chapter A. A few ideas recur throughout the book: a function is recovered from its distributional derivatives, exceptional sets are controlled by capacities, nonlinear measures are constructed by approximation with bounded potentials, and singular potentials are compared through their envelopes. These ideas have different origins, and their formulation has changed as the theory moved from domains in Euclidean space to compact complex manifolds.

When the book is compared with another source, the conventions of that source matter as much as its hypotheses. We use $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}=\mathrm i\partial \bar\partial /(2\pi )$; Lelong numbers are normalized so that $\log |z|^2$ has Lelong number $1$ at the origin, and multiplier ideals are defined with the weight $\mathrm e^{-\varphi }$. Several of the sources cited below use the operator $\mathrm{dd^ c}=\mathrm i\partial \bar\partial /\pi $, measure Lelong numbers against $\log |z|$ and use the weight $\mathrm e^{-2\varphi }$. A source with the last two conventions assigns to $\varphi =u/2$ the Lelong numbers and multiplier ideals that we assign to $u$. If the operator is multiplied by a constant $c>0$, then at fixed potentials a product of $p$ factors of the form $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ is multiplied by $c^ p$. We record these conversions where they matter.

Theorem and page numbers refer to the cited version of a work. When this version is a preprint rather than the published article, whose numbering or scope may differ, we say so. Two existence theorems are used without proof, namely the resolution and principalization theorems (theorem A.5.4, theorem A.5.5); their source is given in section B.21.

B.1 Measures and distributions

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

The representation of positive linear functionals by measures, theorem 1.2.2, is the Riesz representation theorem. For its formulation on locally compact, $\sigma $-compact spaces compare [Theorem 24, Tao09Radon]; on open subsets of Euclidean space our proof constructs the measure from a metric outer measure and identifies the functional by slicing functions into level sets. The regularity and density statements in proposition 1.1.2, proposition 1.2.3 correspond to [Theorem 21, Exercise 22 and Proposition 10, Tao09Radon]. For the measurability part of the outer-measure construction compare [Theorem 5, Tao10Products]. The assertions on product measures and the interchange of integrals in proposition 1.1.7 form the Tonelli–Fubini theorem for $\sigma $-finite measures; compare [Theorem 36 and its proof, Tao10Products], where the monotone class lemma (Lemma 35 there) is applied to the Boolean algebra of finite disjoint unions of rectangles. We instead apply Dynkin's $\pi $–$\lambda $ theorem, lemma 1.1.6, to the rectangles themselves.

For the local order bound, regularization by mollifiers and the fact that positive distributions are measures (definition 1.3.1, proposition 1.4.3, theorem 1.4.5) compare [Section 1, Exercises 4–5; Section 2, Definition 1, Exercise 15 and Lemma 2, Tao09Distributions]. That source writes the test function first and gives the space of distributions the conjugate complex structure. Our pairing $\langle u,\chi \rangle =\int u\chi \, \mathrm d x$ is complex linear in both variables, the convention used for currents later in the book; with this pairing the derivative of a distribution is defined with the same sign as in that source.

The selection theorem for sequences of measures, theorem 1.5.5, is proved by a diagonal argument over countably many test functions. The compactness theorem for nets, theorem 1.6.3, rests instead on Tychonoff's theorem for a product of intervals, which we prove with ultrafilters (lemma 1.6.2); this is where the axiom of choice enters. Subnets are Willard subnets throughout the book, and proposition 1.6.1 characterizes compactness in these terms. The Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, proposition 1.6.4, is proved in the usual way, by showing that an equicontinuous and pointwise bounded family is uniformly bounded and totally bounded for the uniform metric.

For the differentiation of integrals compare [Section 2, Theorems 32 and 36, Exercise 38 and Lemma 39, Tao10Differentiation]. In theorem 1.7.3 we use cubes instead of balls. The covering argument is the same: a finite disjoint subfamily of the cubes covers the original cubes after enlargement by the factor three, and the constant in the maximal inequality becomes $3^ d$. The Poincaré-type inequality lemma 1.7.4 follows by integrating the fundamental theorem of calculus along segments parallel to the coordinate axes.

For the Radon–Nikodym theorem compare [Corollary 1, following Theorem 2, Tao09RN]. The book uses only the case of a Borel measure that is dominated, at least locally, by a constant multiple of a Radon measure, lemma 1.7.1. We prove it by an $L^2$ argument on half-open dyadic cubes, so that no assumption on the measure of cube boundaries is needed. This lemma provides, for instance, the densities of the coefficient measures in lemma 9.13.1.

B.2 Harmonic functions and boundary values

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

Fundamental solutions, the regularity of distributional solutions of the Laplace equation, the Poisson formula and Harnack's inequality are treated in [Theorems 3.1.2–3.1.7, pp. 117–123, Hor07]. As there, the fundamental solution $E$ of proposition 2.2.1 satisfies $\Delta E=\delta _0$, whereas our Green functions are positive and satisfy $-\Delta G=\delta _ x$. Our proof of Weyl's lemma, theorem 2.1.4, uses radial averaging and convolution; Theorem 3.1.3 of that book contains in addition a statement on analytic continuation. The Poisson formula and Harnack's inequality (theorem 2.3.1, proposition 2.3.2) are stated with the explicit Poisson kernel and its two-sided bounds, and they include the case of dimension one.

Perron's method, barriers and the solution of the Dirichlet problem for continuous boundary data are developed in [Section 3.3, pp. 171–174, especially Theorem 3.3.3, Definition 3.3.4 and Theorem 3.3.5, Hor07]. In theorem 2.4.2, theorem 2.4.4 we work with continuous subsolutions. For the characterization of regular boundary points by barriers compare also [Theorem 3.4.17, pp. 219–220, Hor07]. Our local barrier criterion, proposition 2.4.7, uses a positive continuous supersolution near the given boundary point whose continuous extension to the nearby boundary vanishes only at that point; the theorem in Hörmander's book allows more general exceptional sets. The Green function of theorem 2.5.2 is constructed only on bounded regular domains; within this class the theorem also shows that Green functions increase with the domain and converge along increasing sequences of domains whose union is again bounded and regular.

B.3 Subharmonic functions and polar sets

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

The characterizations of subharmonic functions by comparison with harmonic functions and by mean values, their local integrability, the Riesz decomposition and the distributional characterization are developed in [Theorem 3.2.3, Corollary 3.2.8 and Theorems 3.2.9–3.2.11, pp. 143–148, Hor07]; see theorem 3.1.3, proposition 3.1.5, theorem 3.2.3, theorem 3.2.2. Since a subharmonic function is the limit of its means at every point, the canonical representative of a distribution with positive Laplacian is determined by the distribution at every point, not only almost everywhere. Our Riesz measure is $\Delta u$, without a factor $1/(2\pi )$; thus a logarithmic pole in the plane has Riesz mass $2\pi $.

For the compactness theorem and Hartogs' lemma (theorem 3.3.2, theorem 3.3.3) compare [Theorems 3.2.12–3.2.13, pp. 149–151, Hor07]. Theorem 3.2.13 there also contains convergence statements in Sobolev spaces and against measures; the latter are discussed in the next section. Choquet's lemma and the regularized upper envelope (lemma 3.4.1, theorem 3.4.2) correspond to [Proposition 3.4.4, pp. 205–206, Hor07]; theorem 3.4.2 also asserts that the pointwise supremum of an arbitrary family is Lebesgue measurable. For the local definition of polar sets and the removable singularity theorem (definition 3.5.1, theorem 3.5.2) compare [Definition 3.4.1 and Theorem 3.4.3, pp. 203–205, Hor07]. Our proof works directly with the local subharmonic functions of the definition, and the connectedness of the complement of a closed polar set in a domain follows from the maximum principle.

B.4 Energy, capacity and exceptional sets

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

The continuity principle and the restriction of a measure to compact sets on which its potential is continuous (theorem 4.2.2) follow the argument of [Theorems 3.4.6–3.4.7, pp. 207–208, Hor07]; the approximation lemma of Lusin type used there is lemma 4.2.1. The criterion for polarity of compact sets is [Theorem 3.4.5, pp. 206–208, Hor07]. Our relative capacity of a compact set is the total mass of the Riesz measure $\Delta u$ of its regularized relative extremal function $u$, and our Green functions are positive with $-\Delta G=\delta $; the numerical values of the capacity depend on these normalizations.

Strong subadditivity, outer regularity and capacitability are developed in [Section 3.4, pp. 209–216, especially Theorems 3.4.9 and 3.4.12 and Proposition 3.4.13, Hor07]. Choquet's capacitability theorem, theorem 4.4.1, which underlies the capacitability assertion of that section, is proved here by approximating continuous images of countable intersections of countable unions of compact sets from inside by compact sets. The convergence of integrals against measures with continuous potentials in lemma 4.4.3 is part of [Theorem 3.2.13, pp. 150–151, Hor07], and the fact that the exceptional sets of the limit theorems are polar, theorem 4.4.4, corresponds to [Theorem 3.4.14, pp. 216–217, Hor07]. This polarity strengthens the equality almost everywhere of an upper envelope and its regularization in theorem 3.4.2.

For the identity between Green energy and Dirichlet energy and for quasi-continuity compare [Propositions 3.4.19 and 3.4.21, pp. 221–223, Hor07]. In proposition 4.5.1 we work with the explicit, symmetric Green function of a ball. The positivity of the Newtonian energy and of the logarithmic energy (proposition 4.1.2) is proved by expressing the kernels as integrals of Gaussians. For the logarithmic kernel, positivity and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality are proved, and used, only for signed measures of total mass zero. The equilibrium measure is obtained by minimizing the energy, and proposition 4.4.5 describes its potential (Frostman's theorem) with our normalization of the kernels.

The example [Example 6.1.3, XiaSampleBook] uses an atom-free measure on a polar Cantor set and refers to [p. 31, Car83] for the Cantor set. Such a measure $\mu $ is constructed explicitly in proposition 4.2.4: the Cantor set $K$ is built from a rapidly decreasing sequence of lengths, so that the subharmonic function $p(z)=\int _ K\log |z-a|^2\, \mathrm d\mu (a)$ equals $-\infty $ at every point of $K$. The remaining ingredients of that example, namely the potential on the projective line, its integrability and the computation of its mass in degree one, are proposition 15.3.7, corollary 12.3.2, example 15.3.8.

B.5 Fine topology and balayage

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

Fuglede attributes the classical fine topology to Cartan and the quasi-Lindelöf principle to Doob in [Introduction, p. 227, Fug71Fine]. His preliminaries [Section 1, pp. 228–230, Fug71Fine] are formulated in an axiomatic harmonic space satisfying the domination axiom and admitting a positive potential, and they assume the general duality theory of sweeping before fine connectedness is studied. In the Euclidean setting of this book the quasi-Lindelöf principle, theorem 5.4.1, follows directly from Choquet's lemma and the polarity of the exceptional set of an upper envelope. By example 5.1.7, the fine topology is not Lindelöf, so a countable subfamily can only be expected to cover the union of a family of finely open sets up to a polar set.

Reduction and balayage (theorem 5.2.5, theorem 5.3.1, theorem 5.3.2) are treated only on a ball, where the Green function is explicit and symmetric and Green energy is a Dirichlet integral by proposition 5.2.2. In this setting the swept measure is determined by an orthogonality relation for the energy, which also gives additivity, and the general duality of sweeping recalled by Fuglede is not required. Example 5.3.3 expresses the swept potential of a Dirac measure through harmonic measure, for a compact set whose complement in the ball is a regular domain. Proposition 5.5.1 is proved directly from the definitions; it shows that for $n\geq 2$ the classical fine topology of $\mathbb C^ n=\mathbb R^{2n}$ differs from the topology generated by the upper semicontinuous functions that are subharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on complex lines, which is the plurifine topology of chapter 10 (example 10.3.4).

B.6 Fourier analysis and complex-line incidence

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

For Fourier inversion on the Schwartz space and Plancherel's theorem (lemma 6.1.1, theorem 6.1.2) compare [Exercises 38–42, formula (11), Theorem 43 and Exercise 44, Tao09Fourier]. Our Fourier transform uses the character $\mathrm e^{-2\pi \mathrm i x\cdot \xi }$. Gaussian integration gives the exact constant in the Fourier expression of the Riesz energy, theorem 6.1.4; the kernel there is $|x-y|^{-s}$, without the normalization of a fundamental solution.

For the dyadic decomposition compare [Proposition 4.3 and proof, pp. 23–24, Tao06Fourier3]; for the weak type $(1,1)$ estimate and its consequences by interpolation and duality compare [Lemma 2.7, Corollaries 2.9–2.10 and proofs, pp. 7–9, Tao06Fourier4]. We treat only the Hessian multipliers $-\xi _ a\xi _ b/|\xi |^2$ (lemma 6.2.1), whose kernels are obtained by differentiating the heat kernel. Their weak type $(1,1)$ bound and their $L^ p$ bounds are lemma 6.2.2, theorem 6.2.3; the exponents $p>2$ are obtained by duality. The interpolation inequality lemma 6.2.5 and Morrey's inequality lemma 6.2.6 are used for metric Laplacians in chapter 16.

The star-shaped annular sets, the projections onto complex hyperplanes and the incidence argument follow [Lemmas 1.3–1.5, pp. 2–4, PSHD]; this manuscript carries no author's name and no publication data. The Borel selection required by the argument is lemma 6.4.1: the sets of high values on circles are closed and, by the submean inequality, have positive length, so a median with respect to arc length gives a Borel choice, and no general measurable selection theorem for compact-valued maps is needed. The unitarily invariant measures are constructed from the Gaussian measure on matrices, and volumes in projective space are controlled by an explicit estimate for projective balls instead of volume charts. In corollary 6.5.4 the function is assumed to be Borel; in the proof of theorem 8.1.3 this measurability is established before the corollary is applied.

B.7 Differential forms, currents and holomorphic divisors

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

For positive forms and currents, the characterization of weak positivity by restriction to complex subspaces and the fact that positive currents have order zero, compare [Chapter III, Section 1.A–B, pp. 129–134, especially (1.4), (1.6) and (1.14), DemBook]. In addition, proposition 7.2.1 shows that $\beta ^ p$ lies in the interior of the cone of strongly positive forms, which gives the trace bounds for positive currents; the proof averages over sign changes and permutations of the coordinates. Our currents are indexed by bidegree, whereas Demailly's subscripts there refer to bidimension. Pushforward and pullback of currents (proposition 7.5.1, proposition 7.5.2) correspond to [Chapter III, (1.17), p. 134, DemBook]; for the pushforward the map need only be proper on the support of the current.

The mixed Hodge–Riemann theorem of linear algebra, due to Timorin, is recorded in [Proposition 2.1, p. 4, DN2005mixedv2] (second arXiv version). Lemma 7.2.3 is its case of real $(1,1)$-forms, written with mixed discriminants of Hermitian matrices; we prove it by induction on principal minors and deformation to identity matrices, where the form is given by an explicit trace formula.

For local potentials of closed positive $(1,1)$-currents compare [Chapter III, Section 1.18 and (1.19), p. 135, DemBook]. Our proof of theorem 7.3.2 is different: we subtract the complex Hessian of the potential of the trace measure, and the remainder, which has trace zero and therefore harmonic coefficients, is $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$-exact by the smooth local result lemma 7.3.1. In proposition 7.3.3 the composition with a holomorphic map is taken pointwise, for the canonical representative; it is proved by radial regularization.

Our proof of the Weierstrass preparation theorem, theorem 7.4.2, is the proof by contour integrals in [Chapter II, Section 2.A, pp. 79–80, Theorem (2.1), DemBook], where it is attributed to Siegel. The Poincaré–Lelong formula, theorem 7.4.5, is [Chapter III, (2.15), pp. 143–144, DemBook]. The expression $(\mathrm{i}/\pi )\partial \bar\partial \log |f|$ appearing there equals our $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log |f|^2$, while the operator $\mathrm{dd^ c}$ used subsequently in that book is twice ours. In our proof, slicing by coordinate lines and lemma 7.4.4 show that $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log |f|^2$ puts no mass on the singular locus of the divisor, so that it equals the current of integration over the regular part, weighted by multiplicities. Example 7.4.6 gives the corresponding identity in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$ for quotients of holomorphic functions.

B.8 Plurisubharmonic functions and invariant minima

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

The theorem that a function which is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on complex lines is automatically upper semicontinuous is proved in the unsigned manuscript [Theorem 1.1 and its proof, pp. 1–6, PSHD], and our proof of theorem 8.1.3 completes the argument given there. In that manuscript, Lemma 1.2, pp. 1–2, proves measurability by induction on the dimension, using means over bounded fibers; Proposition 2.1, pp. 4–5, identifies a finite function that is subharmonic on lines with the canonical representative; and the final proof, p. 6, uses truncation. The incidence results needed for this argument are proved in chapter 6, and proposition 7.3.3 describes the restrictions of the canonical representative to complex lines without using the theorem. We keep the usual definition of plurisubharmonic functions (definition 8.1.1), which requires upper semicontinuity; theorem 8.1.3, proved shortly afterwards, shows that this condition is redundant.

The distributional characterization, upper envelopes and compactness of plurisubharmonic functions (theorem 8.2.1, theorem 8.3.2, theorem 8.3.4) are deduced from the corresponding results for subharmonic functions in chapter 3, chapter 4. At this stage the set where an upper envelope differs from its regularization is only known to be real polar, that is, polar as a subset of $\mathbb R^{2n}$ in local charts; its pluripolarity is proved in chapter 10. The extension theorem (theorem 8.4.3) gives both local boundedness and extension across a relatively closed set that is locally $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null; to apply it to analytic sets of codimension at least two (corollary A.2.15) one needs the local estimate for the Hausdorff measure of analytic sets in terms of their dimension.

For $L^ p$ integrability and convergence in $L^ p_{\mathrm{loc}}$ compare [Theorem 1.48, parts 1–2 and proof Steps 1–2, pp. 30–32, GZ17]. As there, we pass from discs to polydiscs (lemma 8.3.5, proposition 8.3.6, theorem 8.3.7), but we use the Riesz decomposition with a compactly supported measure instead of the Poisson–Jensen formula on the boundary. Since our Riesz measure is $\Delta v$, its logarithmic potential carries the factor $1/(2\pi )$. The bound (8.6) gives uniform estimates on smaller polydiscs, and convergence in $L^ p_{\mathrm{loc}}$ then follows from Hölder's inequality. The identity with the factor two in the truncation display of [p. 32, GZ17] is incorrect, as is also noted in an unofficial list of errata for that book.

The local exponential integrability estimates (lemma 8.3.8, proposition 8.3.9) are proved with the same compactly supported Riesz decomposition and Jensen's inequality (lemma 1.1.4), with an exponent that depends only on a local $L^1$ bound; a finite covering by charts then gives the uniform estimate for normalized quasi-plurisubharmonic functions on a compact manifold, corollary 14.2.3.

The partial Legendre transform is due to Kiselman [Kis78]. For his minimum principle and the regularization argument in its proof compare [Chapter I, Section 7.B, Theorem (7.5) and its proof, p. 57, DemBook]. In theorem 8.5.1 the existence of an invariant exhaustion is a hypothesis, and the smooth case is proved with a Schur complement. Such an exhaustion is constructed for product tubes over open convex sets in corollary 8.5.2; the case of general pseudoconvex tubes, corollary 11.2.6, uses in addition the plurisubharmonicity of $-\log $ of the boundary distance.

B.9 Bounded products and nonlinear capacity

From Foundations of pluripotential theory

The recursive definition of products of currents $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ with $u$ locally bounded and plurisubharmonic, the Chern–Levine–Nirenberg estimates and continuity along decreasing sequences are developed in [Chapter III, Section 3, (3.1)–(3.11), pp. 144–150, DemBook]; see theorem 9.1.3, theorem 9.2.5. In our proof of decreasing continuity, the integration by parts formula lemma 9.2.1 for functions that coincide near the boundary of a ball is combined with an induction on the number of factors, which also gives the symmetry of the product at each stage. The weighted estimate theorem 9.3.1 concerns only the measures $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u_1\wedge \cdots \wedge \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u_ q\wedge \beta ^{n-q}$; a version relative to a closed positive current, for one factor at a time, is lemma 15.3.1.

For continuity along increasing sequences, the behavior of products under maxima and the comparison principle (theorem 9.5.1, proposition 9.2.7, theorem 9.2.8) compare [Theorem 3.23, pp. 89–91, and Theorems 3.27 and 3.29, pp. 93–94, GZ17]. The domination principle corollary 9.2.9 is [Proposition 1.2.10, XiaSampleBook]; we prove it by a strictly plurisubharmonic quadratic perturbation and the comparison principle, applied on the given bounded open set itself.

The Monge–Ampère capacity, quasi-continuity and convergence in capacity (definition 9.3.2, theorem 9.4.2, theorem 9.4.8) appear in [Proposition 4.19, Theorem 4.20 and Lemma 4.21, pp. 110–112, and Theorem 4.26, pp. 114–115, GZ17]. Our capacity is defined with competitors taking values between zero and one, whereas the competitors in [Definition 4.16, p. 108, GZ17] take values between $-1$ and $0$; since adding a constant does not change Monge–Ampère measures, the two normalizations give the same capacity. Both Demailly's book and [GZ17] use the operator $\mathrm{dd^ c}=(\mathrm{i}/\pi )\partial \bar\partial $, twice ours, so their Monge–Ampère measures and capacities are $2^ n$ times ours.

The estimate of proposition 9.3.5 is attributed to Błocki in [Proposition 3.21, pp. 88–89, GZ17]. The power $M^{n+1}$ of the oscillation printed there should be $M^ n$: the proof uses $n$ integrations by parts, and the left-hand side is homogeneous of degree $n$ in $v$. We prove the estimate with $M^ n$, under the assumption that $u=h$ near the boundary of the ball, first for smooth functions and then, by regularization and corollary 9.2.6, for bounded ones. Corollary 9.5.2 shows that the set where an upper envelope differs from its regularization has capacity zero; this is used in chapter 10 to show that such sets are pluripolar.

The solution of the homogeneous Dirichlet problem on the ball, theorem 9.6.3, goes back to [BT76]. Averaging over automorphisms that move the center exactly to a prescribed point is carried out for polydiscs in [Section 2.3, Theorem 2.3.2 and Proposition 2.3.3, pp. 50–52, Blo02Pluripotential]; lemma 9.6.1 provides the corresponding automorphisms of the ball. Compare also [Theorems 5.13–5.14, pp. 143–149, GZ17]. In Step 3 of the proof of theorem 9.6.3, the determinant of the complex Hessian of the solution is shown to vanish almost everywhere by comparing the solution with its second-order Taylor polynomial at points where this expansion holds. For functions with bounded real Hessian, the expansion holds almost everywhere by proposition 1.7.5, which is proved with the differentiation of integrals and the Poincaré inequality on cubes. The homogeneous balayage of corollary 9.6.4, together with the comparison principle, shows that a locally bounded plurisubharmonic function is maximal if and only if its Monge–Ampère measure vanishes (theorem 9.6.6); in particular, maximality is a local property for locally bounded functions.

The characterization of the inequality $(\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^ n\geq f\eta ^ n$, where $\eta =\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}|z|^2$, by upper test functions and the linearization of the determinant (proposition 9.7.3) are proved in [Proposition 1.5 and Lemma 1.7, pp. 5–7, EGZ10ViscosityV1], the first arXiv version of the later article by Eyssidieux, Guedj and Zeriahi. The passage from upper test functions to distributional inequalities, which the proof in that preprint quotes from Hörmander, is lemma 9.7.2. The trace matrices used there have determinant $n^{-n}$; multiplying them by $n$ gives our matrices of determinant one.

The inequality for mixed products of smooth functions and its extension to bounded functions whose Monge–Ampère measures have densities with respect to Lebesgue measure are stated in [Theorems 1.1–1.2, pp. 1–2, Din07MixedV1], the first arXiv version of Dinew's article; the extension is attributed there to Kołodziej and Błocki. We prove the case of continuous densities, corollary 9.7.4, by linearization and decreasing convolution, and the case of a common measure in corollary 9.11.2. At fixed potentials, the smooth measure displayed by Dinew is $(4\pi )^ n$ times ours.

The Dirichlet problem on a ball with continuous density, theorem 9.8.2, follows the argument of [Theorems 5.13–5.14 and Corollary 5.15, pp. 143–149, GZ17]. Since the automorphisms of lemma 9.6.1 move the center exactly to a prescribed point, the preliminary Lipschitz comparison with translations used there is not needed. Under such an automorphism $S$ the $n$-th root of the density transforms into $(f^{1/n}\circ S)|\det _{\mathbb C}DS|^{2/n}$; on p. 145 this factor is written first without the absolute value and later without the derivative. The symmetric error is quadratic, and the half-sum is taken only once; this corrects the formulas printed on pp. 145–146. The stability estimate (9.33) contains a term for the boundary data, which is omitted on p. 149, and its proof does not require monotone approximation of the boundary data and of the density. Densities are taken with respect to $\eta ^ n=(\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}|z|^2)^ n$; at fixed potentials, the operator of [GZ17] gives $2^ n$ times our measures.

The extension to bounded densities (lemma 9.9.1, theorem 9.9.2) corresponds to [Theorem 5.17 and Lemma 5.18, pp. 150–151, GZ17]. In lemma 9.9.1 the comparison is made on strict sublevel sets, and only the shifted sublevel set is required to be relatively compact. In the proof of theorem 9.9.2 the approximating densities have an explicit common bound. Convergence in capacity of the approximating solutions is proved without assuming that the solution for a bounded measurable density is continuous inside the ball, by applying Hartogs' lemma on compact sets outside the exceptional sets of quasi-continuity. Approximating the $n$-th roots of the densities by convolution extends the stability estimate (9.33) to bounded densities.

The class $\mathcal E_0(B)$ of definition 9.10.1 is due to Cegrell: its definition on hyperconvex domains is recalled in [Section 3, p. 161, Ceg04], where the class is said to have been introduced in [Ceg98], and it is also recalled in [Section 2, p. 3, Din07MixedV1]. The moment condition used in Dinew's article to approximate a measure by its averages over cubes is stated in Theorem 2.2, pp. 4–5, whose conclusions on approximation and stability are quoted from other sources. Theorem 9.10.2 establishes the moment condition for measures dominated by the Monge–Ampère measure of a bounded plurisubharmonic function; it follows from Błocki's estimate proposition 9.3.5 by integration over level sets, after truncating the function with zero boundary values and passing to a smaller ball. The constants depend explicitly on the mass of the measure and on the oscillation of the subsolution, and the estimate also holds in dimension one.

By proposition 9.10.3, which combines this estimate with the local $L^ p$ bound proposition 8.3.6, the cube averages satisfy the moment condition uniformly. Applied to the solutions with zero boundary values, this gives uniform bounds for their local energy moments, and applied to the measure $\eta ^ n$, a constant multiple of Lebesgue measure, it gives compactness in $L^1$ along subsequences. At fixed potentials, Dinew's operator multiplies measures and local energy moments by $(4\pi )^ n$; because of the power of the mass on its right-hand side, both sides of (9.43) are multiplied by the same factor.

The convergence of the energy integrals of the approximating solutions is asserted in [Theorem 2.2(3), p. 5, Din07MixedV1], where it is quoted from Cegrell. Theorem 9.10.5 proves it for compactly supported measures dominated by the Monge–Ampère measure of a bounded plurisubharmonic function, along every subsequence of solutions that converges in $L^1$. One ingredient is the continuity of integrals against a fixed measure that does not charge sets of capacity zero, lemma 9.10.4.

Convergence in capacity, as a consequence of the convergence of the energy integrals, is quoted in [Theorem 2.3, p. 5, Din07MixedV1]. In theorem 9.10.9 we derive it from the comparison of capacities of strict sublevel sets (lemma 9.9.1) and the bound on second moments. By lemma 9.10.7, truncation preserves the total Monge–Ampère mass of a function in $\mathcal E_0(B)$; the lemma also bounds the change of the measure caused by truncation in terms of the energy, and shows that Monge–Ampère measures with bounded energy are uniformly absolutely continuous with respect to capacity.

The definition of the solution as an envelope of subsolutions is recalled in [Definition 2.4 and the following paragraph, p. 5, Din07MixedV1]. We solve the Dirichlet problem with zero boundary values, and identify the solution with this envelope, for compactly supported measures on a ball that are dominated by the Monge–Ampère measure of a bounded plurisubharmonic function (theorem 9.10.12).

The mixed energy inequalities used in the approximation argument are recorded in [Section 2, p. 4, Din07MixedV1] and attributed there to Cegrell. For their proof by integration by parts and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality compare [Lemma 5.4 and Theorem 5.5, pp. 168–170, Ceg04]; Theorem 5.5 there has a fixed weight and exponents $1/n$. Theorem 9.10.13 is the version with $n+1$ functions, with an explicit but weaker constant. If $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$ is multiplied by a constant $c>0$, both sides of (9.69) are multiplied by $c^ n$. Corollary 9.10.14 uses this bound to discard the sets where the functions are very negative before continuity in capacity is applied; the limit functions there are locally bounded.

Dinew's inequality for bounded functions and a common measure is [Theorem 3.3, p. 9, Din07MixedV1]. Its proof by approximation (Proposition 3.1 and Lemma 3.2 there, pp. 7–9) relies on results on the local Dirichlet problem, energies and continuity in capacity that are quoted from other sources. Theorem 9.11.1 allows $n$ different functions; its proof uses the same approximation by cube averages, with these inputs proved in the preceding sections. The inequality for mixed discriminants, lemma 9.7.5, follows from the reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality given by lemma 7.2.3, and linearization of the determinant then gives the case of continuous densities, corollary 9.7.6. In this bounded setting the common measure may charge pluripolar sets. The extension to unbounded functions in Cegrell's classes, [Theorem 3.4, Din07MixedV1], is not treated here.

The computation on contact sets in [Lemma 2.4.7, Step 1, XiaSampleBook] uses Stampacchia's theorem that weak derivatives vanish almost everywhere on level sets, but it also asserts that a function in $W^{2,1}$ has a classical real Hessian. A complete argument is given in [Lemma 3.1 and Theorem 3.2, pp. 4–8, DNT2019v1] (first arXiv version). In this book the vanishing of weak derivatives on level sets is lemma 9.12.1, proved with a smooth truncated chain rule, and functions with bounded Euclidean Laplacian have real $W^{2,2}$ derivatives by lemma 9.12.2. With these lemmas we prove proposition 9.12.3, proposition 9.12.4 for regular potentials and obstacles in $W^{2,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}$; the density on the contact set does not require the determinant of the obstacle to be integrable on the whole domain. Obstacles that are not bounded above are already covered by theorem 16.2.5, because capping the obstacle strictly above the envelope changes neither the envelope nor its contact set. Pseudo-effective classes are treated in corollary 17.4.21, after the criterion for bigness: if the class is not big, every non-pluripolar measure of top degree vanishes.

For functions whose Laplacian with respect to a metric is bounded, and for the reduction to regular contact, compare [p. 4 and Lemma 3.1, pp. 4–5, DNT2019v1]. Such functions are locally in $W^{2,2}$ by lemma 16.2.8, which is derived from the elliptic theory of lemma 14.1.3. Proposition 16.2.9 applies the vanishing of weak derivatives on level sets to obstacles in $W^{2,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}$, for instance continuous obstacles with bounded metric Laplacian; the computation on the contact set needs only this local regularity. The stronger elliptic $W^{2,p}$ estimate quoted in the source is proved separately in proposition 16.2.11, by freezing the principal coefficients and testing the equation against a regularization of $u|u|^{p-2}$, and corollary 16.2.12 then shows that the signed determinant of such obstacles is integrable on all of $X$.

Approximation of envelopes by Monge–Ampère equations with an exponential penalty, and the limit as the parameter tends to infinity, are due to Berman; the equations and estimates are presented in [equations (2.1)–(2.2), pp. 282–283, Tos18], where smooth solvability is referred to Aubin and Yau. We prove solvability for positive parameters in theorem 16.2.21 by the continuity method, with a contraction argument in Sobolev spaces and a priori estimates along the path. The third-order estimate, lemma 16.2.17, bounds the difference of two Chern connections. Its computation follows the tensor calculation in [Proposition 2.8 and Theorem 2.9, pp. 24–26, SongWeinkove2012v1], the first arXiv version of Song and Weinkove's lecture notes, where such estimates are traced back to a calculation of Calabi and the explicit formula in the parabolic case is attributed to Phong, Šešum and Sturm. Our calculation is elliptic: the penalized equation bounds the Ricci tensor and its covariant derivative in terms of the difference of connections.

The constants in the uniform trace bound and in the convergence to the envelope (lemma 16.2.15, proposition 16.2.22) depend only on the fixed geometry, on a bound for the obstacle $f$ and on an upper bound for the trace $\Lambda \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f=-Lf/(4\pi )$ of its complex Hessian, that is, on a lower bound for its metric Laplacian $Lf$. Tosatti writes forms with $\mathrm i\partial \bar\partial $; in the notation of section 16.2.3, the coefficient matrices $h$ and $k$ represent $2\pi \omega $ and $2\pi \kappa $, so that $g_{i\bar j}=k_{i\bar j}+a_{i\bar j}$ is the coefficient matrix of $2\pi (\kappa +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}a)$.

The proof of the Nullstellensatz includes the description of the radical as an intersection of prime ideals.

The support theorem for closed currents of order zero, theorem A.2.17, is the case of bidegree $(1,1)$ of [Chapter III, (2.10)–(2.14), pp. 141–143, DemBook]. Our proof uses smooth normal coordinates, convolution in tangential directions and induction over the singular loci of the analytic set; the theorem is applied in lemma 14.4.6. In the proof of proposition 14.4.7, the pullback is corrected with the pushforward of a Kähler form, which gives the required strict positivity directly.

For universal denominators of weakly holomorphic functions and for normalization (proposition A.3.3, theorem A.3.6) compare [Chapter II, (7.1)–(7.15), pp. 110–116, DemBook]. The cusp and the pair of crossing lines in example A.3.10 illustrate the difference between being normal and being unibranch. The Remmert–Stein extension theorem and Remmert's proper mapping theorem are proved together in [Chapter II, (8.7)–(8.8), pp. 118–121, DemBook]. Our proof of theorem A.6.1, theorem A.6.2 also treats them together, but it uses the resolution of singularities, theorem A.5.4, to reduce to proper maps defined on manifolds, and it proves the statements on finite maps and on the dimension of hypersurfaces separately.

The resolution and principalization theorems (theorem A.5.4, theorem A.5.5) are the analytic versions of [Theorems 1.1 and 1.3, pp. 611 and 614; Definitions 2.1–2.2, pp. 618–619, BM08]; that the results apply to complex analytic spaces is stated on p. 611 there. The relative principalization theorem, theorem A.6.5, follows the lemma on relative principalization in the unpublished manuscript [Lemma relative-principalization, VuXiaNakamaye]. At each step of this construction, the centers in the ambient manifold divide the ideal by exactly one exceptional factor, as required for the controlled transform of the restricted ideal. In our statement the submanifold is closed and the ambient manifold is connected, so that the global blowups and the identities between ideals hold as stated.

Theorem A.7.1 states that a proper bimeromorphic map has connected fibers over the points at which the germ of the target is irreducible, in particular over unibranch spaces. Its proof follows the argument with complements of branches in [Appendix on unibranch spaces, XiaSampleBook]; properness is used to place the inverse image of a small neighborhood inside neighborhoods of the pieces of the fiber. The local upper bound near analytic subsets of codimension at least two in a reduced space, proposition A.8.4, is proved by slicing with curves over a finite projection and the maximum principle for subharmonic functions. It complements the extension theorem on manifolds, corollary A.2.15, which follows from the criterion in terms of Hausdorff measure.

The corollary on the realization of divisors, in the appendix of [XiaSampleBook] on unibranch spaces, underlies the comparison of multiplier ideals with Lelong numbers along divisors in Part I of that book. Theorem A.7.6 proves this corollary by principalizing the successive centers and decreasing the order of the Jacobian along a fixed divisor. The definition of plurisubharmonic functions on reduced spaces by local ambient extension, definition A.8.1, suffices for pullback by holomorphic maps, for the maximum principle on compact fibers and for the upper bound obtained by slicing with curves. On the manifolds used in Part I of that book, the descent of quasi-plurisubharmonic functions under modifications, proposition 14.2.8, is proved directly by extension across an analytic set of codimension at least two.
