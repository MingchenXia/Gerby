21.3 Historical notes

34 Historical notes

Local regularization and products provide a common starting point for the results of this book. A smooth local correction makes these methods available for nonclosed backgrounds, including on Kähler manifolds. Global arguments must distinguish a closed class on a non-Kähler manifold from a nonclosed background: the former may lack the Kähler positivity used in classical existence theorems, while the latter loses the cohomological control of Monge–Ampère masses. Gauduchon duality, relative envelopes and the available mass bounds connect the sources discussed below. Their hypotheses also decide whether a solution keeps the ordinary singularity type of its reference or belongs only to its relative envelope class.

These notes identify the sources of the results of the book, with precise locators, and explain how the arguments given in the book relate to the original ones. Indications such as v1 or arXiv version in a locator refer to the version of the source that was used. Where needed, the conversion from the normalization of $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$ or of the Chern–Ricci form used in a source to the conventions fixed at the beginning of the book is indicated next to the citation.

34.1 Plurisubharmonic functions and regularization

Compactness, integrability and regularization are local properties, and they survive the passage to a nonclosed background because a quasi-plurisubharmonic function becomes plurisubharmonic after a smooth local correction. The $L^1$ compactness of normalized potentials in theorem 1.4.2, also stated by Dinew [Proposition 6.2, Dinew16], follows by propagating local compactness over a connected coordinate cover. Local exponential integrability, as recalled by Zeriahi [Section 1, Zeriahi01], becomes uniform over normalized potentials in lemma 1.5.1, through a contradiction argument with a weighted sum of potentials.

The comparison of convolutions in different coordinate charts in lemma 1.9.1, corollary 1.9.2 is the bounded part of the argument of Błocki and Kołodziej [§2, Lemmas 4–5, BK07]. Their full regularization theorem [Theorem 1, BK07] allows unbounded potentials and a positive continuous background. The proof of theorem 1.11.1 treats only the bounded case, which is all that the construction of products requires; it includes the extraction of a decreasing diagonal sequence and the rescaling that keeps the regularized forms strictly positive.

Demailly's regularization by the flow of the Chern connection [Theorem 4.1, Dem94] applies to $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ for every $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, where $\theta $ is a smooth real $(1,1)$-form: the theorem concerns a closed current with an arbitrary smooth lower bound, here $-\theta $, and this lower bound need not be closed. The monotonicity and logarithmic convexity of the regularization enter the estimate (1.72), whose linear error rate follows from the curvature inequality [Lemma 4.4, Dem94] and the expansion in [Proposition 3.8, equations (3.11), (4.3), and (4.5), Dem94]. In the present situation the curvature inequality needs neither a small $\epsilon $ term nor an $M_\epsilon $ correction, and local trace bounds make the constants uniform in the center and the radius.

The same expansion underlies the truncated Kiselman transform of lemma 1.21.1; see [Theorem 4.1 and Remark 4.7, Dem94] and Kołodziej–Nguyen [Lemma 4.1, KN19]. In lemma 1.21.3 the full base–fiber error is kept as a modulus tending to zero, and, as in [Remark 4.7, Dem94], a term linear in the radius absorbs the mixed and vertical errors. This formulation accommodates the unbounded potentials with finite $L^1$ norm that occur in theorem 14.7.1, where localizing the averaging radius makes the minimum-principle argument available away from the singular barrier.

For a continuous potential, the logarithmic derivative of the Chern averages tends to zero. Together with Dini's theorem and uniform continuity, this gives the uniform smooth approximation with vanishing curvature loss in lemma 1.21.4. For a Hölder reference, lemma 1.21.5 retains a common Hölder norm and rescales to genuine potentials. Dinh, Ma and Nguyên [Theorem 2.1, pp. 551–555, DMNFekete17] prove quantitative smooth regularization in a Kähler background using its local potentials. The proof here uses Chern averages and the continuous-potential curvature-loss estimate on the Hermitian background. Their quantitative Kähler estimate is not used.

Demailly's regularization in the form of theorem 1.15.3, whose approximants become divisorial with a smooth remainder after finitely many blowups, is the statement of Demailly and Păun [Theorem 3.2, DP04]. It allows an arbitrary smooth lower bound on a Hermitian manifold, and the smooth remainder after resolution is essential for its applications here. This sequence is in general different from the multiplier-ideal sequence of theorem 1.15.1, and we do not assume that a single sequence has the additional properties of both.

The analyticity of the sets where the Lelong number is at least $c>0$, recalled in 1.13.1 and used in lemma 26.18.2, theorem 4.5.1, is Siu's theorem [Main Theorem, p. 53, Siu74]; a smooth local correction makes it applicable to quasi-plurisubharmonic functions. The dimension bound for the Lelong level set and the existence of global irreducible components are stated in Demailly's book [Chapter III, Corollary 8.5, p. 174; Chapter II, Theorem 5.3, pp. 102–103, DemCADG]. The non-strict inequality is essential. In example 1.13.3, a summable series of line currents on the projective plane has the property that the set where the Lelong number exceeds one is a sequence of points accumulating at a point outside that set.

The closedness of polar curvature currents, proposition 1.25.6, is proved by the complete pluripolar restriction argument of Boucksom, Eyssidieux, Guedj and Zeriahi [author preprint v2, Theorem 1.8, Lemma 1.9 and Remark 1.10, BEGZ10]. The chain rule for bounded functions allows cutoffs that converge only pointwise, so the complete pluripolar set need not be closed, and a smooth local correction extends the argument to quasi-plurisubharmonic potentials with an arbitrary smooth background. Combined with the recovery of closed currents carried by a Lebesgue-null set from their traces, this removes the hypothesis that the pole set be closed from corollary 16.6.64, proposition 16.6.65. The simultaneous realization of the limiting currents is a separate question.

The Hausdorff estimate of corollary 1.13.2 follows from the Lelong mass formulas in Demailly's book [Chapter III, formulas (5.5)–(5.8), pp. 158–159, DemCADG] and holds in every positive bidimension. When a closed polar current vanishes outside an exceptional locus, the support theorem for currents carried by analytic sets [Chapter III, Corollaries 2.11 and 2.14, pp. 141–143, DemCADG] identifies the remaining divisorial part. Full local Monge–Ampère products at an unbounded locus of Hausdorff dimension zero rely instead on [Chapter III, Theorem 4.5 and Proposition 4.9, pp. 152–156, DemCADG], as stated in theorem 1.6.9. These products keep their top-degree mass on the pole set, whereas every lower-degree coefficient measure vanishes there. The local maximum argument of lemma 1.6.10 compares the residual measures and shows that they are invariant under bounded perturbations. Their global consequences for masses and models are discussed in section 34.2, section 34.9.

The local reference-integral estimate lemma 1.8.3 is related to the Hölder estimates for measure functionals of Dinh and Nguyên [arXiv v1, Lemma 3.5 and Proposition 3.6, pp. 9–10, DNHolder14]. Here the tests are bounded plurisubharmonic functions on nested balls. Keeping the cutoff-gradient terms gives a bound uniform in the Hölder norm of the reference; a finite cover yields corollary 1.8.4 for bounded tests with a common curvature lower bound.

The local moderation theorem of Dinh, Nguyên and Sibony [Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2, pp. 466–467; proof pp. 468–471, DNSModerate10] gives exponential integrability with respect to locally moderate currents. The top-degree estimate lemma 1.8.5 follows here by applying the reference-integral estimate to consecutive truncations and absorbing their polynomial growth into the exponential tail. Guedj and Zeriahi's extremal-capacity comparison [Theorem 5.2, pp. 622–623, and Proposition 7.1, p. 632, GZIntrinsic05] motivates lemma 1.8.6. Its proof uses conservation of mass only for the closed Fubini–Study form on an auxiliary projective space. Local capacity comparison gives the Hermitian estimate proposition 1.8.7 with constants uniform for a common Hölder norm.

The convergence against a fixed non-pluripolar measure in lemma 1.23.1 rests on Cegrell's classical fixed-measure argument, which Kołodziej and Nguyen also state in a form allowing extraction of subsequences [Lemma 2.1 and Corollary 2.2, KN22]. Applying that statement to every subsequence gives convergence of the whole bounded sequence.

Let $f$ be a holomorphic function and $\chi $ a convex nondecreasing function. The sharp Sobolev criteria for the potentials $\chi (\log |f|)$ in 1.6.3 are due to Chen, Shao and Wang [v1, Theorems 1.1–1.2, pp. 2–3 and 19–22, CSW26]; their estimate in terms of the smallest multiplicity and their energy bound on small sublevel sets are [v1, Lemma 4.1 and Proposition 3.8, pp. 16–20, CSW26]. The proofs given here use the regularizations $\chi (\frac12\log (|f|^2+\varepsilon ^2))$, $\varepsilon >0$, to identify the weak gradient and to keep track of the residual divisor term in the distributional Laplacian, a term that the classical Laplacian omits. The power examples and their endpoint obstruction in example 1.6.14 are the calculation in normal coordinates and the table of [v1, Lemma 2.1 and Section 6.1, pp. 6–7 and 24–25, CSW26]. These local criteria for derivatives should not be confused with the theorem on products at a small unbounded locus discussed above.

The concentration example of proposition 1.6.18 is the point-mass case of Qin's [v1, Corollary 3.6, p. 14, QinLimits26]. Here the mass is computed directly from the transverse identity (1.19), which gives a smooth variant of the logarithmic truncation in [Lemma 2.4, pp. 9–10, QinLimits26]. The conclusion of lemma 1.6.16 for sequences with a common local bound agrees with [Proposition 7.2, p. 26, QinLimits26]; in the book it follows from the local range estimate proved earlier.

Theorem 1.6.22, which adds an arbitrary positive Radon measure to the Monge–Ampère measure of a fixed bounded potential in the limit, is Qin's [v1, Theorem B, p. 3, QinLimits26]. The addition along finitely many test functions and the passage to a determining family correspond to [Proposition 3.2 and Theorem 3.4, pp. 11–14, QinLimits26]. In the proof given here, the smooth ridge gives the limits of the lower-degree currents directly, finite iteration over atoms controls every mixed term, and the one-sided logarithmic bound also gives convergence in local capacity. The smoothness of the approximants is asserted for a smooth background, whereas Qin also treats continuous backgrounds.

The classification of vague cluster sets in theorem 1.6.24, according to which every vaguely closed set of positive measures is the full cluster set of a sequence of potentials tending to zero, is the case of the zero potential in [v1, Theorem A, pp. 2–3, QinLimits26]. The metric for the vague topology and the concatenation of dense sequences are [Lemma 3.3, p. 12 and Lemma 4.1, p. 14, QinLimits26], and the comparison example with moving measures is [Example B.1, p. 28, QinLimits26]. In the book, finite addition of atoms gives smooth approximants that converge in local capacity directly. The construction with moving measures over a fixed bounded background in theorem 1.6.26 also corresponds to [Theorem 3.4, pp. 13–14, QinLimits26]. The cutoff argument of lemma 1.6.25 shows that, under convergence in local capacity, every limit measure dominates the Monge–Ampère measure of the background; this gives a complete classification of the cluster sets for bounded backgrounds approximated from below. Convergence in local capacity is a stronger requirement than the $L^1$ convergence in Qin's general classification of reachable sets, whose argument for reaching the zero measure needs additional hypotheses on maximal approximation and on the domain; that argument is not used here.

Let $\phi _1,\ldots ,\phi _ r$ be plurisubharmonic weights with exponents $p_1,\ldots ,p_ r>1$, as in theorem 1.16.10. The division estimate with two summands, lemma 1.16.8, is the case of two weights in Takakura's [v1, Theorem 1.3, pp. 2 and 5–6, TakDivision26]. Since the relevant kernel is a line, the proof can use the Hessian formula for a scalar function instead of the general curvature theorem for subbundles. The constant in the a priori inequality (1.46) follows from the twisted Bochner identity in Demailly's notes [Section 13.A, pp. 123–124, DemAM11] by a weighted Cauchy inequality. Proposition 13.4 there states the weaker constant $2$; keeping the full factor $\eta +\lambda $ gives the constant $1$ used here. For a single weight, proposition 1.16.9 is the case $r=1$ of [Theorem 1.2, pp. 1–2 and 6–7, TakDivision26]. Applying it successively gives the product constant of theorem 1.16.10, with no condition on $\sum _ i1/p_ i$. The coordinate example in its proof extends [Example 3.2, p. 7, TakDivision26] and shows that this constant is optimal when $r\leq n$. For $2\leq r\leq n$ the product is strictly smaller than $1-r^{-1}\sum _ i1/p_ i$, the constant stated in Takakura's theorem. For $r>n$ the product need not improve Takakura's dimension-dependent estimate, and by itself it does not determine the optimal constant.

In dimension one, theorem 1.16.13 gives the optimal constant for every number $r$ of weights, including the range $\sum _ i1/p_ i\geq 1$ in which it vanishes. When $\sum _ i1/p_ i<1$, it equals the dimension-one constant of [Theorem 1.2, pp. 1–2, TakDivision26], and a common logarithmic pole shows that it is sharp. The local argument in lemma 1.16.11 uses the logarithmic fundamental solution and the characterization of positive distributions in [Chapter I, Sections 4.A–4.C, pp. 30–36, DemCADG]; in our normalization, $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log |z|^2=\delta _0$. The global lifting in lemma 1.16.12 is the locally free planar case of vanishing for coherent sheaves. It is proved here with the vector-valued $L^2$ estimate for complete metrics in [Theorem 5.1 and its proof, pp. 33–34, DemAM11], whose Bochner identity is [Section 4.C, p. 30, DemAM11]. We do not determine the optimal constant when $n\geq 2$ and $r>n$.

34.2 Mass and energy

On a nonclosed background, the variation of the Monge–Ampère mass and the failure of the usual energy to have a primitive are related manifestations of torsion. Chiose's polarization argument [Theorems 1–2, Chiose16] gives the characterization of mass invariance in theorem 2.2.2. Yi Li constructed energies corrected by torsion terms [Equation (1.18) and Theorem 1.2, YiLi10]. The correction changes the one-form being integrated, in agreement with theorem 3.1.3, which shows, for $n\geq 2$, that the uncorrected one-form has no primitive unless $\omega $ is closed; proposition 3.3.1 computes the difference by a first variation.

Cutoffs of diffuse poles separate this variation of the background from a fixed loss of mass at the singularity. Let $n\geq 2$, and let $H$, $C$ and $M$ be the cutoff, the curvature constant and the residual mass of proposition 16.6.71; fix $0<t<1/C$ and put $A=\int _ X\omega ^ n$. The mass of $tH$ is a polynomial in $t$ whose finite torsion coefficients come from Stokes calculations for compactly supported bounded products. By proposition 16.6.73, every representative that is ordinarily bounded-equivalent to $tH$ loses the same top residual mass $Mt^ n$, although its lower-degree coefficients can vary. If $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(\omega ^ q)=0$ for $1\leq q<n$, invariance of the mass under bounded changes and invariance of the residual give the mass $A-Mt^ n$ throughout the class (corollary 16.6.72); this applies to every compact complex surface with a Gauduchon form. On a surface, corollary 16.6.74 shows that the mass is constant on the class if and only if $\omega $ is Gauduchon, since admissible smooth bounded perturbations detect any failure of the Gauduchon condition.

The failure of the bounded mass property on a Hopf threefold is the counterexample of Xia and Zhang [Theorem 1.1, XZ26]. There a Gauduchon background converts vertical Dirichlet energy into Monge–Ampère mass, a mechanism developed in chapter 8. Combined with theorem 6.6.1, it produces infinite extended mass with trivial multiplier ideals at every positive weight, and the results on integrability and convergence of coefficients in section 6.7 describe the linear current carried by this construction. Xia and Zhang use the operator $\mathrm i\partial \bar\partial $ [Section 1, XZ26]; dividing their background forms by $2\pi $ and leaving their potentials unchanged preserves positivity and changes the coefficient of the mass identity.

The local matrix, heat potential, Gaussian gradient estimate and completed square used in section 8.8 are those of Xia and Zhang [v1, Lemma 2.2, pp. 4–5; Lemmas 3.1–3.3, pp. 6–9; equation (4.2), p. 10, XZ26]. The faster initial time in theorem 8.8.5 changes the radial length and the integrated defect, so its positive correction is proved at that scale. The positive fiber flux and depth estimate give the square-root upper bound for all potentials in the declared unitary invariant class. The lower-density regions then give a single infinite weighted measure and concentration on a pole divisor at the critical weight; they also impose necessary conditions without the symmetry assumption. The unrestricted optimal sufficient rate remains open.

The passage from smooth to bounded and unbounded invariant potentials uses the classical bounded product and capacity theory developed in section 1.6, section 1.8. The local product construction and decreasing continuity are Bedford and Taylor's [Theorem 2.1, pp. 4–7; Theorem 2.4, pp. 7–9; Corollary 2.8, p. 10, BT82]; their quasi-continuity and zero-capacity results are [Theorem 3.5, p. 14; Theorems 6.9–6.10, pp. 25–26, BT82]. They use $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}=2\mathrm i\partial \bar\partial $, which is $4\pi $ times our operator and changes capacities only by a fixed factor. The local $L^1$ compactness underlying theorem 1.4.2 is also described in [Chapter I, Proposition 4.21, pp. 36–37; Proposition 5.9, p. 40, DemCADG]. In the present argument, group convolution and canonical truncation give the original Hermitian capacity convergence, including the boundary mass at a truncation level.

The extension in theorem 8.6.2 keeps the same threefold heat family. Its proof first bounds the actual mass on the negative radial half-line by exact integration over the elliptic fiber, then uses lemma 8.6.1 to compare the angularly integrated densities in different dimensions. The additional angular factor has a fixed positive lower bound on the half-line carrying the divergent mass. Corollary 8.6.3 transfers this failure to every Hermitian metric on the same scalar Hopf manifold.

The failure of positive lower mass is a different phenomenon; it occurs on the elliptic threefold of section 26.18. Sufficient conditions for a positive lower bound come instead from the sign of the torsion: (26.92) is the three-dimensional case of a formula of Angella, Guedj and Lu [Theorem 3.2, AGL23], who call the condition $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega \leq 0$ plurinegativity.

The principal elliptic bundles of section 33.3.3 are special cases of the torus bundles of Goldstein and Prokushkin [Section 2, GP04]. The metric calculation on these bundles gives an upper bound for the mass, but it does not decide whether the lower mass vanishes. The radial calculation of proposition 33.3.7 bounds the root volume of the specified affine test family at fixed degree; the lower mass over all potentials remains open.

The completion energy of section 3.4 is the Dinew–Popovici functional. Hanwen Liu defines it and computes its variations in [v1, Section 2.1, pp. 3–5, LiuCritical26], and proves the identity relating energy and volume along potential variations, together with the indefiniteness of the Hessian, in [Theorems 5.1–5.2, pp. 16–19, LiuCritical26]. Liu assumes that the ambient manifold is Kähler. The proofs in the book use only the finite-dimensional space of closed holomorphic completions and Stokes' theorem, so these statements about potential variations hold on every compact Hermitian symplectic fourfold. Liu's calculation of criticality for products [Lemma 2.3 and Theorem 2.4, pp. 7–9, LiuCritical26] gives the mechanism of example 3.4.5, where an explicit Fourier form on the square torus replaces the general existence argument on surfaces. Neither the general existence criterion of that paper in terms of the canonical bundle nor its convergence theorem for the global torsion flow is used here. Liu's convention is $\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}=\mathrm i(\bar\partial -\partial )$; rewritten with the book's $\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}$ and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$, the formulas of that paper for potential variations give (3.15) with the coefficients shown there. Combined with Chiose's criterion, the energy identity gives the equivalence between flatness of the energy and invariance of the mass in corollary 3.4.4. The calculation with one nonclosed factor in example 3.4.6 shows that this flatness along potentials implies neither criticality in the full Aeppli class nor the existence of a primitive for the Monge–Ampère one-form.

34.3 Gauduchon metrics and positivity

Gauduchon metrics replace the cohomological conservation of mass by a linear trace identity. In the form recalled by Tosatti and Weinkove immediately before [Lemma 2.3, TW10], Gauduchon's conformal theorem starts, in dimension $n\geq 2$, from a Hermitian form $\eta $ and constructs a smooth real function $f$ such that $\gamma =\mathrm e^ f\eta $ is Gauduchon; the multiplier in theorem 4.1.2 is then $\rho =\mathrm e^{(n-1)f}$. The resulting trace comparison, corollary 4.2.2, also underlies the first mixed-volume bounds of Guedj and Lu [Proposition 3.3, GL22].

For lemma 4.9.1, the constant-coefficient estimate is the order-zero multiplier theorem in Taylor [Chapter 13, Theorem 5.1 and its proof, pp. 16–21, Taylor13]; compactness is [Chapter 13, (6.9), p. 24, Taylor13]. Freezing the continuous coefficients gives the Sobolev estimate, and the proof writes out the classical Alexandrov–Bakelman–Pucci contact-set bound and tangent-ball maximum principle before applying the method of continuity and compact-perturbation index theory. It does not differentiate the coefficients.

The trace identity has a dual form: positivity can be tested against Gauduchon metrics. For an arbitrary smooth background, the generalized Lamari criterion theorem 4.3.1 is stated by Boucksom, Guedj and Lu [Lemma 1.15, BGL25]; its proof combines a separation argument with a right inverse of a scalar elliptic operator. In theorem 4.5.1, Lamari duality combines with normalized compactness and Siu analyticity to turn bounded transverse test pairings into a global hypersurface. The regularized logarithmic potentials in proposition 4.5.2 give the converse bound when a Cartier divisor agrees with the cutoff hypersurface throughout its chart. The same linear control gives the bound (4.3) for the normalization constant, which is used in the weak existence arguments of [Section 4, BGL25].

The deformation obstruction for convex norms in theorem 4.6.4 is due to Liu and Wan [v1, Theorem 3.2 and Sections 3.1–3.3, pp. 9–13, LiuWanKobayashi26], and the normalization of line metrics in lemma 4.6.1 is their Lemma 3.1, p. 8. The proof combines derivative estimates along the line axes with an average of the horizontal curvature and the formal adjoint on a fixed sphere bundle. The resulting $C^4$ neighborhood is uniform over all smooth complex norms, and the positive Gauduchon test forms may differ from one line summand to another.

The regularization of homogeneous functions in theorem 4.6.8 is the case of a compact base of a lemma of Kuang-Ru Wu [v1, Section 2.3, Lemma 13, pp. 10–13, WuFinslerIII23]. Its gluing step uses the closure under maxima, the stability under smooth perturbations and the semilocal approximation of Greene and Wu [Definitions 1.1, 1.3–1.4, pp. 48–49; Lemma 4.1 and Theorem 4.1, pp. 80–81, GreeneWu79], while the local positivity properties are proved directly from a holomorphic shear formula. The replacement in proposition 4.6.9 assumes that a strictly plurisubharmonic homogeneous function is given, and corollary 4.6.10 keeps the hypothesis of local strong convexity in the fibers.

The local example example 4.6.5 adapts the quadratic maximum of Fukuoka and Setti [v1, Example 3.18, p. 20, FukuokaSettiStrongConvex26] to a complex rank-two bundle over a disk. A geometric series of squared holomorphic moduli gives its total-space plurisubharmonicity. Its fiberwise quadratic margin tends to zero at nearby base points, showing why local strong convexity in theorem 4.6.8 requires a common margin on a total-space neighborhood. The example concerns this distinction between hypotheses; the general real-Finsler smoothing and connection-convergence theorems of that paper are not used.

The existence result of Liu and Wan [v1, Theorem 2.1, p. 7, LiuWanKobayashi26] that is needed here is proved directly by the tautological construction of lemma 4.6.11, proposition 4.6.12: a metric with positive curvature on the dual of the tautological line bundle gives a homogeneous squared norm that is strictly plurisubharmonic off the zero section. When the dual vector bundle is ample, such a line metric is a root of the Fubini–Study metric pulled back by a very ample embedding. The result is the strictly plurisubharmonic homogeneous function used in their regularization [v1, Lemma 3.4, pp. 13–14, LiuWanKobayashi26], and it gives corollary 4.6.13.

The construction of polar norms in 4.6.3 gives a proof of Liu and Wan's [v1, Proposition 3.5, pp. 14–16, LiuWanKobayashi26]. A Schur complement and a holomorphic correction of the second jet give local pluriharmonic supporting functions for the logarithm of the polar square. A uniform upper bound for the real Hessian of the primal squared metric gives strong convexity of the dual through its exact Legendre expression; neither real convexity of the primal metric nor uniqueness of a maximizer is assumed. The tautological construction for an ample dual and the homogeneous regularization then give the smooth replacement and the curvature obstruction of corollary 4.6.18, under the hypotheses stated there.

On Hirzebruch surfaces, the ample comparison quotient and the two prioritary points of proposition 4.6.20, lemma 4.6.21 are Liu and Wan's [v1, Proposition 4.2 and Lemma 4.3, pp. 17–20, LiuWanKobayashi26]. The facts about Hirzebruch surfaces that they need are proved directly in lemma 4.6.19, from sections along the ruling and a very ample embedding. The positive Gauduchon tests for the individual dual line bundles in proposition 4.6.22 are the divisor calculation in their proof of Theorem 1.1, p. 23. These constructions give the ample point and the test forms used in theorem 4.6.30, corollary 4.6.31.

The stability of extensions in proposition 4.6.23 is Liu and Wan's [v1, Proposition 4.4, pp. 20–21, LiuWanKobayashi26], including the equality case on $\mathbb F_0$. The proof of proposition 4.6.24 uses the exact sequences of $\operatorname {Hom}$ groups and applies to every extension, while restriction to a fiber of the ruling shows that none of them is ample. The explicit family of Dolbeault operators in proposition 4.6.25 converges to the split operator in every finite $C^ m$ norm. These stable, nonample approximations give the stable point used in the deformation argument of theorem 4.6.30.

The approximation theorem theorem 4.6.30 is Liu and Wan's [v1, Theorem 4.1, p. 17; proof, p. 22, LiuWanKobayashi26]. Its input on the stack of prioritary sheaves is Walter's [v1, Proposition 2, p. 2, WalterPrioritary93], which applies to all prioritary torsion-free sheaves of fixed rank at least two with fixed numerical Chern classes, without any semistability assumption. The required openness statements are proved directly by the family arguments in lemma 4.6.26, lemma 4.6.27, lemma 4.6.28; on a Hirzebruch surface, slope stability reduces to finitely many classes of line bundles. Parallel transport and compactness, in lemma 4.6.29, give convergence on the fixed smooth bundle and on its dual in every finite $C^ m$ norm. Combining this approximation with the Gauduchon-degree obstruction and the polar construction above gives corollary 4.6.31, which is Liu and Wan's [v1, Theorem 1.1, p. 2; proof, p. 23, LiuWanKobayashi26].

The fixed Stein hypersurface of theorem 4.6.34, corollary 4.6.35 corresponds to Liu and Wan's [v1, Proposition 5.1, pp. 24–27, LiuWanKobayashi26] for explicit algebraic bundles. A positive horizontal direction and the vertical critical hyperplane give the test on the Levi index, and a holomorphic quadratic jet makes the real Hessian nondegenerate without changing the Levi form. Separability of the relevant metric space gives countably many robust local tests, which lemma 4.6.32, lemma 4.6.33 place on a single global graph by Runge and Oka–Weil approximation. The graph is chosen before the metric, whereas the critical point that detects the failure may depend on the metric.

The dual norm of a concave neighborhood, theorem 4.6.37, is Liu and Wan's [v1, Lemma 5.2, pp. 27–32, LiuWanKobayashi26]. After a correction by a pure jet, a negative tangent plane of the boundary gives a supporting holomorphic section. Balls of uniform size tangent to the boundary from inside then express the squared dual norm as a supremum of functions with a common margin of strong real convexity, and ampleness together with homogeneous regularization gives a smooth replacement. Let $E$ be an ample bundle of rank $r$, and write $U_ E=\mathbb P(\mathcal O_ X\oplus E)\setminus \mathbb P(\mathcal O_ X)$ for the complement of the zero section in its projective completion. The argument at high levels of an exhaustion in corollary 4.6.39, which shows that $U_ E$ is not $r$-convex when $E^*$ admits no smooth complex norm with plurisubharmonic square, is their [v1, Corollary 5.3, p. 32, LiuWanKobayashi26]. Combined with the norm obstruction and the stable ample approximation, it gives corollary 4.6.40.

The case of positive mean curvature in theorem 4.6.42, corollary 4.6.44 is Liu and Wan's [v1, Proposition 5.4, pp. 32–33, LiuWanKobayashi26]. The calculation with the reciprocal of the norm gives the more general count, in which $s$ positive horizontal directions of the curvature give $(n+r-s)$-convexity of $U_ E$ for a bundle $E$ of rank $r$. For stable bundles of positive degree, the application uses the existence of Hermitian–Einstein metrics, proved by Uhlenbeck and Yau [UhlenbeckYau86] in the Kähler case and extended to Gauduchon metrics by Li and Yau [LiYau87]. The degree of definition 4.6.43 pairs the Bott–Chern first Chern class with the Gauduchon form; with the unscaled curvature $F_ h$ of a Hermitian metric $h$, the integrated trace carries the factor $2\pi n$. The resulting least convexity index three in corollary 4.6.45 is [v1, Corollary 5.5, p. 33, LiuWanKobayashi26]; the lower bound uses the dual-norm obstruction above.

The negative-bundle weight in proposition 4.6.46 is the construction of Kusakabe and Matsumura [v1, Lemma 3.2(a,b,d), pp. 20–21, KusakabeMatsumuraOka26]. The complete metric of corollary 4.6.48 uses the two exhaustion barriers in the first paragraph of their proof of [v1, Lemma 3.3, p. 21, KusakabeMatsumuraOka26]. Their jet-vanishing condition [v1, Lemma 3.4, p. 21, KusakabeMatsumuraOka26] follows here from the smooth-center multiplier-ideal formula, which gives the exact ideal and the sharp threshold for every real weight in corollary 4.6.47. In their argument the logarithmic weight supports weighted $\bar\partial $ estimates and the gluing of local sprays; here its direct Levi calculation relates negative bundle curvature to the singularity along the zero section.

Suppose that, for a holomorphic bundle $F$ of rank $r$, the complement $F^\times $ of the zero section carries a smooth positive plurisubharmonic function that is homogeneous of degree two. Taking maxima over the level spheres, proposition 4.6.52 then shows that the least convexity index of $U_ F$ is $n+r$: a negative radial derivative makes the Levi form restricted to the level set nonpositive on a complex hyperplane. Unitary-flat metrics give corollary 4.6.54. For the line bundles $E_ a$ on a Hopf manifold in example 4.6.55, restriction to an elliptic fiber and positive curvature give the least index two when $|a|<1$, whereas a homogeneous plurisubharmonic metric gives the least index $n+1$ when $|a|\geq 1$; the Gauduchon degree distinguishes the three signs, although the de Rham first Chern class vanishes. A metric with Griffiths-nonpositive curvature gives the same homogeneous function in corollary 4.6.53. For split bundles on Hopf manifolds, proposition 4.6.56 combines the weighted curvature with the exact loss of dimension under restriction to closed submanifolds in lemma 4.6.38. It gives the least indices $r+1$ and $n+r$ in the two pure regimes; in the mixed regime, only separate upper and lower bounds are obtained.

For external tensor products, theorem 4.6.50 applies the same exhaustion with one positive curvature direction from each factor. The positive mean curvatures lifted from the factors control every vector of the tensor product, including the indecomposable ones, and give corollary 4.6.51. For the tangent bundle of a surface with a nonflat Ricci-flat Kähler metric, corollary 4.12.4 combines the exhaustion instead with the directional positivity of theorem 4.12.3: this tangent bundle admits no metric whose mean curvature is positive definite, but its RC-positive metric still gives the required exhaustion.

For the logarithmic parabolic Monge–Ampère flow on Kähler manifolds, the calculation of $L^ p$ norms of the volume ratio is Jiyuan Han's [v1, Lemma 3.2, pp. 13–14, HanParabolic26]. The chain rule and Stokes computation in proposition 4.8.4 keep the additional term coming from the derivative of the Hermitian background. When the forms along the flow remain Gauduchon, convex entropies decrease (corollary 4.8.5), and varying the target probability measure gives the exact criterion at the initial time in proposition 4.8.6. These statements apply on any interval on which a smooth solution exists. Han's uniform estimates for degenerating families of Calabi–Yau manifolds, and the Green-function estimates on which they depend, are not part of this calculation.

The calculation of the total variation in proposition 4.8.8 is a direct variation of determinants on a flat torus, whose coefficient is given by the two Haar averages of lemma 4.8.7. Corollary 4.8.9 compares this growth of the distance between two solutions with the initial derivatives of the individual entropies.

The support lemma for currents carried by a smooth hypersurface, lemma 4.7.1, is the local statement used by Dinh and Sibony in [proof of Theorem 4.1, pp. 965–966, DinhSibonyRegularization04], where it is attributed to Bassanelli. The proof given here tests against a quadratic function of the normal coordinate and polarizes horizontal forms; it applies whenever $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}R$ has order zero, $R$ being the positive current supported on the hypersurface. The estimate for pullbacks in lemma 4.7.2 follows directly from the classical $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$-lemma for currents on compact Kähler manifolds, applied to a fixed closed pushforward; the current that is paired with the distributional primitive is smooth.

The smoothing of positive pluriharmonic forms with continuous coefficients in lemma 4.7.3 uses the heat equation of the Hodge Laplacian, the Kähler identities and a positive correction tending to zero. For continuous potentials and closed backgrounds on a compact Kähler manifold, Dinh and Sibony construct the product of the curvature current with a positive pluriharmonic current through an energy [Theorem 5.1, Proposition 5.2 and identity (12), pp. 967–969, DinhSibonyRegularization04]. The proofs of lemma 5.3.1, theorem 5.3.2 integrate by parts against the current of bidegree $(n-1,n-1)$ itself, so that no auxiliary power of a Kähler form is needed. They give a positive measure as product, depending jointly continuously on the factors, on any compact Hermitian manifold, for every smooth real background and continuous potential.

The descent of horizontal currents in lemma 4.7.4 follows directly from the positivity of the coefficient matrix together with the Liouville theorem for harmonic functions on the complex line, or the maximum principle on a compact connected curve. It therefore applies both to bundles with fiber $\mathbb C$ and to bundles of compact curves. On the elliptic threefold, the exact vertical trace form gives lemma 28.9.1 for positive $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$-closed currents, and its closed case gives the calculation of the Bott–Chern cone.

The mean-curvature criterion for RC-positivity and the conformal construction in lemma 4.11.2, theorem 4.11.3 correspond to Chou and Wu's [v1, Definition 5 and Lemmas 7–8, pp. 5–6, ChouWuRC26]. The scalar equation in the construction is the Gauduchon compatibility condition of proposition 4.8.1, and taking the curvature margin over a closed ball makes it uniform in both the base point and the unit fiber vector. Example 4.11.4 shows directly that the order of these two quantifiers matters, and proposition 4.11.5 gives a global obstruction to interchanging them when the determinant bundle admits a flat Hermitian metric.

The RC-positive tangent metrics on Ricci-flat surfaces in theorem 4.12.3 are Chou and Wu's [v1, Theorem 1 and Lemmas 9–15, pp. 7–12, ChouWuRC26]. The pointwise criterion is the invertibility of the anti-self-dual curvature operator. The identities behind (4.32) are Derdziński's [equations (33)–(35), p. 414, Derdzinski83], with the nonnegative Laplacian on functions used there replaced by $\operatorname {div}\nabla $ and with the same norm on endomorphisms; summing the three equations for the eigenvalues gives the coefficient $36$. The commuting curvature factors in [Lemma 2, p. 412, Derdzinski83] also show directly that a nonzero parallel operator with three distinct eigenvalues cannot occur here. This gives the required nonempty open set and a conformal tangent metric whose associated form is not closed.

The symmetrized sectional-curvature average and the integrated trace identity in lemma 4.13.1, proposition 4.13.2 are Xueyuan Wan's [v1, Lemma 2.1 and Proposition 3.3 with Lemmas 3.1–3.2, pp. 5–8, WanHermitianSectional26]. The average is proved here by complex Gaussian moments, and the connection calculation retains the exact term until Stokes' theorem removes it. The auxiliary metric $g$ is Kähler, while the tangent metric $h$ can be nonclosed. Their identity gives corollary 4.13.3 directly from the signs of its three terms. This use of the identity does not require the auxiliary Hessian equation or the canonical-bundle positivity argument of that paper. The torus and finite torus-cover statements of proposition 4.13.4, corollary 4.13.5 follow from this identity and periodic integration by parts: the symmetric connection difference vanishes, forcing the Hermitian coefficients to be constant. In dimension one, the same conclusion is the maximum principle for the logarithm of the metric coefficient.

The first-jet characterization of Griffiths positivity on projective manifolds, theorem 4.14.5, is due to Du and Xie [v1, Theorem 1.1 and Proposition 2.1, pp. 1–4, DuXieFirstJet26]; the identity in normal frames is written in the curvature convention used above. The density with two derivatives that the argument requires is proved directly in lemma 4.14.3, lemma 4.14.4, through normalized projective coordinates and Bernstein polynomials. The immersion test without twist, corollary 4.14.6, is the calculation in [v1, Section 3.1, pp. 4–5, DuXieFirstJet26]. The positive measure in the dual alternative lives on the sphere bundle of the dual bundle, so the condition that it annihilates all metrics is different from Gauduchon duality on the base.

The double cover in section 4.15, branched over $2q$ points, gives an explicit model with a computable space of sections in which a strictly positive line bundle fails the test without twist. The decomposition of sections into even and odd parts and the projective embedding are proved explicitly. A root of a very ample Fubini–Study metric gives a separately defined weighted family of tests, and proposition 4.15.2 identifies its first successful level $j=q-1$, together with obstructions by one or two atoms at the lower levels. The original criterion with a fixed very ample twist $m$ follows the subsequence $j=qm$, so this calculation does not determine its first successful twist. The obstructions at a single level should also be distinguished from an adjoint measure annihilating every smooth metric.

34.4 Bounded products and extended measures

The Bedford–Taylor product of bounded potentials is local even when the background is not closed: expanding against smooth local corrections, as Kołodziej and Nguyen [Section 1, KN15] and Boucksom, Guedj and Lu [Section 2.1, BGL25] do, gives proposition 1.7.1. Restricting the products of the truncations to the sets where the potential exceeds the truncation level then defines the extended measure of theorem 6.1.2, by the mechanism of [Proposition 2.1, BGL25]. Finiteness becomes decisive when these restrictions are removed in the category of currents, and [Theorem 2.2, BGL25] uses a uniform bound on the variation for this purpose. An infinite extended positive measure is still given by the local construction, but it is not a global current of order zero.

The independence of the bounded mass property from the metric, and the reduction to smooth potentials, in proposition 6.5.2 recover the comparison made after [Definition 1.16, BGL25]; decreasing regularization with exact curvature bounds gives the passage from smooth to bounded potentials. The Gauduchon trace bound and the cancellation of the exact top-degree wedge product used in proposition 7.1.1 also appear in Guedj and Lu's [Proposition 3.3(i), (vi), GL22], and the estimate in terms of the range of the potential keeps their polynomial dependence on the oscillation.

The local mixed Monge–Ampère inequality, theorem 1.6.4, is Dinew's [Theorem 1.2, Dinew09], for locally bounded plurisubharmonic functions whose Monge–Ampère measures dominate nonnegative measurable densities with respect to Lebesgue measure. Its extension to smooth backgrounds is proved in section 1.7 and used in section 14.17.1. Freezing the coefficients of the two smooth backgrounds gives lemma 1.7.6 and the trace inequalities (14.12) and lemma 6.4.2, the latter keeping the mass of the linear trace on the pole set. The same argument also recovers the inequality for smooth volume forms used by Nguyen [Lemma 1.9, Nguyen16].

34.5 Envelopes and comparison

Narzillaev studies weighted extremal functions of a compact set $K\subset \mathbb C^ n$ with a bounded weight $\psi $ on $K$ and logarithmic growth $\delta \log ^+\lVert z\rVert $, $\delta >0$. The concavity in the growth parameter and the alternatives for regular contact in theorem 13.1.11 are stated in [v1, Lemma 3.1 and Theorem 1.1, p. 6, NarzillaevWeighted26]; the proof given here also shows directly, by scaling, that the set of parameters at which the unregularized envelope attains the obstacle on $K$ is empty or a ray. Lemma 13.1.7, Proposition 13.1.8 derive the correspondence with compact envelopes on projective space, the finiteness of the envelope and its almost-everywhere order from the compactness and extension results proved earlier in the book. In the book's convention, the growth $\delta \log ^+\lVert z\rVert $ corresponds to the projective background $(\delta /2)\omega _{\mathrm{FS}}$ and to the obstacle $\psi -\delta \log (1+\lVert z\rVert ^2)/2$; Narzillaev's operator $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}=\mathrm i\partial \bar\partial /2$ is $\pi $ times the book's. None of the theorems of that preprint on products, plurithinness or weighted counterexamples is used in these proofs.

For the zero obstacle, corollary 13.1.10 gives the case of compact sets of the classical fact, recalled in [v1, Section 2, equation (4), p. 4, NarzillaevWeighted26], that removing a pluripolar set does not change the extremal function. For bounded weights, the proof uses the logarithmic pole barrier of lemma 13.1.9. Proposition 1.19.6 carries out the corresponding argument for compact sets in an arbitrary smooth big background, using the correction of pointwise competitors in lemma 1.19.5.

The estimates for increments in the growth parameter in lemma 13.1.12 are [v1, Lemma 3.1, p. 6, NarzillaevWeighted26], and at a point of regular contact proposition 13.1.13, corollary 13.1.14 recover [v1, Proposition 3.2, p. 7, NarzillaevWeighted26]. The proof given here keeps the estimates for the initial and the later slopes at every point of $\mathbb C^ n$ and every positive growth parameter, including the uniform error for large increments. The representation by probability measures and the attained derivative in theorem 13.1.15, theorem 13.1.16 come from [v1, Theorems 3.3–3.4, pp. 8–9, NarzillaevWeighted26]; the same argument with the finite envelope extends them to every point and every positive parameter. The passage to weak limits of these probability measures uses the continuity of the weight. The joint response to changes of the weight and of the growth, and the criteria for differentiability, in lemma 13.1.17, proposition 13.1.18, corollary 13.1.19, corollary 13.1.20, follow directly from the attained representation and the compactness of its set of active pairs. Example 13.1.21 gives an explicit corner in the dependence on the weight, on the closed unit disk.

The relative extremal function of definition 1.6.3 follows the convention for compact sets in [v1, Section 2, equation (8), p. 5, NarzillaevWeighted26]. Lemma 13.1.22, which shows that the relative extremal function equals $-1$ at a point exactly when the global extremal function vanishes there, is proved by an explicit logarithmic gluing and the pole barrier above. Lemma 13.1.24, Theorem 13.1.25 give [v1, Lemma 4.1 and Theorem 4.2, p. 10, NarzillaevWeighted26], including the local strictness hypothesis $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\Psi \geq c\omega _0$ near the point, with $\omega _0=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\lVert z\rVert ^2$, a global weight $\Psi $ in the given growth class extending the weight on $K$, and the closed compact pieces inside the ball. Narzillaev's operator and Euclidean form are both $\pi $ times those of the book, so the coefficient $c$ and the bound for the defect are unchanged. The pointwise consequence for the Hessian in corollary 13.1.26 is the observation following that theorem on p. 10.

The planar disks and the quantitative barrier of lemma 13.1.27 come from [v1, Lemma 6.1, pp. 12–13, NarzillaevWeighted26]. Proposition 13.1.28 proves part of the product construction in [v1, Lemma 6.2, pp. 13–14, NarzillaevWeighted26], namely the contact defect along the exceptional fiber and the failure of plurithinness at the interior point specified there. The logarithmic series is constructed by convergence of canonical representatives on compact sets, and an explicit pole barrier produces competitors that satisfy the obstacle everywhere. The proof needs neither a general product formula nor a classification at all boundary points.

The formula on normal fibers for the pluricomplex Green function with a divisorial pole, proposition 1.19.8, is the case of the unit ball of Dao, Nguyen and Tran's [v1, Proposition 4.2, p. 25, DNTGreen26], with logarithms normalized as $\log |\zeta |^2$ in the normal coordinate $\zeta $; the proof uses the maximum principle in one variable. The bounded minorants of proposition 1.19.9 converge at every interior point, including the points of the divisor, with an explicit error bound. This is a special model for the approximation by bounded envelopes in [Theorem 1.7, pp. 5–6, DNTGreen26], whose general result assumes an inward deformation in a sector and uses B-regularity for continuity. The calculation of boundary cluster values in example 1.19.10 concerns the trace of the divisor on the boundary, whereas that theorem asserts continuity inside the domain.

Contact sets connect the order of potentials with the order of their Monge–Ampère measures. For semipositive big backgrounds, theorem 13.1.4 is due to Alehyane, Lu and Salouf [Theorem 2.2, ALS26]; the passage from bounded to unbounded potentials in the book removes the bigness assumption and a uniform mass assumption. For a general big background, Boucksom, Guedj and Lu [Theorem 2.9 and Proposition 2.11, BGL25] prove that the measure of the envelope is concentrated on the contact set under bounded mass, and local replacement together with a strict singular barrier gives theorem 14.7.4 without that assumption.

The measure estimate for envelope fixed points, proposition 13.1.6, is [Proposition 2.12, ALS26]. Let $\theta $ be smooth and semipositive, and let $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ satisfy $P_\theta [\varphi ]=\varphi $ for the ordinary envelope of definition 1.19.4. The proof given here uses the identity $P_\theta (\varphi +1,0)=\varphi $, where $P_\theta (\varphi +1,0)$ is the largest $\theta $-plurisubharmonic function lying below $\min (\varphi +1,0)$ (section 1.19); contact with the first obstacle occurs only on the pluripolar set $\{ \varphi =-\infty \} $, so only contact with zero contributes to the measure. For bounded potentials, the proof of the domination principle by envelopes, theorem 13.2.2, is due to Guedj and Lu [Proposition 2.8, GL22].

Difference envelopes give a relative class in which singular potentials can still be compared. The construction appears for semipositive big backgrounds in [Proposition 2.4 and Lemma 2.5, ALS26] and for arbitrary big backgrounds in Xuan Li's [Section 2.1, XL26]; the estimate for sublevel sets shows that the envelope is not identically $-\infty $ as soon as its family of competitors is nonempty. The relative envelope class of definition 13.4.1 follows [Definition 3.2, ALS26], and the proof of its closure under convex stretching, lemma 13.4.3, is the argument of [Proposition 3.5, ALS26].

The condition (13.26) on the reference and its consequence, positivity of the mass of each individual potential in the class, appear in [Proposition 2.10 and the discussion after Definition 2.13, ALS26]; that paper does not assume bounded mass for its equations with $L^ p$ data in a fixed ordinary type. This positivity for individual potentials must be distinguished from a positive infimum of the mass over the whole ordinary singularity type. The mechanism giving the lower bound for the mass in theorem 13.5.3 is that of [Proposition 3.8, ALS26].

For lower semicontinuous obstacles the contact argument requires an approximation. The proof of proposition 13.1.2 follows Guedj and Lu [Theorem 2.3, GL22] and uses the Bedford–Taylor convergence theorem for increasing bounded sequences [Theorem 7.4, BT82]; smooth local corrections and an expansion against the remaining background extend it to nonclosed backgrounds.

The modified comparison principle, theorem 10.2.1, is due to Kołodziej and Nguyen [Theorem 0.2, KN15]. The proof given here follows the finite induction of Guedj and Lu [Theorem 1.5, GL22], with larger dimensional constants.

34.6 Smooth and continuous equations

On a Hermitian manifold, the smooth Monge–Ampère equation determines a normalization constant together with the potential. For $n\geq 2$, the existence theorem theorem 9.1.1 is Tosatti and Weinkove's [Corollary 1, TW10]. Their equation, for a smooth function $F$ prescribing the logarithm of the density, is written with $\mathrm i\partial \bar\partial \varphi $; the substitution $u=2\pi \varphi $ gives the convention of this book, and a prescribed smooth volume form $\mathrm dV$ is absorbed by replacing $F$ with $F+\log (\mathrm dV/\omega ^ n)$. The concavity argument with Gauduchon metrics for the normalization constant in theorem 9.7.1 also appears in Collins's [Theorem 2.13, Collins21].

The proof of the uniform estimate for the potential in chapter 9 by weighted integration follows Cherrier and Tosatti–Weinkove [Cherrier87, TW10]: a finite recurrence absorbs the torsion terms before the Sobolev iteration. The calculation of the torsion terms in the second-order estimate of chapter 9 goes back to Cherrier; its form with completed squares and the maximum-principle argument follow [Sections 3 and 9, TW15].

The interpolation in lemma 9.4.3 is the mechanism of Jiaogen Zhang and Xi Zhang's [v1, Proposition 4.1 and its proof, p. 16, ZhangHessianInterpolation26]. The fixed scalar operator is uniformly elliptic for a Hermitian metric as well, so the linear implication here does not require closedness. Their nonlinear Hessian application assumes a Kähler metric and a small product of its negative bisectional-curvature bound with the solution's oscillation. Its argument for data close to one in $L^1$ uses a stability estimate with $q>n/k$ and a uniform $L^ q$ bound; the smallness threshold depends on that bound. The interpolation lemma supplies neither this nonlinear trace estimate nor an arbitrary-$L^1$ stability criterion.

Prescribing the volume form of a Gauduchon metric leads to a different equation, which contains first-order torsion terms. The existence theorem theorem 9.9.1 is due to Székelyhidi, Tosatti and Weinkove [Theorem 1.4, STW17], again with the potential multiplied by $2\pi $. The bound for the potential in section 9.10 follows Székelyhidi's [Propositions 10–11 and Remark 12, Sze18], specialized to the determinant of the Gauduchon equation, and the second-order argument in section 9.13 follows [Section 3, STW17]. The identities (9.30), taken from [Section 4, STW17], explain the structure of the first-order perturbation.

The gradient estimate combines a blow-up argument with a Liouville theorem. In (9.52), where $A_ j$ are the rescaling factors of the blow-up, the first-order term is kept before rescaling and tends to zero at the rate $A_ j^{-1}$, as in [Section 6, Sze18]. The Euclidean argument in section 9.14 specializes [Theorem 20, Sze18] to the Gauduchon cone. Once the equation is uniformly elliptic, lemma 9.16.1 uses the Evans–Krylov theorem for variable coefficients of Tosatti, Wang, Weinkove and Yang [Definition 3.2 and Theorem 3.4, TWWY15].

For densities in $L^ p$, the existence of continuous solutions of the equation with a normalization constant (theorem 10.1.1) and of the exponential equation (10.1) is due respectively to Kołodziej and Nguyen [Theorem 0.1, KN15] and to Nguyen [Theorem 0.1, Nguyen16]. Their operator is twice the one used here: for the exponent $\lambda >0$ in (10.1), the conversion is $u=2\varphi $, where $\varphi $ is their solution, and the exponent in their equation is $2\lambda $. These results require neither bounded mass nor positive lower volume. Compactness comes from local capacity estimates: the arguments of [Section 5, KN15] and [Section 6, Dinew16] lead to theorem 10.3.4, which is the statement of [Corollary 5.10, KN15]. A common bound for the densities is essential here; the $L^1$ compactness of normalized potentials alone does not suffice.

For measures satisfying a power bound in capacity, Kołodziej and Nguyen [Proposition 2.4, pp. 2954–2956, KNHolder18] give stability measured against the Monge–Ampère measure of the solution. The thin-sublevel estimate and backwards iteration of lemma 10.3.1, lemma 10.3.2 follow the capacity argument in [v2, Theorem 5.3 and Lemma 5.4, pp. 10–12, KN15], using their modified comparison theorem [v2, Theorem 2.3, pp. 5–6, KN15]. The geometric iteration in the book covers every power $1+\alpha $, $\alpha >0$, including the total-capacity endpoint. Choosing the sublevel height proportional to the cube of the comparison reserve gives the exponent $\alpha /[3n(1+\alpha )+\alpha ]$ in proposition 10.3.5; the square-capacity case has exponent $1/(6n+1)$. The measure can be singular. The preprint [v2, p. 1, KN15] uses $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}=\mathrm i\partial \bar\partial /\pi $, twice our operator; its backgrounds become $2\omega $ with the same potentials, and its top products acquire the factor $2^ n$.

The threshold equation in theorem 14.32.1 combines this iteration with bounded-density solvability and the full Gauduchon formula. Local $L^1$ compactness uses Demailly's Propositions 4.21 and 5.9 [Chapter I, pp. 36–37 and 40, DemCADG]. The weighted local product estimate is Nguyen's [v1, Proposition 1.1, pp. 5–6, Nguyen16]; uniform continuity of bounded products is Bedford and Taylor's [Corollary 2.8 and Theorem 2.10, p. 10, BT82]. For a Hermitian background, the local closed product must still be expanded with every smooth correction factor, as in proposition 1.7.1. The logarithmic construction in theorem 14.32.2 uses the exponential volume–capacity estimate (1.25) and gives a datum outside every higher $L^ p$ class with an exact continuous threshold solution. The added capacity hypothesis leaves the general root-tail and exact-equation questions open.

For a continuous solution whose measure is dominated by a Hölder reference, theorem 14.4.2 combines the weighted Chern estimate with the square-capacity stability. The coordinated transform parameter and comparison reserve keep the minimizing radii comparable to the smoothing radius. The resulting exponent is sufficient for the range theorem; no optimal exponent is asserted. The argument assumes continuity of the solution.

The smoothness theorem theorem 10.5.2 is the strictly positive, globally smooth-data case of Badiane, Lu and Zeriahi's [v1, Theorem 1.1, p. 2; Section 4.2, p. 17, BLZBoundedSmooth26]. Their auxiliary second-order estimate is Proposition 4.1, pp. 13–17. The square of the prescribed potential in lemma 10.5.1 absorbs the derivatives of the frozen density, and bounded exponential comparison identifies the uniform limit with the original solution. The argument then uses the concave uniformly elliptic extension of the logarithmic determinant and the Evans–Krylov theorem for variable coefficients [Definition 3.2, Theorem 3.4, and the paragraph following it, TWWY15]. Only the strictly positive Hermitian background and global smooth positive density are treated here; the primary paper also considers degenerate backgrounds and data with a singular regular locus.

The exponential term gives a comparison principle even when the densities have no positive lower bound; lemma 10.4.2 recovers [Lemma 2.3, Nguyen16]. Letting the exponent tend to zero then identifies the normalization constant through (10.19), as in [Corollary 0.2, Nguyen16]. This proves uniqueness of the constant, but not uniqueness of the limiting potential. The weighted mean (10.16), taken from [Proposition 2.5, Nguyen16], controls the normalization; here its lower bound follows directly from the estimate for sublevel sets.

Equations with prescribed singularities require relative envelopes and separate assumptions on the reference potential and on the ambient metric. The results of Alehyane–Lu–Salouf and of Xuan Li [ALS26, XL26] are therefore treated separately in chapter 14, chapter 31, chapter 32; their sources are discussed in section 34.10.

For fully nonlinear concave equations, the uniform estimate under a quantitative subsolution condition is due to Guo and Phong [v1, Theorem 3.1 and its proof, pp. 8–11, GP24]. The strict asymptotic gap of an admissible reference, the matrix bound, the linear stability estimate and the uniform compactness in section 10.8 follow Guo and Song [v1, Lemma 2.1, Proposition 2.8 and Lemma 2.9, pp. 4–11, GS26]. In the book, the zero-order estimate uses the real contact-set construction of proposition 9.10.1, and theorem 10.3.3 bounds both the auxiliary potential and its normalization constant; with these inputs the constants do not depend on a positive lower bound for the right-hand sides. Writing the potential of these sources as $u/(2\pi )$ converts their convention for smooth curvature forms into the normalization of this book.

The gradient estimate in section 10.9 is Guo and Song's [v1, Theorem 1.1 and Proposition 3.1 with Lemmas 3.2–3.6, pp. 11–17, GS26]; the earlier alternative for convex level sets is Székelyhidi's [arXiv v3, Propositions 5–6, pp. 5–7, Sze18]. 34.7 Uniqueness and selection

When the exponent is zero, uniqueness of the normalization constant leaves open the separate question of uniqueness of the potential. Degeneration of the density affects comparison, whereas the family of equations with positive exponent can select a particular solution of the critical equation. The argument by concentration of mass for densities restricted to a set, around (11.38), follows Lu, Phung and Tô [proof of Theorem 3.7 and Remark 3.8, LPT20]. The exterior-ball argument for (11.19) instead propagates the maximum, following Bardi and Goffi [Proposition 2.4 and Theorem 2.5 in the cited preprint, BG18].

The uniqueness theorem for bounded potentials, theorem 11.4.1, concerns a continuous nonnegative density whose positivity set is connected and dense, and is proved with energy estimates on level sets and auxiliary test potentials. 34.9 Volumes under mass bounds

For a nonclosed background, the mass of a single potential does not determine the masses in its singularity type. The lower and upper volumes $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)$ and $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)$ of definition 13.3.1, the infimum and the supremum of the mass over the ordinary singularity type of $u$, record the information that a single mass loses. Under bounded mass, comparison of these volumes controls envelopes and defines a topology on models of positive lower volume. On curves, polar curvature describes the boundary where the lower volume vanishes.

34.9.1 Singularity volumes and relative classes

The volumes used in section 30.1 are those of Boucksom, Guedj and Lu [Definition 3.1, BGL25], expressed in terms of potentials. For closed backgrounds the two volumes coincide, because the non-pluripolar mass is invariant under bounded changes of the potential, as recalled in [Example 3.2, BGL25]. In the big case, the monotonicity theorem theorem 30.1.1 is [Proposition 3.7, BGL25], and the argument for lower volumes in proposition 30.1.2 is Li's [Proposition 2.5, XL26].

The order test in 30.8.6 uses the projective valuative characterization of Darvas and Xia [Theorem 2.13 and Proposition 2.30, DX22] in a common closed Kähler background. Their Lelong convention differs from the divisor coefficient used here by a factor of two, which leaves equality of order profiles unchanged. The countable divisorial domination and envelope arguments are those of lemma 1.17.4, corollary 16.2.8; they identify the whole ordinary type before its mass is computed. The upper bound for the closed top mass uses Witt Nyström's monotonicity theorem [WN19], while the lower bound follows directly from local bounded truncations. Expansion with smooth corrections, as in [Section 2.1, BGL25], then gives the nonclosed mass throughout this type. This computation applies to the prescribed summable hyperplane profile with positive smooth residual; it does not assert mass invariance for arbitrary unbounded types in a pluriclosed background.

The relative full-mass class of definition 30.2.3 is [Definition 3.2, XL26]. The stretching criterion lemma 30.2.1 is [Lemma 3.1, XL26]; the proof given here keeps track of the deficit of upper volumes and chooses a representative whose mass is close to its upper volume. The case of zero deficit gives theorem 30.2.4, and a uniform bound for Lelong numbers turns the convex comparisons into the estimate of proposition 30.2.2.

The domination principle theorem 30.3.1 is [Proposition 3.2, XL26]; its proof uses that the reference has positive lower volume, but not that it is an envelope fixed point. The estimate for relative obstacles, lemma 30.3.3, is [Proposition 3.3, XL26]. Together they give the identity for upper volumes in [Lemma 3.2, XL26] and the identity for lower volumes in [Corollary 3.3, XL26], both stated in theorem 30.3.4. For $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, the proof first shows that the increasing limit of the obstacle envelopes has the ordinary singularity type of $P_\theta [u]$, so that domination can be applied before the equality of volumes is established. The resulting characterization of full mass gives the volume criterion in proposition 30.8.2.

When the pole set is compact of Hausdorff dimension zero and the lower volume is positive, theorem 30.3.5 shows that ordinary projection preserves the whole residual measure. Bounded mass gives uniform bounds for the traces of all local closed powers of the ordinary rooftop envelopes. Away from the pole set, convergence in capacity of bounded functions identifies the limits, and the Hausdorff estimate removes all mass of the lower-degree coefficients on the pole set; hence the lower-degree global pairings preserve the full total mass. Since the rooftop envelopes all have the same residual measure, positivity together with the local order of residuals forces the model to have this residual as well. The argument also identifies the weak limit of the full Monge–Ampère measures of these rooftop envelopes. The sources of the local inputs are given in section 34.1.

The construction of small stretches, lemma 30.4.1, is [Proposition 3.1, XL26], and its bound for mixed masses gives an explicit uniform scale of stretching. The additive estimate for lower volumes in proposition 30.4.4 is the case of smooth perturbations of [Proposition 3.3(ii), BGL25], with the mixed bound of [Lemma 2.4, BGL25] written in terms of potentials. The strict barrier gives the multiplicative comparison at positive lower volume, as in [Proposition 3.5(ii), Step 1, XL26]. Consequently, corollary 30.5.4 gives continuity of the lower volume of every fixed potential as the background varies, while continuity of the upper volume and of the ordinary envelope still requires positive lower volume.

The continuity along increasing sequences, theorem 30.5.1, is [Proposition 3.5(i), XL26]: contact identifies the limit of the envelopes and its upper volume, and the deficit estimate then shows that the approximating lower volumes are positive. The continuity along decreasing sequences of models, theorem 30.5.2, is [Proposition 3.5(ii), XL26], including its conclusion when the lower volume of the limit vanishes. The uniform small stretch shows that a positive lower bound along the models forces their limit to have positive lower volume, and strict corrections and tail envelopes reduce the variation of the background to the argument for a fixed background. Li states the result for perturbations $\theta +\varepsilon _ j\omega $ with $\varepsilon _ j\downarrow 0$, whereas the proof here treats arbitrary smooth backgrounds that decrease uniformly in the order of forms. The criterion for positive lower volume, proposition 30.5.3, is [Proposition 3.5(iii), XL26]. The deficit bound gives uniform convergence of Lelong numbers on each fixed compact model, and the envelopes of the restricted analytic approximants give the exact traces of corollary 30.5.5.

For semipositive backgrounds, proposition 30.4.3 is the forward direction of [Proposition 2.11, ALS26], deduced here from the strict barrier. This direction requires neither the identity for ordinary envelopes nor positivity of the lower mass $v_-(\theta )$ of definition 6.5.1; that assumption is used for the converse. Applied to an envelope fixed point, the proposition compares Li's condition (30.10) with the condition (13.26) of Alehyane, Lu and Salouf.

34.9.2 Model topology and analytic approximation

The comparison function $d_\theta $ of definition 30.6.1, defined through the upper volumes of two potentials and of their maximum, is introduced in [Section 4.2, XL26]. That it separates models follows from the characterization by envelopes. The normalized stretching argument of [Lemma 4.2, XL26] gives the quantitative $L^1$ bound in proposition 30.6.3, and the three-point estimate (30.14) passes convergence from a subsequence to the whole Cauchy sequence. The construction by maxima of tails in theorem 30.6.5 follows [Theorem 4.3, XL26], with a fixed $\delta >0$ and the non-strict lower bound $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)\geq \delta $. The strict bound $>\delta $ used in version 1 of Li's preprint is not preserved under limits of Cauchy sequences, as example 30.6.6 shows. Li explicitly allows $d_\theta $ to violate the triangle inequality, and example 30.11.1 gives an example of such a failure with intersecting divisors.

The criterion for finite rooftop envelopes, lemma 30.7.1, is [Lemma 4.1, XL26]: after choosing representatives whose masses are close to their upper volumes, a collapse would contradict the lower volume of the common upper bound. Li's argument with softened stretches [Proposition 4.4, XL26] gives proposition 30.7.2; only the limiting potential needs positive lower volume, and the same comparison gives convergence of the lower volumes of the rooftop envelopes. The two stretching scales in [Theorem 4.4, XL26] produce the monotone bounds of theorem 30.7.3. Since finite rooftop envelopes are again normalized models, continuity along decreasing models passes their volume bounds to the infinite tails; monotone convergence in capacity then applies, and the subsequence criterion gives convergence of the original sequence.

The results on analytic approximation in section 30.8 combine this continuity of volumes with the limit of the dyadic multiplier-ideal envelopes. Mixing with a strict analytic potential, with rational coefficients, removes the curvature error in any smooth big background, and two comparisons of backgrounds then give the exact excess of upper volume in (30.23). Conversely, stretching and concavity of the multiplier-ideal envelope show that its fixed points are closed under the upper-volume comparison at positive lower volume. This proves the analytic criterion theorem 30.8.4 and the Cauchy completeness inherited there. The boundary where the lower volume vanishes in higher dimension, and positivity criteria in terms of ideals, are the separate questions of 30.8.8.

The comparisons of backgrounds in section 30.9 follow from the same arguments with volumes and envelopes. Transport by a smooth gauge preserves the competitor currents, and ordinary projection corrects the zero obstacle; the explicit scale of small stretches makes the strict barrier uniform on sets of models whose lower volumes are bounded below by a positive constant. This yields (30.25) and extends the analytic criterion to positive background errors that tend to zero. These statements concern singularity volumes and canonical models; they neither identify the mass of the unregularized envelope with a gauge invariant nor assert convergence of arbitrary representatives.

The two-sided transport in section 30.10 mixes the original model with a strict analytic potential. A common upper background bounds both this transport and the maximum of two transported models, and the resulting volume bounds give uniform comparison, convergence of the transported reference, and equivalence of the limiting comparisons obtained with different strict corrections. The estimate for nearby backgrounds and the three-point modulus then reduce the analytic criterion of that section to theorem 30.9.4.

The complete metric in section 30.11 is obtained by the standard construction with finite dyadic chains, carried out directly from the three-point modulus. Stretching first gives continuity of the lower volume, and the reciprocal of the lower volume controls the boundary where it vanishes; the statement about the analytic completion then follows from the approximation criterion above. This metric induces the topology of the upper-volume comparison; it does not turn the comparison function itself into a metric.

In dimension $n\geq 2$, polar curvature on all modifications still does not detect all ordinary models. The Cantor construction of example 30.12.7 gives the curve potentials of lemma 16.6.67; their maximum has vanishing first polar currents, but its truncations keep a positive top-degree mass $M$ near the common pole set. A rank bound for smooth maxima gives the construction in every dimension. By proposition 16.6.69, corollary 16.6.70, every compact Kähler manifold, with Kähler form $\omega $ and $A=\int _ X\omega ^ n$, carries a complete ordered interval of diffuse models $p_ t$ with volumes $A-Mt^ n$ and distances $M|s^ n-t^ n|$ between $p_ s$ and $p_ t$, and every choice of the first polar comparison collapses this interval. Hence the closure of the analytic models for the polar comparison contains models that are not ideal. The identities for invariant mass extend these formulas to the setting of corollary 16.6.72.

For an arbitrary Hermitian background with bounded mass and positive lower mass, the mass bounds for representatives give proposition 16.6.75. This applies to every Hermitian metric on a compact complex surface, by the existence of Gauduchon metrics, the independence of the bounded mass property from the metric (proposition 6.5.2) and corollary 26.18.5. Preservation of residuals and additivity of volumes then give corollary 16.6.76 for all parameters in the strict range. The upper volume along this interval is strictly decreasing and Lipschitz, although in general the length of the interval is not given by the power formula valid for closed backgrounds, and all first polar coordinates still vanish. In proposition 16.6.77, two cutoffs with disjoint supports have equal total residual masses and vanishing first polar data, yet their maximum is zero and their distance is positive; in backgrounds with invariant mass, both singularity volumes coincide as well. These scalar data therefore cannot recover the spatial distribution of the residual mass.

A direct calculation with maxima in lemma 16.6.68 identifies the full residual measure as the tensor product of the measures on the curve factors, multiplied by the product of the positive weights; for a smooth maximum the coefficient reduces to the volume of a simplex. In proposition 16.6.78, a common cutoff and a uniform curvature bound give a compact family of models indexed by a box of positive weights. Invariance under projection identifies the comparison distance in this family, up to a fixed scale, with the Lebesgue measure of the symmetric difference of coordinate rectangles. The subfamilies on which the product of the weights is constant are complete and have real dimension $n-1$, although their members have the same full residual measure on $X$, the same two singularity volumes, the same multiplier ideals and the same first polar currents. The construction takes place in a background with invariant mass and concerns the residual measure on $X$ itself; it does not identify the full residual products on every modification. The problem of describing the complete topology remains open.

34.9.3 Curves and zero-volume limits

The inverse maximum principle in the plane, lemma 1.6.7, is the two-dimensional case of the result stated by Brezis and Ponce [Theorem 3.1, BP04], who attribute it to Dupaigne and Ponce; the proof given here uses harmonic barriers on shrinking neighborhoods of a compact polar set. Applied to differences of potentials, it shows that the polar curvature measures are ordered as the potentials are, and subtracting a common Green potential gives the formula (1.14) for the maximum. Since these local facts are general, they are proved in chapter 1.

The classification of models on curves in section 30.12 combines this order of polar measures with the Green representation of section 19.3. Every prescribed polar measure gives an ordinary model equivalent to its Green potential, and the characterization of envelopes of positive mass, together with uniqueness of harmonic functions at zero mass, identifies every ordinary envelope with such a model. The formula for the maximum then turns the upper-volume comparison into the total variation of measures, and completeness, geodesics and the nearest ideal model follow from the corresponding operations on measures. The exact defect of analytic approximation is the nonatomic polar mass, which for the Cantor example is the full curvature mass.

For a finite map of curves, the fiber maximum in 1.22.10 follows from the local branched coordinates and the removable-singularity theorem for subharmonic functions. The weighted trace in 1.22.11 includes the local degrees at atoms; its agreement with current pullback follows from the Riesz representation on a disk and the Poincaré–Lelong formula. These elementary operations give the envelope identities of 16.6.53. Applying the curve classification gives the degree-scaled metric embedding and the averaging retraction of 16.6.54. The component factorization then gives the complete subspace of models descending to the original base in 16.6.55. The cubic example 16.6.56 distinguishes this averaging from the nearest ideal retraction.

The continuity statement for curves, theorem 30.12.4, uses the theorem on convergence in capacity at positive volume in the interior. At zero mass the polar curvature is the whole current, which gives convergence of the currents in total variation. The local estimate lemma 1.6.6, which follows from the Green representation on a disk, the mean-value estimate for harmonic functions and the weighted Chern–Levine–Nirenberg inequality, gives convergence in capacity at this boundary. Moving a pole distinguishes this topology from that of total variation, while the approximation by finitely many atoms in theorem 16.4.2 distinguishes weak convergence of the curvature currents from convergence in capacity.

The correspondences between backgrounds in section 30.13 follow from the same classification by polar measures, which smooth gauges extend to every smooth background of positive area on a curve. Changes of background with equal area preserve total variation, whereas rescaling the area gives homotheties compatible with extraction of atoms and hence with ideal projection. These correspondences are realized by a smooth gauge, obtained from the scalar Poisson equation, followed by ordinary projection. Comparing the new obstacle with its maximum and minimum gives a uniform bound, which yields continuity in $L^1$ and in capacity up to the boundary of zero mass. Rationality is needed only for the assertion that analytic singularities are preserved under rescaling of the area, because analytic singularities in this book have rational coefficients. At zero area, positivity and Stokes' theorem leave a single normalized potential. The general convergence statement theorem 1.8.15, proved in chapter 1, follows from compactness of normalized potentials, uniqueness of harmonic functions and the local estimate relating total variation and capacity. It also gives the collapse of the model representatives as the unscaled metric diameters tend to zero.

34.10 Equations with prescribed singularities

Relative envelopes allow the solution of an equation to keep prescribed singularities while the measure on the right-hand side changes. Equations with $L^ p$ data in a prescribed ordinary type, equations with general measures, and equations under mass bounds on the metric use this framework in different ways. They differ both in the estimates available and in the singularity type of the solution obtained.

34.10.1 Equations of prescribed ordinary type

The equations of prescribed ordinary type in theorem 14.11.1 are [Theorems 5.4 and 5.7, ALS26]. Replacing their reference volume $\omega ^ n$ by $\mathrm{d}V$ multiplies the density by a smooth positive function and preserves its $L^ p$ class, and a change of the normalization of the complex Hessian by a positive constant is absorbed by rescaling both the potential and the exponent.

The two constructions of [Lemmas 5.5–5.6, ALS26] are the relative estimate theorem 14.11.3 and the rooftop supersolution lemma 14.11.4; together they keep the solution in the ordinary type of the reference. For a big background, the argument with bounded subsolutions of Guedj and Lu [Lemma 3.3 and Theorem 3.4, GL23E] gives lemma 14.2.1, theorem 14.2.2.

The trace calculation with a barrier in section 14.18 follows Guyett and Mc Gowan [v1, Proposition 3.2, pp. 7–12, GM26]. Their geometric setting is a small crepant resolution, in Fujiki's class $\mathcal C$, of a normal projective Calabi–Yau variety, and their abstract Theorem 3.1 treats a closed semipositive big background with an upper bound for the mass. In the book, the arguments with bounded subsolutions and exponential comparison of lemma 14.2.1, theorem 14.2.2 give uniform bounds for the potentials and the scalars when the density is a fixed smooth positive function. The trace argument keeps the torsion of the perturbation and also allows a fixed nonzero Chern–Ricci form. Its conclusion is convergence of the potentials along subsequences, in a semipositive background with an explicit strict analytic barrier. The assertions of that paper about metric completions and conifold contractions are not part of this argument.

The bundle and the contraction in 14.18.1 form the standard model of a node used in [v1, Equation (2.1), p. 4, and Section 4, pp. 15–16, GM26]. The logarithmic curvature identity (14.54) gives a strict barrier for this model directly, and a cutoff in a positive collar makes it global. This proves the convergence of the potentials and the assertion about the area of the exceptional curves for these local resolutions, without using a classification of small resolutions or a theorem on metric completions. Since the canonical bundle is torsion, its local holomorphic trivializations give the Chern–Ricci-flat density.

The equation on the singular space, (14.56), is obtained through the general correspondence of theorem A.2.1 between potentials on a resolution and potentials on the singular space defined by disk inequalities. For closed backgrounds, the identification in [v1, Lemma 3.1 and the proof of Corollary 1.1, pp. 13–14, GM26] uses the equation on normal spaces and the estimate for adapted measures of Eyssidieux, Guedj and Zeriahi [arXiv v2, Theorem 6.3 and Lemma 6.4, pp. 28–29, EGZ09]. In the standard model of a node, (14.55) gives in lemma 14.18.6 the exact range $p<3$ of integrability and the weak $L^3$ endpoint, for every smooth positive density on the resolution. The bounded energy identity already used in theorem A.6.2 proves theorem 14.18.7, using smooth strict positivity only away from the exceptional curves.

The arguments for the metric completion and the distance in lemma 14.18.10, proposition 14.18.11 are those of [v1, Lemma 5.2, pp. 23–24, and Proposition 5.1, pp. 24–26, GM26]. Compact collars in the regular part separate the points added in the completion, and the first and last visits of a path to the finitely many contracting regions give a distance error controlled by these regions; uniform convergence of the metrics on each compact subset of the regular part suffices. The hypotheses on the diameters of punctured neighborhoods in the limit and on the intrinsic diameters for the approximating metrics are stated explicitly; the conifold estimates and the asymptotics of cones needed to verify them are separate geometric inputs.

For curves, [v1, Lemma 4.1, p. 17, GM26] bounds the diameter of $\mathbb P^1$ by a multiple of $A^{1/3}$, where $A$ is the area, under a uniform upper bound for the metric. The projective model with a point pole in example 27.10.13 makes explicit the Jacobian order $q-1$, where $q$ is the dimension of the base, and the positive residual on the blowup. In example 27.10.15 the original base is a blowup. 34.15 Gauduchon variational volume

The Gauduchon variational volume extracts a homogeneous concave quantity from an elliptic normalization problem. The method goes back to Collins's construction [Theorem 2.13, Collins21]; chapter 26 develops the adjoint formula and its dependence on the chosen probability measure.

For a Kähler class represented by a closed form $\theta $, this construction meets the Hessian geometry of the Kähler cone: the metric of section 26.3 is $1/n$ times the Hessian of $-\log \int _ X\theta ^ n$ used by Magnússon [Section 1, Magnusson12]. Completeness and boundary points of positive volume at finite distance are studied in [Proposition 4.4 and Lemma 4.5, Magnusson12]; section 26.5 gives the argument by homogeneity in the present normalization.

The cones of invariant nonclosed forms in section 26.5.2 are computed directly. Stokes' theorem on the compact quotient shows that invariant $(n-1,n-1)$-forms are closed, and the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality for matrices then determines the quotient and its normalized minimizer. The resolvent argument of lemma 26.5.4 and the normalized chord in a prescribed linear subspace give the completeness statement. The Iwasawa example comes with an explicit compact quotient and a proof, by averaging coefficients, that this quotient is not Kähler. The initial construction uses the normalized invariant probability measure. In dimension three, proposition 26.5.7, corollary 26.5.8 follow from the formula for the variation of mass proved in the book and from the polynomial continuity of bounded products. On the Iwasawa quotient, theorem 26.5.9 extends the determinant formula to probability measures invariant along the central fibers, by means of a closed equation on the base for the Schur complement. The exact vertical Dirichlet defect characterizes all maximizing smooth densities, and the completion estimates for this larger family concern the means of the coefficients of the minimizing forms.

For the warped cones in section 26.5.3, a closed equation on the base and an explicit balanced cofactor give the full quotient. The pullback of the affine Hessian metric becomes flat, and the variance of the trace at the endpoints gives the three alternatives: a strip, a half-plane or the whole plane. Smooth existence for the equation on the closed base is theorem 9.1.1, and integration fixes its scalar; the warped background on the product may be nonclosed. That the finite faces are not big is tested against closed top-degree forms on the base, so the conclusion applies to singular current representatives as well. In the computations on projective space and on periodic tori, the density and the cone are given explicitly throughout.

The cones of currents in proposition 26.5.14 are determined by localized closed test forms and Stokes' theorem for distributions. At the finite faces of the warped cone, proposition 26.5.15 applies theorem 10.1.1, the existence theorem for continuous solutions with $L^ p$ densities, on the closed base; it fixes the scalar by invariance of the mass under bounded perturbations and expands the pulled-back products. The obstruction to global $C^{1,1}$ solutions is the general bound of currents by determinants in proposition 1.7.5, derived from the identity for coefficients in proposition 1.7.4.

The convergence of the whole sequence in proposition 26.5.16 combines bounds for the reciprocal densities with the compactness for a fixed background in theorem 10.3.4. Since the base is closed, the uniqueness theorem for invariant mass, 11.5.1, identifies every cluster point of the potentials. For any bounded competitor at the endpoint pulled back from the base, the density forced away from the extreme set already exhausts the total mass of the closed class, so positivity excludes residual mass on that set before uniqueness is applied.

The alternatives for the trace in proposition 26.5.17, proposition 26.5.18 follow from the explicit balanced cofactor and from integrals of reciprocal squares over tubes, while the mass on the closed base controls the remaining cofactor term. When the trace diverges, its normalization concentrates on the extreme components where the ratio is minimal; if there is a single extreme point, the whole sequence converges to a fiber current. Both the smooth numerator and the denominator then tend to zero, while their quotient remains equal to one.

In section 26.11, bigness of the closed nef core with positive top self-intersection follows from Demailly and Păun's criterion [Theorem 0.5, DP04], as recalled in [SGPT]; no such criterion for arbitrary nonclosed nef backgrounds is used.

On the warped paths of section 26.12, the same balanced cofactor as in section 26.5.3 computes the cost for every minimizing test. The finite improper identity follows from the first variation and the actual nef limit, while the five rates of proposition 26.12.2 come from the first and second inverse moments of the fiber coefficient. The exclusion of every closed nef positive-volume core in proposition 26.12.3 uses the smooth-test obstruction to bigness and Demailly–Păun's [arXiv v2, Theorem 2.12, pp. 12–13, DP04], together with the global potential consequence of the Kähler $\partial \bar\partial $-lemma. The constant closed case gives exactly the expression in Tosatti's Morse inequality [v2, Theorem 1.1, p. 1, Tosatti26]; this comparison does not assign a closed cohomology class to a nonconstant warped coefficient.

In the radial model on a projective manifold in section 26.13, a global bounded potential compensates the coefficient along the fibers and gives the lower bound against every smooth Gauduchon test, and explicit balanced metrics give matching values after perturbation. The test against closed top-degree forms on the base in theorem 26.13.2 excludes every quasi-plurisubharmonic potential whose curvature current is strictly positive. This disproves the conjecture that positive volume implies bigness without further restrictions; its versions for closed forms and for surfaces remain separate questions.

The limits of test forms in section 26.13.1 are computed from these explicit balanced metrics by Stokes' theorem for closed forms and radial integration. In dimension three the powers normalized by the trace concentrate on the degenerate fiber at a logarithmic rate, whereas in higher dimension their threshold quotient stays positive. Localized closed forms on the base, with a small Kähler correction, give a different balanced family approaching the fiber in every dimension of the model.

The initial metric of the Chern–Ricci flow in 26.20.2 is chosen so that, up to a smooth global change of gauge, the radial background is the limiting form at the maximal time, and the maximal-time theorem determines this lifetime. In the normalization $\operatorname {Ric}=-\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log \omega ^ n$, time one corresponds to time $1/(2\pi )$ for the Chern–Ricci form $-2\pi \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log \omega ^ n$ used in this book. A test against a closed form on the base excludes every positive representative at later times, and the support and the vertical rank of all annihilators at the endpoint follow directly from the semipositive representative and the formula for the trace matrix. The averaged trace is a special case of the general identity for the flow proved before.

In the criteria of 26.9.6, invariance of the mass of bounded potentials and the support of the measure on the contact set prevent the stretched obstacle envelopes from collapsing, and the sublinear condition provides competitors. 34.16 Closed classes and singular loci

For closed classes on a Hermitian manifold, almost positive representatives keep the cohomology class separate from the auxiliary metric. The convention in section 28.1 follows Boucksom's thesis [Section 2.1.2, BouThesis02]; it does not assign a Bott–Chern class to a nonclosed Hermitian form.

The smooth and analytic regularizations of 1.15.1, used for proposition 28.2.2, have the lower bounds of [Theorem 2.1, Bou04Zariski]; see also [Theorems 1.2.9–1.2.10, BouThesis02]. Smooth regularization loses positivity by a multiple of a continuous approximation of the Lelong numbers, whereas analytic regularization has a curvature error tending uniformly to zero and does not increase Lelong numbers.

The minimal multiplicities of definition 28.2.1 are defined through these almost positive approximating families, as in [Definition 2.1.5, BouThesis02]. They must be distinguished from the Lelong numbers of a single current with minimal singularities: in the example of a nef class on a ruled surface in [Section 1.2.3, BouThesis02], a current with minimal singularities has nonzero Lelong numbers.

The non-Kähler locus of definition 28.3.1 is defined in [Definition 2.1.19, BouThesis02], and the proof of theorem 28.3.2 by finite intersections and regularized maxima is the argument of [Theorem 2.1.20, BouThesis02].

The equivalence between the existence of a Kähler current and the existence of a Kähler modification, used in chapter 28, is the classical criterion recalled in [Theorem 1.2.11, BouThesis02]; it is used as an external geometric input. The equality of the non-Kähler locus and the null locus of a nef and big class is the transcendental Nakamaye theorem of Collins and Tosatti [CT15Loci], which also holds on compact manifolds in Fujiki's class $\mathcal C$; chapter 28 recalls this theorem from the literature.

The existence of Kähler models for every subvariety uses Fujiki's theorem that class $\mathcal C$ is closed under passing to closed subspaces [Lemma 4.6(2), Fujiki78], followed by Varouchas's theorem on Kähler modifications in the form stated and explained by Barlet [Theorem 10, pp. 31–32, Barlet00]. The descent of potentials is proved in proposition 1.22.12, so the proof of mass invariance does not repeat it.

In proposition 28.6.2, positive non-pluripolar mass implies bigness by [Proposition 1.22, BEGZ10]. The definition of the positive product, its agreement with the cup product for nef classes, and the fact that it is attained by currents with minimal singularities are in [Definition 1.17, arXiv pp. 17–18, and Proposition 1.20, arXiv p. 20, BEGZ10]. The family obtained from four points and its central metric in section 28.18 are Qin's construction [v1, Theorem A, p. 1, and Section 3, Lemmas 3.1–3.3, pp. 3–4, QinMetricExtension26]. Inoue surfaces were introduced by Inoue [Inoue74], and their higher-dimensional analogues by Oeljeklaus and Toma [OT05], who proved that the action of $\mathcal O_ K\rtimes U$ used in section 28.19 is free, properly discontinuous and cocompact, and that the resulting manifolds are not Kähler. The decomposition of Inoue surfaces into three-dimensional tori filled by dense leaves, and a Liouville argument along the leaves for bounded harmonic functions, appear in the work of Fang, Tosatti, Weinkove and Zheng on the Chern–Ricci flow [Section 2, Lemma 2.1, FTWZ16], where the Tricerri metric and the identity $\mathrm{Ric}(\omega _ T)=-\alpha $ are also recorded; their form $\alpha =\mathrm i\, \mathrm dw\wedge \mathrm d\overline w/(4y^2)$ equals $2\pi \theta $ in the normalization of section 28.19, and their $\omega _ T=4\alpha +\beta $ is the Tricerri metric used there. The description of all $\theta $-plurisubharmonic functions in theorem 28.19.2, the mass computations of proposition 28.19.5 and the absence of plurisigned metrics in proposition 28.19.6 are proved directly here. Angella, Guedj and Lu proved that, on a compact threefold, a Hermitian metric with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega \geq 0$ gives finite upper mass and one with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega \leq 0$ gives positive lower mass; in particular a pluriclosed metric gives both [Theorem B, AGL23]. The general question of finiteness and positivity of these masses is [Problem 1.8, AGL23]. Angella, Dubickas, Otiman and Stelzig showed that an Oeljeklaus–Toma manifold admits a pluriclosed metric only if $s=t$ [Corollary 3, ADOS24]; proposition 28.19.6 shows that for type $(s,1)$ with $s\geq 2$ there is not even a metric whose $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$ has a sign.

34.17 Closed classes and metric completeness

The examples theorem 28.14.1, theorem 28.14.2, theorem 28.14.4 test the completeness question raised by Di Nezza, Guedj and Lu [v1, Theorem 3.4 and Question 3.5, pp. 14–15, DNGL26]. The first uses a big class that is not nef and an ordinary solution with minimal singularities. The second has a semipositive nef and big background, a solution of full mass and finite energy with vanishing Lelong numbers, and the prescribed nonintegrable pole with smooth strictly positive coefficient; its cusp is complete, but a disjoint null curve lies at finite normal distance. The third, theorem 28.14.4, satisfies exactly the identities of [v1, Section 3.1, pp. 12–14, DNGL26] for the initial potential, the resolution, the discrepancy, the class and the prescribed volume. Its class is not nef and has a divisorial reference with minimal singularities, while the solution adds a cusp with vanishing Lelong numbers along a disjoint curve. These examples disprove the two separate completeness assertions for the elliptic equations; whether nefness and compatibility can hold simultaneously, and whether the evolving maximal flow itself is complete, remain open. The torus examples for normal lengths in section 28.15 have instead logarithmically weakened poles whose coefficients tend to zero at the divisor.

The energy argument in section 28.14 uses the classical finite-energy class of [Section 2, BEGZ10], relative to a reference with minimal singularities; finiteness of the mixed masses and of the relative energy comes from [Proposition 1.6 and Section 2.2, BEGZ10]. Uniqueness follows from the comparison principle for full mass [Corollary 2.3, BEGZ10]: the unweighted reference measure is infinite, but the two Monge–Ampère measures being compared are finite.

In section 28.14.1, the potential with minimal singularities is $V_\theta =0$, and exact radial identities show that the volume form is smooth and positive across both divisors. The bounded truncations have masses $1-p(T_ k)^2$ for the square and $1-p(T_ k)$ for the mixed product on the transition shell, where $p$ is the slope of the radial profile and $T_ k$ the radius at which the truncation at level $k$ begins; their contributions to the energy tend to zero. The products are computed by smooth convex approximation and the continuity along decreasing sequences proved by Bedford and Taylor [Theorem 2.1, pp. 4–7, BT82]. This direct computation of the energy agrees with the criterion by canonical approximation of [Propositions 2.10–2.11, arXiv pp. 26–27, BEGZ10]. Here vanishing Lelong numbers do not imply ordinary minimal singularities.

In section 28.14.2, radial barriers and the inversion of a Volterra operator construct the cusp along the curve for a fixed smooth volume form, and explicit charts on the blowup give a smooth positive residual volume and a Kähler residual at the initial time. The reference with minimal singularities is obtained from the extension across isolated points in lemma 1.3.8. The canonical truncations at level $k$ have cutoff energy $O(k\mathrm{e}^{-k/2})$, so the continuity of bounded products and the criteria for relative energy cited above apply. The remaining smooth pulled-back metric puts the exceptional curve at finite distance. This is an auxiliary solution of an elliptic equation; it is not identified with the evolving smoothing metric.

Hölder regularity imposes a further restriction on the metric. If a positive residual current has a Hölder continuous potential and is smooth and strictly positive outside a proper analytic set, theorem 1.18.1 shows that every point of that set lies at finite distance in the metric completion; on a compact manifold, removing a nonempty analytic set therefore gives an incomplete metric. Continuity alone allows complete ends: for the prescribed radial densities of theorem 28.12.2, boundedness of the minimal remainder and finiteness of the normal length have different logarithmic thresholds. The resulting complete examples with bounded potentials solve equations with prescribed densities; they do not have the compatibility with the flow discussed above.

The mechanism of Dang [v2, Proposition 5.2, pp. 16–17, Dang26], which bounds distances through a Dini condition on the square root of the modulus of continuity of the potential, is the subject of theorem 1.18.2. The trace calculation given here keeps the smooth background through the additive term in (1.61) and needs no doubling condition. It gives logarithmic rates and uniform limits of pseudometrics along subsequences in corollary 1.18.3, corollary 1.18.4; a common positive lower bound for the metrics is needed to exclude collapse.

34.18 Locally conformally Kähler geometry

In locally conformally Kähler geometry, the Lee form constrains both the rigidity of metrics and the volume forms that can be prescribed. The conjecture on pseudoeffectivity of the Lee class, 29.2.4, is formulated in terms of Gauduchon degrees by Ornea and Verbitsky [arXiv version, Conjecture 4.14 and Remark 4.16, OV25Balanced]; its consequence for balanced metrics is proposition 29.2.3. Their theorem on the volume forms of Vaisman metrics [arXiv version, Theorem 4.1 and Lemmas 4.2–4.4, OV24Vaisman] requires invariance under both the Lee and the anti-Lee vector fields. The proof of theorem 29.4.2 uses theorem 9.1.1: uniqueness gives a basic potential, transverse integration by Stokes' theorem fixes the scalar, and the Lee form of the new metric is parallel because it is closed and its dual vector field is Killing. The uniqueness argument keeps the scale of the candidate metric in (29.19) as an unknown until integration determines it.

The least-eigenvalue criterion 29.2.6 is proved by applying smooth Stokes to the volume polynomial of 29.2.5. Nonvanishing of the ordinary Bott–Chern class already follows from the conformal Gauduchon argument and the Lee degree (29.4). The Hopf calculations in 29.2.7, 29.2.8 distinguish constancy of the least eigenvalue from constancy of the whole spectrum, and distinguish first pointwise degeneration from collapse of total volume. These are explicit compact metrics in every dimension at least two. The criterion gives a smooth semipositive representative under its spectral hypothesis; pseudoeffectivity without that hypothesis remains the conjecture above.

The classification of radial metrics in 29.4.2 follows from explicit identities for the Hopf forms and a periodic scalar equation. Its positive periodic solutions are strictly ordered by the constant of integration, and their Lee periods fill the negative half-line. The positive twisted potential is constructed in the gauge given by the Lee form of the metric itself. Nonconstant radial volume forms force the metric to be non-Vaisman, and uniqueness of the metric is asserted only within the radial family considered there.

The proof of theorem 29.5.1 keeps the divergence terms in the Chern–Ricci-flat specialization of the argument of Barbaro and Otiman [v2, Section 4, equations (2)–(4), pp. 12–13, BO25LCK]. Their Theorem A gives the Gauduchon rigidity conclusion; retaining the scalar-curvature term in the integrated Bianchi identity gives the divergence identity and the equivalence with holomorphicity of the Lee field. The quotient and the two metrics in example 29.5.2 are computed directly: the holomorphic volume trivializes the canonical bundle, the flat gauge has nonzero Lee divergence, and compact pluriharmonic rigidity prevents a simultaneous conformal gauge. The conjecture for manifolds, 29.5.3, is stated in [Introduction, BO25LCK]. The Kodaira example admits a Vaisman metric on its given complex structure, so its conformal obstruction does not refute that conjecture.

The dual description of the Lee–Gauduchon cone and its bimeromorphic invariance in section 29.6 are due to Ornea and Verbitsky [Theorems 3.11 and 4.5, OV25LeeGauduchon]. The distinction between vanishing of the Lee–Gauduchon class and the strongly Gauduchon condition in section 29.7 follows [Remarks 5.6–5.8, OV25LeeGauduchon], and the criterion for the existence of strongly Gauduchon metrics used there is due to Popovici [Proposition 1.2, Pop13SG].

34.19 Regularity and singular spaces

Regularity in a big class is first a local question on the locus where strict barriers are smooth. For a closed class on a compact Kähler manifold, existence and uniqueness for (28.20) are due to Boucksom, Eyssidieux, Guedj and Zeriahi [Theorem A, equivalently Theorem 3.1, BEGZ10], and the bounds (28.21) in terms of minimal singularities are [Theorem B, quantitatively Theorem 4.1, BEGZ10]. The same uniqueness theorem for full mass identifies the solutions in closed classes in section 14.21, section 14.22, section 28.12, section 28.13. Smoothness on the ample locus in the nef case is [Theorem C, BEGZ10]; theorem 28.10.1 gives this smoothness in every closed big class, for a smooth positive volume form.

For a big background that need not be closed, the local Hölder theorem theorem 14.16.1 is Dang's [v1, Theorem A and Theorem 3.1, Dang26]. It gives the local input for the argument on the ample locus, while allowing the global potential to be unbounded and the lower curvature bound to be nonclosed. Formulating the equation with a positive scalar and only on the regular locus is equivalent, since the complementary analytic set carries neither smooth volume nor non-pluripolar mass.

The proof of local regularity also needs a quantitative stability estimate. The estimate with a strict barrier in section 14.14 is [v1, Theorem 3.3 and Remark 3.4, Dang26] in the case of minimal singularities, with the comparison potential normalized explicitly; the construction with auxiliary subsolutions in section 14.3 first treats a semipositive big background.

The interior regularity theorem theorem 14.17.1 combines this Hölder control with the assumption that the density is smooth and positive on the whole regular locus of one fixed strict analytic barrier. This domain is part of the hypothesis: smoothness of the density on a smaller open set does not give the same conclusion by restriction. The compact Pogorelov example example 28.11.5 has a smooth positive density on an inner chart, but its density loses regularity along the divisor in the gluing region, so it does not satisfy this hypothesis.

Local smoothing uses the theorem of Guan and Li on the Dirichlet problem [Theorem 1.1, p. 1186, GL10]. In (14.71) the background is zero and, in local coordinates $z$, the metric is $(\mathrm i/2)\partial \bar\partial |z|^2$; for a smooth positive density $G$, a large quadratic correction gives a strict subsolution. The same theorem constructs the approximants in section 14.17.3 and starts the passage to continuous boundary values and to zero density in section 14.6; in dimension one it is replaced by the Dirichlet problem for the Poisson equation. Once (14.17) is uniformly elliptic, the Evans–Krylov theorem for variable coefficients [Definition 3.2, Theorem 3.4, and the paragraph following it, TWWY15], followed by difference quotients and Schauder estimates, gives smoothness.

The local identification in section 14.20 uses Bedford and Taylor's uniqueness theorem for the bounded Dirichlet problem [Corollary 4.4, p. 17, BT82]. Degeneration of the density also matters for global stability: Kołodziej and Nguyen [Theorem 4.1, KN21] assume that the density satisfies $f\geq c_0$ for a constant $c_0>0$, in addition to their hypotheses on capacity. The densities giving nonuniqueness in chapter 11 have no such lower bound, even when they are positive almost everywhere.

For compact sets in a Hermitian manifold, Ahn [Theorems 1.1–1.2, Ahn26] proves that Hölder continuity of the extremal function is a pointwise local property. The weighted version assumes that both the unweighted extremal function and the weight are Hölder continuous, and that the set is uniformly dense in capacity; the same density condition enters the equivalence with the local and weakly local Hölder properties. These are the regularity settings referred to in chapter 33.

On singular spaces, regularity on a resolution must be combined with descent. Dang [v1, Theorem B, Dang26] proves continuity results for bounded solutions on resolutions under additional geometric and integrability hypotheses, notably for log terminal singularities; this result does not cover the arbitrary unibranch singularities in the problem posed in chapter 33. Pan [Theorems A and A', Pan25] constructs a Gauduchon metric on the regular locus of a compact reduced irreducible Hermitian space of dimension at least two; it is conformal and uniformly comparable to the given metric, and unique up to scale among such metrics. Control of the conformal factors on a resolution is central to the construction. This result motivates the questions about singular traces in chapter 33.

The boundary-weighted quadric surface in A.4.1 is Seo's [v1, Example 6.2, pp. 29–30, SeoToricIdeals26]. The paper uses the weight $\mathrm{e}^{-2\varphi }$, so the potential here is $u=2\varphi $. The adapted volume comes from a generator of $\omega _ X^{[4]}(4D)$ and pulls back to a constant times $|s|^{-1}$ times Euclidean volume on the finite quotient cover. Splitting the logarithmic quadrant gives the strict monomial test; angular orthogonality and division of convergent series give the full ideal. This proves the explicit example directly, without using the paper's general Newton-body description. The boundary-weighted ideal and the resolution-ideal relation in proposition A.4.1 use different volume conventions.

The affine toric monomial test in theorem A.4.5 is the holomorphic-character part of Seo's [v1, Theorem 6.1, pp. 28–29, SeoToricIdeals26], here restricted to full-dimensional cones and effective rational boundary. Its convex-cone criterion is [v1, Lemma 4.4 and Theorem 4.6, pp. 18–19, SeoToricIdeals26]; the proof here obtains coercivity by a compactness argument and sandwiches the actual logarithmic neighborhoods between translated cones. The subtraction test of corollary A.4.6 is the integral calculation behind [v1, Proposition 5.10 and Corollary 6.3, pp. 27 and 30, SeoToricIdeals26]. For this holomorphic-character integrability assertion the exponent can be any positive real number, without assigning a rational boundary to the subtracted weight. The quadric threefold and its whole-germ ideal in proposition A.4.7 are computed directly from the same criterion and the homogeneous coordinate ring. No general fractional-sheaf generation or ideal-envelope statement is used.

In the construction on unibranch curves in section A.7, the Green function, the exact mass of the pole, uniqueness in the full singularity type, descent through disks and the projective cusp are all obtained directly. $L^ p$ data on the normalization, $p>1$, give a continuous residual even when the background coming from a local embedding pulls back to a degenerate form. The example of the cusp also computes the exact threshold for densities on the singular curve. This is a result in dimension one; it neither extends the theorem on log terminal singularities cited above nor gives a general equation on nonclosed singular spaces.

The calculation at the endpoint in theorem A.7.4 is another direct construction from the Green function of the curve and the radial primitive of theorem 28.13.1. For one logarithmic family it distinguishes three properties: finite mass of the density, a bounded residual in the ordinary singularity type, and equivalence of multiplier ideals at all weights. It does not characterize arbitrary integrable data.

The equations with nonclosed backgrounds on products with cuspidal curves in section A.8 combine a correction of the reference to a smooth positive form, quantitative Fourier inversion, and a comparison of bounded energies in a background all of whose powers are $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$-closed. The weighted limit lemma 1.6.2 is proved by adding one complex variable $w$ and passing to the maxima with $\log |w|^2$, which reduces it to the ordinary convergence of mixed products along decreasing sequences, including top-degree products in one higher dimension. The corresponding classical input is Bedford and Taylor's [Theorem 2.4 and its proof, pp. 7–9, BT82]; their operator is $4\pi $ times ours, so products of degree $r$ scale by $(4\pi )^{-r}$. The resulting family with small data in all variables has Hölder continuous solutions on the ambient space, unique among all bounded competitors. The existence of bounded solutions for arbitrary nonnegative $L^ p$ data on the normalization, $p>1$, follows separately from theorem 32.1.1. These results complement the construction with Green functions on curves, without giving a general equation on singular spaces or a sharp criterion for densities on the singular space.

34.20 Geometric problems

A closed strictly positive current has geometric consequences that a positive nonclosed background need not have, and this distinction governs the passage from the normalization of equations to the geometric criteria below. The characterization of Fujiki's class by Kähler currents used in section 33.3 is Demailly and Păun's [Theorem 0.7, DP04], and the transcendental Grauert–Riemenschneider conjecture is [Conjecture 0.8, DP04]; in the arXiv version of that paper these are Theorem 0.6 and Conjecture 0.7.

For nef classes with positive top self-intersection, the equivalence between bigness and bounded mass cited in section 33.3 is Guedj and Lu's [Theorem 4.9, GL22]. The characterization of Fujiki's class by bounded mass and positive upper volume of the class is [Theorem C, BGL25]; that upper volume is different from the Gauduchon functional for a fixed probability measure.

The transcendental Morse inequality for closed classes on compact Kähler manifolds, and its consequences for differentiability of the volume and duality of cones, mentioned in section 33.3, are Tosatti's [Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2, version 2, Tosatti26]; they are quoted from the literature, and their proofs are not reproduced. The qualitative criterion under bounded mass is [Theorem 4.12, GL22].

The non-Hermitian locus in section 33.3 is that of Dang [Definition 2.12 and Theorem 2.14, DangNull26], where almost analytic singularities have the precise meaning of [Definition 2.8, DangNull26], including a smooth remainder on a suitable log resolution. Dang's qualitative identification of this locus with the null locus, for nef and big backgrounds that may be nonclosed, is [Theorem 1.1, DangNull26]. Theorem A of the same paper gives a numerical criterion under different hypotheses: bounded mass on the ambient manifold and positive lower volume on its submanifolds of positive dimension.

Theorem C of [DangNull26] identifies the singularities of the Chern–Ricci flow at a finite time with the null locus when the limiting lower volume is positive, for arbitrary initial metrics in dimension two and for initial metrics of Guan–Li type in higher dimensions, that is, initial metrics $\omega _0$ with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega _0=0$ and $\mathrm{d}\omega _0\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}\omega _0=0$. Once the limiting background is big, [Theorem 4.3, DangNull26] gives convergence as currents and smooth local convergence away from its non-Hermitian locus. This conditional parabolic input should be distinguished from the implication from positive volume to bigness without further hypotheses, which fails by theorem 26.20.5.

The theorem on the maximal existence time, theorem 26.20.3, is Tosatti and Weinkove's [Theorem 1.2, (1.4), and Section 4, arXiv version, TW15]. The proof given here combines the positive affine path and time-weighted estimates for the potential with lemma 9.4.1 and classical parabolic regularity. In the conversion to the book's conventions, potentials are multiplied by $2\pi $ and time is unchanged. The conditions for geometric smoothing in [Section 3.1, DNGL26] include the further compatibility discussed in section 34.17.

Distance estimates for a twisted flow also depend on its parameters. In Section 5.2 of [v2, p. 18, Dang26] the twist is fixed to be $\operatorname {Ric}(\omega _ X)$, for the reference metric $\omega _ X$ of that paper, and Theorem 5.7, p. 21, there allows the constants to depend on the endpoint of a finite time interval. The flat flows of example 26.20.7 show why the formulation for arbitrary twists in Theorem C of the introduction of that paper needs further control: a fixed initial density and a fixed lifetime do not bound the diameter independently of the twist, and a single positive twist can give unbounded diameter over an infinite lifetime.

The conjecture on balanced and pluriclosed metrics referred to in section 33.3 is stated by Fino and Vezzoni [Conjecture 1.1, FV26]; there the balanced and the pluriclosed metric are allowed to be different. That a compact manifold in Fujiki's class admitting a pluriclosed metric is Kähler is Chiose's [Theorem 0.2 in the author manuscript, Chiose14]; the locator refers to Chiose's manuscript version of that paper.

The case of parallel torsion, theorem 33.3.11, is a theorem of Chen and Zheng [v1, Theorem 1.2, pp. 2 and 8, CZParallel26]. Their projection by integration of parallel forms and their description of the holonomy are [v1, Lemmas 3.1–3.2 and Propositions 3.3–3.5, pp. 3–6, CZParallel26]; lemma 33.3.9 proves both descriptions again in a form that keeps track of positivity and of commutation with the differential. The slice of fixed trace and the maximum of the volume are [v1, Propositions 4.2 and 4.5, Lemmas 4.3–4.4, pp. 7–8, CZParallel26]. The inverse of the positive cofactor map proves the step of taking roots directly, and lemma 33.3.10 proves, by linear algebra of primitive forms, that a Hermitian form which is both balanced and pluriclosed is closed. The additional obstruction of Zhao and Zheng for non-balanced metrics with Bismut-parallel torsion, used in their Corollary 1.3, is a separate ingredient.

The conjectured long-time existence and convergence of the pluriclosed flow on balanced manifolds with $c_1(X)=0$ is [Conjecture 1.2, FV26]. The discussion in section 33.3 distinguishes a conserved balanced trace from a pointwise bound for the metric.

The persistence of pluriclosed metrics under deformations, theorem 33.3.14, which assumes that the Aeppli number $h_ A^{1,1}$ is constant, is Cavalcanti's [v5, Theorem 8.11, Section 8.3, pp. 41–42, CavSKT13]. The fourth-order Aeppli Laplacian and the realization of Aeppli classes by harmonic forms used in lemma 33.3.13 are due to Schweitzer [v1, Section 2.c, pp. 9–10, SchAeppli07], who constructs the elliptic symbol in Section 2.b, pp. 7–8. The proof given here writes the projection onto $\partial \bar\partial $-closed forms explicitly and obtains its smooth dependence on the parameter from the resolvent and the constancy of the dimension of the kernel. It applies to any finite-dimensional smooth parameter space and keeps the prescribed initial metric. This is also the analytic input stated in [v1, Theorem 2.1, p. 7, BarDef26]; Barbaro's construction of deformations of Lie groups there requires a separate argument with transverse cohomology.
