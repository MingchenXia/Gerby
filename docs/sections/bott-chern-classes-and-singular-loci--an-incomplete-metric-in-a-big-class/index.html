10.24 An incomplete metric in a big classE61E86F27D3F

E61E86F27D3F

We now consider exponential Monge–Ampère equations whose reference measure has a nonintegrable pole along a divisor, and ask whether the solution defines a complete metric on the locus where its current is smooth and positive. A divisorial part of a solution with minimal singularities can cancel the pole and leave the deleted divisor at finite distance. On the blowup of the projective plane at a point, the resulting metric away from the exceptional divisor is the pullback of the Fubini–Study metric. The class is big but not nef, and the equation has a unique solution of finite energy.

Finite energy refers to the classical closed theory of Definition 16.5.10. For a smooth closed real $(1,1)$-form $\theta $ with big class on a compact Kähler surface $Y$, and for $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )$ with minimal singularities, the Monge–Ampère energy relative to the envelope $V_\theta $ is

\[ E_\theta (u)=\frac13\sum _{j=0}^2 \int _ Y(u-V_\theta ) \left\langle (\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^ j\wedge (\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}V_\theta )^{2-j}\right\rangle . \]

For general $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )$, $E_\theta (u)$ is the decreasing limit of $E_\theta (\max (u,V_\theta -k))$ as $k\to \infty $, and $\mathcal E^1(Y,\theta )$ is the set of potentials with $E_\theta (u)>-\infty $; they have full mass.

Theorem 10.24.1.8A2E8673E914 There exist a smooth projective surface $Y$, a smooth irreducible divisor $E$, a smooth closed real $(1,1)$-form $\theta $ with big class $\alpha $, a Hermitian metric on $\mathcal O_ Y(E)$, a defining section $\sigma $ of $\mathcal O_ Y(E)$, and a smooth positive volume form $\mathrm{d}V_ Y$ with the following properties. The equation

0340B9B3D110 10.24.2
\begin{equation} \left\langle (\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho )^2\right\rangle =\mathrm{e}^{\rho }|\sigma |^{-2}\, \mathrm{d}V_ Y \end{equation}

has a unique solution $\rho \in \mathcal E^1(Y,\theta )$, and this solution has minimal singularities. Moreover,

\[ \operatorname {Amp}(\alpha )=Y\setminus E \]

is precisely the open set on which $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho $ is smooth and strictly positive. The restriction of this metric to every nonempty connected open subset of $Y\setminus E$ is incomplete. In particular, no dense Zariski open subset carries a complete induced metric.

Let $p=[1:0:0]\in \mathbb P^2$, and let $\omega _2$ be the Fubini–Study form on $\mathbb P^2$, normalized by $\int _{\mathbb P^2}\omega _2^2=1$.

Proof of theorem 10.24.1. The surface and the divisor. Use the incidence model

\[ Y=\{ ([Z_0:Z_1:Z_2],[W_1:W_2])\in \mathbb P^2\times \mathbb P^1: Z_1W_2=Z_2W_1\} . \]

The projections $\pi :Y\to \mathbb P^2$ and $q:Y\to \mathbb P^1$ identify $Y$ with the blowup of $\mathbb P^2$ at $p$, with exceptional divisor $E=\pi ^{-1}(p)$. Let $\omega _1$ be the Fubini–Study form on $\mathbb P^1$ with the same normalization as $\omega _2$, and write

\[ \beta =\pi ^*\omega _2,\qquad \gamma =q^*\omega _1, \qquad \Omega =\beta +\gamma . \]

The form $\Omega $ is Kähler, being the restriction of a product Kähler metric.

The linear map $(Z_0,Z_1,Z_2)\mapsto (Z_1,Z_2)$ defines a section $\sigma $ of

\[ \pi ^*\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(1)\otimes q^*\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(-1), \]

because on $Y$ the vector $(Z_1,Z_2)$ lies in the tautological line $[W_1:W_2]$. The section vanishes simply along $E$, which identifies this line bundle with $\mathcal O_ Y(E)$. For the product of the Fubini–Study metric and the metric induced on the tautological bundle, its squared norm is

\[ s=|\sigma |^2= \frac{|Z_1|^2+|Z_2|^2}{|Z_0|^2+|Z_1|^2+|Z_2|^2}. \]

In a blowup chart $\pi (t,w)=(t,tw)$, $q(t,w)=[1:w]$, one has

4BD50BB63FAE 10.24.3
\begin{equation} s=\frac{|t|^2(1+|w|^2)}{1+|t|^2(1+|w|^2)}, \qquad \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log s=[E]+\gamma -\beta . \end{equation}

The current identity follows either from this formula and the second blowup chart or from the Poincaré–Lelong formula.

The big class and its equation. Set

8F4A3AEF872F 10.24.4
\begin{equation} \theta =3\beta -2\gamma ,\qquad \rho =2\log s, \qquad T=\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho =\beta +2[E]. \end{equation}

Thus $\rho \in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )$. The class $\alpha =\{ \theta \} $ is big, because the potential $\psi =(5/2)\log s$ satisfies

4035514E1789 10.24.5
\begin{equation} \theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\psi =\tfrac 12(\beta +\gamma )+\tfrac 52[E]\geq \tfrac 12\Omega . \end{equation}

It is not nef, since $\int _ E\theta =-2$: the restriction of $\beta $ to $E$ vanishes and $q|_ E$ is an isomorphism onto $\mathbb P^1$. A nef class has nonnegative degree on every curve, by its defining smooth approximations.

On $Y\setminus E$ define $\mathrm{d}V_ Y=\beta ^2/s$. To check that it extends across $E$, put

\[ \mathrm{d}\lambda _{t,w} =\tfrac {\mathrm{i}}2\mathrm{d}t\wedge \mathrm{d}\bar t \wedge \tfrac {\mathrm{i}}2\mathrm{d}w\wedge \mathrm{d}\bar w, \]

and let $\mathrm{d}\lambda _{z_1,z_2}$ be the analogous Euclidean volume in affine coordinates of $\mathbb P^2$. The normalized Fubini–Study volume is

\[ \omega _2^2=\frac{2}{\pi ^2} \frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda _{z_1,z_2}}{(1+|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2)^3}. \]

Since the complex Jacobian determinant of $(t,w)\mapsto (t,tw)$ is $t$, we obtain

\begin{align*} \beta ^2& =\frac{2}{\pi ^2} \frac{|t|^2\, \mathrm{d}\lambda _{t,w}}{[1+|t|^2(1+|w|^2)]^3},\\ \mathrm{d}V_ Y& =\frac{2}{\pi ^2} \frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda _{t,w}}{(1+|w|^2)[1+|t|^2(1+|w|^2)]^2}. \end{align*}

The second expression is smooth and strictly positive at $t=0$. Interchanging the two homogeneous coordinates gives the extension on the other chart. The two extensions agree off $E$, hence everywhere on their overlap.

The non-pluripolar product of $T$ agrees with $\beta ^2$ off $E$ and gives no mass to $E$. The smooth measure $\beta ^2$ also gives no mass to $E$, so

\[ \langle T^2\rangle =\beta ^2, \qquad \mathrm{e}^{\rho }|\sigma |^{-2}\, \mathrm{d}V_ Y =s^2s^{-1}\frac{\beta ^2}{s}=\beta ^2. \]

The right side of (10.24.2) is defined on $Y\setminus E$ and extended by zero on $E$, so no product of zero and infinity occurs. This proves the equation and gives $\int _ Y\langle T^2\rangle =\int _{\mathbb P^2}\omega _2^2=1$.

Minimal singularities and energy. Let $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )$. Since

\[ \beta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(u-\rho )=\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0 \quad \text{on }Y\setminus E, \]

the function $u-\rho $ on $Y\setminus E$ descends to an $\omega _2$-plurisubharmonic function on $\mathbb P^2\setminus \{ p\} $. By lemma 5.24.8, it is bounded above, so $u\leq \rho +C_ u$ there. This inequality holds almost everywhere on $Y$, and lemma 5.24.3 makes it hold everywhere. In particular $u=-\infty $ on $E$, and $\rho $ has minimal singularities.

Let $V_\theta $ be the normalized envelope of section 5.40. Since $s\leq 1$, we have $\rho \leq 0$, and minimality gives

\[ \rho \leq V_\theta \leq \rho +C. \]

Thus $\rho -V_\theta $ is bounded off $E$, and the energy is

\[ E_\theta (\rho )=\frac13\sum _{j=0}^2 \int _ Y(\rho -V_\theta ) \left\langle (\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho )^ j\wedge (\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}V_\theta )^{2-j}\right\rangle . \]

The mixed measures have finite mass in the closed Kähler theory and do not charge $E$. Hence $E_\theta (\rho )>-\infty $, so $\rho \in \mathcal E^1(Y,\theta )$. A potential with minimal singularities has full mass; in this example that mass is $1$.

Uniqueness. Suppose $u\in \mathcal E^1(Y,\theta )$ is another solution, and let $\mathrm{d}\mu =s^{-1}\mathrm{d}V_ Y$ on $Y\setminus E$, extended by zero on $E$. This measure is smooth and positive off $E$, but has infinite total mass. The classical comparison principle for full-mass potentials in the closed theory gives

\[ \int _{\{ u<\rho \} }\mathrm{e}^{\rho }\, \mathrm{d}\mu \leq \int _{\{ u<\rho \} }\mathrm{e}^{u}\, \mathrm{d}\mu . \]

Both integrals are finite, since the two Monge–Ampère measures have mass $1$. The integrand on the left is strictly larger, which forces $\mu (\{ u<\rho \} )=0$. Interchanging $u$ and $\rho $ gives $\mu (\{ \rho <u\} )=0$ as well. Therefore $u=\rho $ almost everywhere on $Y\setminus E$ with respect to a smooth volume form, hence almost everywhere on $Y$, since $E$ has zero volume. By lemma 5.24.3, $u=\rho $ everywhere.

The ample locus and incompleteness. The Kähler current in (10.24.5) is smooth off $E$. On the other hand, every $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )$ satisfies $u\leq \rho +C_ u$, so its Lelong numbers at points of $E$ are at least those of $\rho =2\log s$, which are positive. Thus every Kähler current in $\alpha $ is singular at every point of $E$, and theorem 10.13.2 gives

\[ \operatorname {Amp}(\alpha )=Y\setminus E_{\mathrm{nK}}(\alpha ) =Y\setminus E. \]

The current $T$ equals $\beta $ there, and it is strictly positive because $\pi $ is a biholomorphism away from $E$. Its nonzero divisorial part $2[E]$ prevents smoothness at every point of $E$.

Let $U\subset Y\setminus E$ be a nonempty connected open set, and set $W=\pi (U)$. The map $\pi :(U,T|_ U)\to (W,\omega _2|_ W)$ is an isometry. Choose $x\in W$ and a smooth path $c:[0,1]\to \mathbb P^2$ from $x$ to $p$. Its first exit time

\[ t_0=\min \{ t\in [0,1]:c(t)\notin W\} \]

is positive, and $c([0,t_0))\subset W$. The path has finite Fubini–Study length. For $t_ k\nearrow t_0$, the lengths of its tails show that $c(t_ k)$ is a Cauchy sequence for the intrinsic metric of $W$. It has no limit in $W$: intrinsic convergence implies ambient convergence, and the ambient limit is $c(t_0)\notin W$. Thus $U$ is incomplete. Applying this argument to each connected component proves the assertion for a dense Zariski open set, which may be disconnected. $\square$

The cancellation $\mathrm{e}^{\rho }|\sigma |^{-2}=s$ removes the pole, and the divisor $E$, which the metric collapses to the point $p$, stays at finite distance. Thus an exponential equation in a big class can have an incomplete metric even when its finite-energy solution is unique and has minimal singularities. In this example the class is not nef. The next example shows that nefness alone does not restore completeness; there the pole produces a complete cusp, but a different curve lies at finite distance.

10.24.6 A nef class with an incomplete null end903A353086FC

Nefness of the class does not prevent incompleteness. In the next construction, the pole of the measure along a divisor $D$ does produce a complete cusp, but a disjoint curve $E$, on which the class has degree zero, lies at finite distance.

Theorem 10.24.7.8D207AFD99F5 There are a smooth projective surface $Y$, disjoint smooth divisors $E,D$, a smooth closed semipositive form $\theta $ with nef and big class, a smooth Hermitian norm on $\mathcal O_ Y(D)$, its defining section $\sigma $, and a smooth strictly positive volume form $\mathrm{d}V_ Y$, such that the equation

D648DC7F97C0 10.24.8
\begin{equation} \left\langle (\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho )^2\right\rangle =\mathrm{e}^{\rho }|\sigma |^{-2}\, \mathrm{d}V_ Y \end{equation}

has a unique solution $\rho \in \mathcal E^1(Y,\theta )$. The solution has zero Lelong numbers and is smooth on $Y\setminus (E\cup D)$, where its current $\Omega =\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho $ is strictly positive. The curve $E$ lies in the null locus of $\{ \theta \} $. Every dense Zariski open set on which $\Omega $ is smooth and strictly positive is incomplete, although every path in $Y\setminus (E\cup D)$ approaching $D$ has infinite length.

The measure $|\sigma |^{-2}\mathrm{d}V_ Y$ has a nonintegrable pole along $D$ with smooth strictly positive coefficient. The solution is unbounded near $D$ and therefore does not have minimal singularities: since $\theta $ is semipositive, the envelope $V_\theta $ is zero. The theorem concerns nefness and bigness only; the additional compatibility identities of the geometric smoothing problem in subsection 10.24.13 are a separate question.

The solution will be a radial function on $\mathbb C^2\setminus \{ 0\} $, identified with $Y\setminus (E\cup D)$. Its current is $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F(\log |z|^2)$ for a convex function $F$ whose derivative increases from $0$ at $E$ to $1$ at $D$, and the volume form $\mathrm{d}V_ Y$ is defined from $F$. The following lemma constructs the profile $F$; its two end identities make the volume form smooth and positive across $E$ and $D$.

Lemma 10.24.9.0A057E71255D There is $F\in C^\infty (\mathbb R)$ with $0<p:=F'<1$ and $p'>0$ such that, for $c=1/100$, some $B\in \mathbb R$ and $K=(9c^2/8)\mathrm{e}^{-B}>0$,

49DD99303236 10.24.10
\begin{align} F(t)& =B-3\log (1-c\mathrm{e}^{t/2}),& pp'& =K\mathrm{e}^{F+t} & & (t\leq -2),\\ F(t)& =t+\log H(p(t)),& pp'& =\mathrm{e}^{F-t} & & (t\geq 2),\tag{10.24.11} \end{align}

where $H(p)=(1-p)^2(1+2p)/6$. At the two ends,

\begin{align*} F(t)& =B+3c\mathrm{e}^{t/2}+O(\mathrm{e}^ t),& p'(t)& \sim (3c/4)\mathrm{e}^{t/2} & & (t\to -\infty ),\\ 1-p(t)& \sim 2/t,& p'(t)& \sim 2/t^2 & & (t\to +\infty ), \end{align*}

and $F(t)-t=-2\log t+\log 2+o(1)$ as $t\to +\infty $.

Proof. For $t\leq -2$ put $L(t)=-3\log (1-c\mathrm{e}^{t/2})$. With $q=c\mathrm{e}^{t/2}$,

\[ p_ L=L'=\frac{3q}{2(1-q)},\qquad p_ L'=\frac{3q}{4(1-q)^2},\qquad p_ Lp_ L'=\frac{9c^2}{8}\mathrm{e}^{t}(1-c\mathrm{e}^{t/2})^{-3}. \]

This proves the left identity for every additive constant $B$.

Since $H'(p)=(p-1)p$, the increasing function

\[ \mathcal T(p)=2+\int _{3/4}^{p}\frac{q}{H(q)}\, \mathrm{d}q \]

tends to $+\infty $ as $p\to 1$, because its integrand is asymptotic to $2/(1-p)^2$ there. Hence it has an inverse $p_ R(t)$, defined for $t\geq 2$ and on a neighborhood of $2$, with $p_ R(2)=3/4$ and $p_ R'=H(p_ R)/p_ R$. The function $F_ R=t+\log H(p_ R)$ has derivative $p_ R$ and satisfies the right identity. For $q=1-p_ R$,

\[ (1/q)'=\frac{3-2q}{6(1-q)}\longrightarrow \frac12. \]

Hence $q\sim 2/t$ and $H(p_ R)\sim 2/t^2$, and the asymptotics at the right end follow.

It remains to interpolate the derivatives. Set $d=p_ R(2)-p_ L(-2)>0$. Choose disjoint short collars in $[-2,2]$ at the two endpoints, and cutoffs $\alpha _ L,\alpha _ R$ equal to one on smaller collars. For small $\varepsilon >0$, the function

\[ Q_0=\alpha _ Lp_ L'+\alpha _ Rp_ R' +\varepsilon (1-\alpha _ L-\alpha _ R) \]

is positive and agrees with the derivative germs at the endpoints. The collars and $\varepsilon $ can be chosen so that $\int _{-2}^2Q_0<d$. Adding a nonnegative smooth bump in the interior, with integral $d-\int _{-2}^2Q_0$, gives a positive function $Q$, and we put

\[ p(t)=p_ L(-2)+\int _{-2}^ tQ(x)\, \mathrm{d}x. \]

This function matches both endpoint germs, is increasing and takes values in $(0,1)$. Integrating $F'=p$ with $F(2)=F_ R(2)$ gives the required $F$ and fixes the constant $B$ at the left end. The left expansion follows directly. $\square$

Proof of theorem 10.24.7. The background and the pole. Use the incidence surface of theorem 10.24.1, with blowup map $\pi :Y\to \mathbb P^2$ and exceptional curve $E$ over $[1:0:0]$, and let $\beta ,\gamma $ be the pulled-back Fubini–Study forms as there. Put $D=\pi ^{-1}(Z_0=0)$ and $A=\pi ^*c_1(\mathcal O(1))$. Then $E\cap D=\varnothing $, $A^2=1$ and $A\cdot E=0$. The class $A$ is nef, since it has the smooth semipositive representative $\beta $. Moreover $\{ \gamma \} =A-[E]$, and $\beta +\varepsilon \gamma $ is Kähler for $\varepsilon >0$. Thus

\[ \frac{\beta +\varepsilon \gamma }{1+\varepsilon } +\frac{\varepsilon }{1+\varepsilon }[E] \]

is a Kähler current in $A$, which proves bigness.

Choose an even nonnegative smooth function $\zeta $, supported in $[-1,1]$, positive on its interior and of integral one. The smooth convex function

\[ U(t)=\int _\mathbb R\max (0,t-x)\zeta (x)\, \mathrm{d}x \]

satisfies $U=0$ for $t\leq -1$, $U=t$ for $t\geq 1$, and $U',U''\geq 0$. On $Z_0\ne 0$, write $z=(Z_1/Z_0,Z_2/Z_0)$, $s=|z|^2$ and $t=\log s$, and give the section $Z_0$ of $\mathcal O(1)$ the squared norm $\mathrm{e}^{-U(t)}$; this norm is constant near the affine origin. Near the line at infinity, use

\[ w=Z_0/Z_1,\qquad v=Z_2/Z_1,\qquad h=1+|v|^2, \qquad s=h/|w|^2. \]

The section $Z_1$ then has squared norm $\mathrm{e}^{-U}/|w|^2=1/h$ near $w=0$. These formulas define a smooth Hermitian metric on $\mathcal O(1)$. Pull it back to $\mathcal O_ Y(D)$ and let $\sigma =\pi ^*Z_0$. Its curvature form $\theta $ and the norm of $\sigma $ are

\[ \theta =\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}U(t),\quad |\sigma |^2=\mathrm{e}^{-U(t)},\qquad \theta =\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log h,\quad |\sigma |^2=|w|^2/h\quad \hbox{near }D. \]

The radial Hessian shows that $\theta $ is semipositive. It vanishes near $E$ and has rank one near $D$. In particular $V_\theta =0$, and the nef class has volume $A^2=1$.

Extension of the potential. On $Y\setminus (E\cup D)\simeq \mathbb C^2\setminus \{ 0\} $, set

\[ \rho =F(t)-U(t),\qquad \Omega =\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F(t), \]

so that $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho =\Omega $. The eigenvalues of the Hermitian matrix of $\Omega $ are $p/s$ and $p'/s$, which are positive. Extend $\rho $ to $E$ by $B$ and to $D$ by $-\infty $.

Near the affine origin, the smooth functions $F(\log (|z|^2+\varepsilon ))$ are plurisubharmonic and decrease to the continuous extension of $F(\log |z|^2)$ as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$. Indeed, their two Hessian eigenvalues are $p/(s+\varepsilon )$ and $(s p'+\varepsilon p)/(s+\varepsilon )^2$, with $p,p'$ evaluated at $\log (s+\varepsilon )$. Since $\theta =0$ there, pulling back to $Y$ proves positivity near $E$.

Near $D$ put $t_\varepsilon =\log h-\log (|w|^2+\varepsilon )$ and $g(t)=F(t)-t$. The local potentials $g(t_\varepsilon )+\log h$ decrease to $\rho +\log h$, and their Hessians are

\[ p(t_\varepsilon )\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log h +(1-p(t_\varepsilon ))\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log (|w|^2+\varepsilon ) +p'(t_\varepsilon )\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi } \partial t_\varepsilon \wedge \bar\partial t_\varepsilon \geq 0. \]

Thus $\rho $ is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic on $Y$. Since $\rho $ is bounded near $E$ and grows like a double logarithm near $D$, all its Lelong numbers vanish.

The volume form and full mass. With Euclidean area $\mathrm{d}A$ in each complex variable, the radial determinant is

26865AC0337F 10.24.12
\begin{equation} \Omega ^2=\frac{2}{\pi ^2}\frac{pp'}{s^2} \mathrm{d}A_{z_1}\mathrm{d}A_{z_2}. \end{equation}

Define $\mathrm{d}V_ Y=\mathrm{e}^{-F}\Omega ^2$ off the two divisors. In an exceptional chart $z=(u,uv)$, the real Jacobian is $|u|^2$ and $s=|u|^2h$, so the left profile identity gives

\[ \mathrm{d}V_ Y=\frac{2K}{\pi ^2h}\, \mathrm{d}A_ u\mathrm{d}A_ v. \]

At infinity, the map $z=(1/w,v/w)$ has real Jacobian $|w|^{-6}$, and the right profile identity gives

\[ \mathrm{d}V_ Y=\frac{2}{\pi ^2h^3}\, \mathrm{d}A_ w\mathrm{d}A_ v, \qquad |\sigma |^{-2}\mathrm{d}V_ Y =\frac{2}{\pi ^2|w|^2h^2}\, \mathrm{d}A_ w\mathrm{d}A_ v. \]

The analogous second charts cover each divisor. The extensions are smooth and strictly positive, and they agree on overlaps because they agree on the punctured overlaps. The last expression exhibits the nonintegrable pole with smooth positive coefficient.

On the complement of the two divisors, $\mathrm{e}^{\rho }|\sigma |^{-2}\mathrm{d}V_ Y=\Omega ^2$; we extend this density by zero on $D$. Both measures give zero mass to the two divisors. In polar coordinates on $\mathbb C^2$, the Euclidean volume is $\pi ^2s\, \mathrm{d}s$ times the normalized angular measure, so

\[ \int _ Y\langle \Omega ^2\rangle =\int _{-\infty }^{\infty }2pp'\, \mathrm{d}t =p(+\infty )^2-p(-\infty )^2=1. \]

This proves the global equation and shows that $\rho $ has full mass.

Bounded truncations and energy. Near $D$ write $\alpha =\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}t=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log h$ and $\beta _ t=(\mathrm{i}/2\pi )\partial t\wedge \bar\partial t$. These forms have rank one, and

\[ \theta =\alpha ,\quad \Omega =p\alpha +p'\beta _ t,\quad \Omega ^2=2pp'\alpha \wedge \beta _ t,\quad \Omega \wedge \theta =p'\alpha \wedge \beta _ t,\quad \theta ^2=0. \]

Integration of $\alpha \wedge \beta _ t$ over the angular and projective variables gives $\mathrm{d}t$. Since $|\rho |=O(\log t)$ and $p'=O(t^{-2})$, the integrals of $|\rho |$ against $\Omega ^2$ and $\Omega \wedge \theta $ near $D$ are finite. Near $E$ the potential is bounded and $\theta =0$, and the remaining region is compact with smooth data.

Choose $M$ so that $\phi =\rho -M\leq 0$, and set $\phi _ k=\max (\phi ,-k)$. Convolving $G_ k$ in $t$ with an even nonnegative smooth kernel gives convex functions decreasing to $G_ k$ as the scale decreases. The corresponding bounded local potentials converge uniformly on compact sets, so their Bedford–Taylor products converge. Integrating the smooth radial measures through the transition shows that the transition carries mass $1-p(T_ k)^2$ for $(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\phi _ k)^2$ and mass $1-p(T_ k)$ for $(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\phi _ k)\wedge \theta $. The contributions of the remaining region converge absolutely by the bounds above. At $E$, bounded potentials give no mass to the curve, in accordance with the vanishing radial boundary mass $p(-\infty )^2=0$. Consequently

\[ E_\theta (\phi _ k)=\frac13\sum _{j=0}^2 \int _ Y\phi _ k(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\phi _ k)^ j\wedge \theta ^{2-j} \]

has a finite limit, and in particular is bounded below. This proves $\rho \in \mathcal E^1(Y,\theta )$.

The two metric ends. Fix $v=v_0$ in an exceptional chart and write $h_0=1+|v_0|^2$. The restriction of $\Omega $ to the normal line $\{ v=v_0\} $ is

\[ \Omega |_{v=v_0}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi } \frac{p'(\log (|u|^2h_0))}{|u|^2} \, \mathrm{d}u\wedge \mathrm{d}\bar u. \]

Its coefficient is asymptotic to $(3c\sqrt{h_0}/4)|u|^{-1}$, so the current has no smooth extension at any point of $E$. The radial path $u=r>0$ has finite remaining length, bounded by $C\int _0^{r_0}r^{-1/2}\, \mathrm{d}r$. At $D$ the normal coefficient is asymptotic to $2/(|w|^2t^2)$ and also diverges. Thus every open set on which $\Omega $ is smooth and strictly positive misses both curves.

Let $W$ be a dense Zariski open set on which $\Omega $ is smooth and strictly positive. Its complement $Y\setminus W$ is a proper analytic set containing $E$, and by local finiteness of irreducible components and compactness it has finitely many components, which are curves or points. Each curve other than $E$ meets $E$ in finitely many points. Choose a point of $E$ that avoids all these intersections and all point components. In a sufficiently small chart around it, the complement of $W$ is exactly $E$, so a tail of the radial path above lies in $W$. Its points approaching $E$ form a Cauchy sequence for the intrinsic metric with no limit in $W$: the smooth positive metric induces the manifold topology of $W$, and the only ambient limit is the removed point. Hence $W$ is incomplete.

Finally, on the cusp $\Omega =p\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}t+p'\, \mathrm{d}t\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}t$, both terms are nonnegative, and $p'\geq c_0/t^2$ for large $t$ and some $c_0>0$. The length of any path with $t\to \infty $ is therefore bounded below by a positive multiple of $\int |\mathrm{d}t|/t$, which diverges. Thus the end at finite distance is the null curve $E$, disjoint from the pole. $\square$

Exponential equations of this kind arise as auxiliary elliptic equations in the smoothing of singular initial data by geometric flows; the completeness question for them is discussed in section 25.19. In that setting the class, the divisor and the volume form are not arbitrary: they are determined by the initial potential through a log resolution. The following example shows that incompleteness persists under these compatibility conditions.

\[ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w=\left(-\frac2L+\frac2{rL^2}\right)\alpha ,\qquad Q(r):=\frac{c\alpha +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w}{\mathrm{e}^{w}h^{-1}c\alpha } =\frac{crL^2-2rL+2}{c(1+r)}. \]

For $k>0$, the regular radial solution $v=V(q)$ of $(-\Delta +k)v=F(q)$ satisfies
