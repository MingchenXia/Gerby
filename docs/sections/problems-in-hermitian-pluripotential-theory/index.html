21.1 Problems in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Definitions, results, and proofs on this subject.

33 Problems in Hermitian pluripotential theory

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Throughout this chapter $X$ is a connected compact complex manifold of dimension $n\geq 1$. The Hermitian forms, volume forms and backgrounds needed for each problem are fixed in the section where the problem is stated.

Many arguments in this book begin with linear control. A uniform bound on Gauduchon traces makes a family of positive currents relatively compact, and Gauduchon duality expresses scalar invariants through optimization over test currents. Most of the questions below arise because such linear control does not determine the nonlinear data of a limit, namely its Monge–Ampère mass and its singularities. Let $\omega $ be a Hermitian form on $X$ that fails the bounded mass property of definition 6.5.1. By proposition 6.7.1, corollary 6.7.2, currents $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ with integrable coefficients can converge in the coefficient $L^1$ norm, with Gauduchon trace measures converging in total variation, while their determinant masses are infinite. By proposition 16.3.3, even the relative envelope class $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\omega ,0)$ of definition 13.4.1 contains potentials of infinite mass. Passing from linear bounds to an exact equation therefore requires additional control of the nonlinear limit.

Two further requirements recur. Positivity of a scalar invariant does not imply strict positivity: the smooth semipositive background on a projective manifold in theorem 26.13.2 has positive Gauduchon variational volume but is not big. Likewise, an envelope realizing prescribed divisorial lower bounds may acquire larger orders, and then it does not recover the prescribed singularity type. Thus the mass of a limiting solution, strict positivity of a current, and exactness of its divisorial orders have to be checked in their respective arguments.

33.1 Threshold equations and singular limits

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Solving a Monge–Ampère equation with singular data involves two separate steps: one constructs a subsolution at the optimal coefficient, and then passes to a limit of approximate solutions without losing Monge–Ampère mass. Throughout this section, $h$ is a Hermitian form on $X$ and $\mathrm{d}V$ a smooth positive probability volume form. We write $v_-(h)$ and $v_+(h)$ for the lower and upper masses of $h$ (definition 6.5.1), $\mathrm{MA}^{\mathrm{ext}}_ h(u)$ for the extended Monge–Ampère measure of $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,h)$ (theorem 6.1.2), which may have infinite mass, and $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,h,0)$ for the relative envelope class with reference $0$. For a nonzero nonnegative density $f\in L^1(\mathrm{d}V)$, the density threshold $b_ h(f)$ is the largest $a\geq 0$ for which some $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,h)$ satisfies $\mathrm{MA}^{\mathrm{ext}}_ h(u)\geq af\, \mathrm{d}V$ (theorem 14.27.2); such a $u$ with $a=b_ h(f)$ is called a threshold subsolution. Finally, $\mathcal D_ h$ is the space of density roots $p\in L^ n(\mathrm{d}V)$ whose root norm $\rho _ h(p)=b_ h(|p|^ n)^{-1/n}$ is finite (proposition 14.31.3).

In two regimes the equations are solved for arbitrary finite non-pluripolar measures. For a semipositive big background $\theta $ with positive lower mass and a model reference satisfying (13.26), theorem 31.1.1 solves both the equation with a positive exponent and the normalized equation. For an arbitrary smooth big background $\theta $, theorem 32.7.1, theorem 32.7.2 solve both equations under bounded mass and positive lower volume of the model reference. The existence theorem for $L^ p$ densities in the minimal ordinary singularity type, in section 14.15, requires neither mass property; in the second regime, corollary 32.8.3 also places the solutions for $L^ p$ densities in the prescribed ordinary model type.

Under bounded mass the reference may also vary. Suppose the ordinary model references converge for the volume comparison $d_\theta $ of section 30.6 and have a common positive lower bound for their lower volumes, and the densities converge in $L^1$ of a fixed finite non-pluripolar measure. Then, by theorem 32.5.4, theorem 32.7.3, for a fixed positive exponent the solutions converge in capacity and their Monge–Ampère measures converge in total variation. At exponent zero the scalars and the measures converge, and every $L^1$-convergent subsequence of normalized solutions converges to a solution of the limiting equation; singling out one limit potential requires a further uniqueness or selection argument.

To remove these mass hypotheses, one must replace each place where they enter the compactness arguments. The proof in the semipositive regime uses finite auxiliary contact measures to construct a common lower envelope, and then obtains the limit for a moving measure from convergence in capacity and control of deep sublevel tails. The proof for an arbitrary big background uses finite upper relative volumes to construct a rooftop between classes and to maximize the weighted measure. In either case one must still prove that the measure of the solution is finite; the formal existence of the envelopes does not give this.

33.1.1 Individual density thresholds

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

One must distinguish solvability for all measures from solvability for one fixed density. By corollary 14.26.4, the normalized equation $\mathrm{MA}^{\mathrm{ext}}_ h(u)=c\mu $ has a solution in $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,h,0)$ for every nonzero finite non-pluripolar measure $\mu $ exactly when $v_-(h)>0$. When $v_-(h)=0$, theorem 14.26.3 produces a probability density in $L^1(\mathrm{d}V)$, bounded below by a positive constant, that admits no positive scaled subsolution. Such obstructing densities are dense in $L^1$, although every smooth strictly positive density is solvable. The compact threefold of theorem 26.18.1 has $v_-(h)=0$ for every Hermitian form $h$, so the obstruction does occur.

For one nonnegative density $f\in L^1(\mathrm{d}V)$, the threshold $b_ h(f)$ is the decreasing limit of the normalization constants of the bounded truncations $\min (f,k)$, see (14.101). An argument with upper envelopes and damped continuity shows that a subsolution attains this coefficient, but it proves neither an exact equation nor finiteness of the mass. Smooth comparison tests alone do not detect the threshold, by theorem 14.27.5. On the other hand, the Gauduchon formula of theorem 14.28.1 expresses the root norm as a supremum of linear tests, which makes $b_ h$ upper semicontinuous in $L^1$. The densities with positive threshold are those whose roots $f^{1/n}$ lie in $\mathcal D_ h$, and this space does not depend on the Hermitian form (proposition 14.28.2). With the norm $\| p\| _{L^ n}+\rho _ h(p)$ it is a Banach space; it equals $L^ n$ when $v_-(h)>0$, and it is complete for $\rho _ h$ alone exactly when $v_+(h)<\infty $, by proposition 14.31.3, proposition 14.31.4.

Problem 33.1.1. Let $h$ be a Hermitian form on $X$ with zero lower mass, $v_-(h)=0$, and let $\mathrm{d}V$ be a smooth positive probability volume form. Let $0\leq f\in L^1(\mathrm{d}V)$ be nonzero, with positive density threshold $b_ h(f)>0$. Does there exist $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,h)$ such that

\[ \mathrm{MA}^{\mathrm{ext}}_ h(u)=b_ h(f)f\, \mathrm{d}V? \]

Determine the singularity type or envelope class in which such an equation should be posed.

The hypothesis $v_-(h)=0$ is what makes the problem open. For $\lambda >0$, write $u_\lambda $ for the greatest solution of the exponential equation $\mathrm{MA}^{\mathrm{ext}}_ h(u)=\mathrm{e}^{\lambda u}f\, \mathrm{d}V$, when it exists (theorem 14.34.1). When $v_-(h)>0$, corollary 31.7.1 solves the threshold equation in $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,h,0)$ for every nonzero nonnegative $L^1$ density: comparison of finite measures identifies the scalar, and dominated continuity shows that every $L^1$-convergent subsequence of the normalized greatest exponential solutions $u_\lambda -\sup _ Xu_\lambda $ converges in capacity. This covers the Hopf example of chapter 8, which has positive lower mass but not bounded mass. For $L^ p$ densities with $p>1$, the continuous solution of the normalized equation and proposition 14.27.4 answer the question without any lower mass assumption.

At zero lower mass, theorem 14.29.3 solves the threshold equation with finite mass for explicit densities on a split product with projective space; these densities are integrable but belong to no $L^ p$ with $p>1$. There the background $h$ is a nonclosed Hermitian form, and the canonical solutions $u$ belong to $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,h,0)$; moreover, for every $0<\delta <1$, the envelope $P_{\delta h}(u)$ of section 1.19, the largest $\delta h$-plurisubharmonic function below $u$, is not identically $-\infty $. More generally, theorem 14.29.5 solves the exact finite-mass equation for every nonzero integrable density from a closed Kähler factor, with the appropriate change of reference volume. The threshold is the split background's mass divided by the integral of the factor density. On the product of the elliptic threefold with $\mathbb P^2$, this gives threshold $240\pi $ for every probability density from that factor. An arbitrary density on the product need not have this form, and the general problem remains open.

For product measures from a projective-line factor, proposition 14.30.4 gives an exact finite-mass equation for every non-polar factor probability. In the separated sector, boundedness and continuity are exactly those of its logarithmic potential. When that potential is finite and continuous, theorem 14.30.5 identifies the attained coefficient over all genuine subsolutions, constructs the greatest exponential solution for every positive exponent, and gives whole-family uniform selection with error $O(\lambda )$. The Sobolev measure trace supplies existence, and bounded comparison extends greatestness to singular competitors. Weighted circle probabilities in proposition 14.30.6 give measures singular with respect to smooth volume. The zero-lower-mass specialization retains the factor hypothesis. These product criteria do not classify arbitrary measures on a fixed Hermitian manifold.

A different geometric condition applies directly to singular measures on a fixed Hermitian manifold. The probability $\rho $ of (14.99) is chosen from a coordinate ball before the datum. If $\nu $ is finite, nonzero and has bounded density with respect to $\rho $, proposition 14.25.1, theorem 14.25.3 give a strict Hölder scaled subsolution and a unique bounded exponential solution for every positive exponent. In addition, corollary 14.25.2 gives a normalized Hölder solution of $\mathrm{MA}_ h(w)=c\nu $ with $c>0$ obtained as part of the solution. This includes bounded densities on one of the spheres and needs neither a product decomposition nor positive lower mass. It is a sufficient condition; it does not classify arbitrary individual measures or identify a homogeneous threshold coefficient.

For the data of 33.1.1, corollary 14.34.2 shows that the greatest exponential solutions $u_\lambda $ exist for every $\lambda >0$, that the scalars $c_\lambda =\mathrm{e}^{\lambda \sup _ Xu_\lambda }$ converge to $b_ h(f)$ as $\lambda \downarrow 0$, and that the measures $\mathrm{e}^{\lambda u_\lambda }f\, \mathrm{d}V$ converge in total variation. What is missing in general is convergence in capacity of the normalized potentials. If $f^{1/n}$ lies in the subspace $\mathcal D_ h^0$ of density roots with uniformly vanishing tails (proposition 14.31.5), proposition 14.34.3 gives a modulus for the errors of the scalars and of the measures; a rate linear in the exponent would require further control.

For $n\geq 3$, a power capacity bound (14.115) supplies both root tails and the compactness needed for an exact equation. By theorem 14.32.1, the threshold is positive and finite, the root belongs to $\mathcal D_ h^0$, and a subsequence of the normalized clipped solutions converges uniformly to a continuous solution at $b_ h(f)$. The logarithmic density of theorem 14.32.2 satisfies this condition on every fixed Hermitian background, while belonging to no $L^ p$ with $p>1$. Its reference potential is continuous but not Hölder, and its root tails have the bound (14.118). These conclusions use the capacity assumption on the fixed datum. Whether all positive-threshold roots have vanishing tails, and whether tails alone imply an exact equation at zero lower mass, remain open.

Positivity and integrability of $f$ do not make the threshold positive, and exponential equations can be solvable even when it vanishes. For zero threshold, theorem 14.34.5 gives a sufficient growth condition in the radial variable, and theorem 14.34.6 constructs a single strictly positive probability density with $b_ h(f)=0$ whose exponential equation has, for every positive exponent, a greatest solution of finite mass; all its solutions are unbounded. In that construction the projective factors are chosen after the collapsing sequence on the base.

For this density, proposition 14.34.8 shows that any choice of exact exponential solutions has total mass tending to zero as the exponent decreases to zero; the argument says nothing about convergence of the normalized potentials. In general, the set $\Lambda _ h(f)$ of positive exponents for which the exponential equation is solvable is an upper interval, by proposition 14.34.7. A necessary criterion on the density for solvability at zero threshold, solvability for an arbitrary prescribed concentration family, and an extension to arbitrary non-pluripolar measures are open.

Suppose that $a=\inf \Lambda _ h(f)$ is positive. By proposition 14.34.10, $a$ belongs to $\Lambda _ h(f)$ if and only if there are exact solutions at exponents decreasing to $a$ whose suprema are bounded below; if $a\notin \Lambda _ h(f)$, the total masses of every such sequence of solutions tend to zero. The common upper barrier of lemma 14.34.9 also shows that the greatest solutions become monotone in the exponent after a logarithmic correction. Consequently, by proposition 14.34.11, they are continuous from the right in $L^1$ and in capacity at every exponent in $\Lambda _ h(f)$, and their Monge–Ampère measures converge in total variation. At every interior exponent, proposition 14.34.12 gives a left limit in $L^1$ and in capacity that solves the equation at that exponent and lies below the greatest solution; the equation measures converge in total variation. By proposition 14.34.13, these potentials can differ at only countably many exponents. Whether a positive endpoint can fail to belong to $\Lambda _ h(f)$, or an actual jump from the left can occur, remains open.

33.1.2 Dual attainment and exact equations

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

For the current formulation of the dual problem, suppose $n\geq 2$. By Gauduchon duality, the threshold is determined by a maximization problem over test currents. Let $\mathscr G_ h$ be the compact convex set of normalized Gauduchon currents, the positive $(n-1,n-1)$-currents $T$ with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T=0$ and $\int _ Xh\wedge T=1$ (section 4.4), and let $k_ T$ be the determinant density of $T$ (theorem 26.8.1). For $0\leq p\in \mathcal D_ h$, the root norm $\rho _ h(p)$ is the supremum of $D_ p(T)=\int _ Xp\, k_ T\, \mathrm{d}V$ over $T\in \mathscr G_ h$. A maximizing current can turn the threshold inequality into an equality, provided its regularity, or control of its weighted moments, prevents loss of mass in the limit. For roots in the closed order ideal $\mathcal D_ h^0$, theorem 26.9.1, proposition 26.9.4 show that the supremum is attained and represent all supporting probabilities of the threshold functional by maximizing currents. These probabilities are absolutely continuous, and by proposition 26.9.5 the trace errors along smooth maximizing sequences tend to zero in total variation. Uniform integrability along a single maximizing sequence already gives attainment and convergence of the determinant-weighted measures of that sequence (proposition 26.9.3), but it does not give the representation of all supporting probabilities.

Regularity of a maximizer has strong consequences. Let $n\geq 2$ and let $f$ be a nonzero density with $b_ h(f)>0$. If $D_{f^{1/n}}$ has a smooth strictly positive maximizer, then $f^{1/n}$ is smooth and nonnegative, and every threshold subsolution is smooth and solves the equation exactly, uniquely up to constants (corollary 26.9.6). For a Kähler background, corollary 26.9.8 shows that such a maximizer exists exactly when $f$ is smooth and strictly positive. The strict positivity fails for nonclosed backgrounds: on every compact complex manifold of dimension at least two, example 26.9.9 gives a smooth strictly positive maximizer for a density vanishing on an open set.

Theorem 26.9.10 weakens the hypothesis to a smooth semipositive maximizer that is strictly positive outside a proper analytic subset $Z\subset X$. It still gives an exact equation on all of $X$, smoothness of the solutions off $Z$, and uniqueness of threshold subsolutions up to constants. The proof takes the maximum of two subsolutions and uses a Gauduchon integral to eliminate the divisorial part of their difference; no singularity type is prescribed in advance. The prime divisors along which the threshold solution has positive Lelong number have linearly independent Bott–Chern classes, by corollary 26.9.22. In this regime write $b=b_ h(f)$ and $c_\lambda =\exp (\lambda \sup _ Xu_\lambda )$ for the greatest exponential solutions $u_\lambda $, $\lambda >0$. By proposition 26.9.11, the whole family of normalized solutions converges in $L^1$ as $\lambda \downarrow 0$, it has a fixed lower barrier, and $b\leq c_\lambda \leq b\mathrm{e}^{C\lambda }$ for a finite constant $C$ independent of $\lambda $. If the envelope $P_{\delta h}(u_0)$ of the normalized threshold solution $u_0$ is not identically $-\infty $ for some $0<\delta <1$, the convergence also holds in capacity (corollary 26.9.12); for $L^ p$ data with $p>1$ the threshold solution is continuous and the convergence is uniform (corollary 26.9.18). In example 26.9.23 the solution is smooth, but the root $f^{1/n}$ is not differentiable on $Z$. For general $L^1$ data we do not know whether $P_{\delta h}(u_0)\not\equiv -\infty $ holds automatically, nor how regular the solution is across $Z$.

The moment criterion of theorem 26.9.24 requires no regularity of a maximizing current. Suppose that the supremum-normalized $h$-plurisubharmonic functions have uniformly bounded first moments against the determinant-weighted probabilities of one smooth maximizing sequence. Then the exponential solutions have a fixed lower barrier, and their normalized cluster limits lie below the greatest normalized threshold subsolution $\Phi _ f$ of proposition 14.27.3. If, in addition, $P_{\delta h}(\Phi _ f)\not\equiv -\infty $ for some $0<\delta <1$, the lower-barrier criterion gives convergence in capacity along subsequences and an exact threshold equation, without either mass property. An entropy bound on these test probabilities implies both the bound on first moments and the uniform integrability along one sequence used above. By proposition 15.6.1, such an entropy bound is equivalent to a uniform bound for $H_0$, the convex dual of the normalization functional with zero reference (chapter 15). An entropy bound does not, however, give the condition on $P_{\delta h}(\Phi _ f)$.

Attainment by a singular current therefore does not settle the equation by itself. The roots from a closed factor form a closed subspace of $\mathcal D_ h^0$ isomorphic to $L^ n$ on that factor, by proposition 14.31.6; some of these roots belong to no higher $L^ r$ space, and their tails decay only as a power of $\log K$. At zero lower mass we do not know in general whether the root-tail condition $f^{1/n}\in \mathcal D_ h^0$ suffices for an exact threshold equation, or even whether $\mathcal D_ h^0=\mathcal D_ h$. By proposition 26.9.2, the tail condition is equivalent to continuity of the threshold under logarithmic perturbations $\mathrm{e}^{F_ j}f$ with $F_ j$ uniformly bounded and tending to zero in measure. This is continuity on order intervals, not continuity of the threshold under arbitrary $L^1$ perturbations.

Convergence in capacity cannot be replaced by $L^1$ convergence in these arguments. On a curve, the ideal envelopes with finitely many logarithmic poles in theorem 16.4.2 have zero non-pluripolar mass, yet they converge in $L^1$ to a smooth potential of positive mass. The existence theorems obtain the stronger convergence from their measure bounds; representing the measure by a carrier, even within a prescribed singularity type, does not provide it.

33.1.3 Regularity of singular solutions

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Regularity estimates provide the compactness needed to single out a limit as the exponential parameter tends to zero. In this subsection $\theta $ is a smooth big real $(1,1)$-form, and $\rho $ is the strict barrier with analytic singularities fixed in section 14.12, with pole set $Z$ and regular locus $\Omega =X\setminus Z$. For bounded solutions with $L^ p$ density, $p>1$, theorem 14.5.1 gives continuity wherever $\rho $ is locally bounded. A locally Hölder continuous solution with minimal singularities is smooth on $\Omega $ when the density is smooth and positive there (theorem 14.17.1), and global $L^ p$ control of the density gives the Hölder hypothesis (theorem 14.16.1, corollary 14.17.11).

For a fixed reference $\eta $ with $\rho \leq \eta \leq 0$, corollary 14.14.2 shows that the potentials $u$ with $\eta -C_0\leq u\leq \eta $ and uniformly bounded $L^ p$ densities form a compact family for uniform convergence of differences on $\Omega $. Since the reference need not have minimal singularities, this compactness does not by itself make the potentials regular. In the minimal type, the convergence of scalars and densities in proposition 14.16.2 identifies the equation satisfied by every cluster limit of the normalized exponential solutions.

Smoothness of the density on an open set is not enough. On a compact Kähler manifold with a smooth positive volume form, theorem 28.10.1 proves that the full-mass solution is smooth on the ample locus of every big class, nef or not. Smoothness of the density only on the ample locus, however, does not imply global $L^ p$ integrability. On an arbitrary open set where the density is smooth, it does not even imply continuity of a bounded solution with minimal singularities: theorem 14.20.4 gives a compact Kähler counterexample with constant density on a ball. When the background has smooth local potentials, theorem 14.20.1 gives a sufficient condition in terms of smooth boundary traces on small balls. Among prescribed singularity types, the resolved logarithmic types of theorem 14.21.1 admit Hölder remainders when their residual form is positive, while for exceptional minimal poles theorem 14.22.2, theorem 14.22.4 give a Hölder remainder on the target and quantify the loss of integrability caused by the Jacobian. Neither result covers arbitrary prescribed types, and neither derives a global Hölder bound from information on the density confined to the regular locus. At a zero of the density, even smooth data need not give a smooth residual: proposition 28.11.4, example 28.11.6 give a necessary vanishing order and show that it is not sufficient. Beyond these resolved and explicit geometric cases, the regularity questions are open.

For the nonlogarithmic projective types of section 15.14, the central density criterion gives residual regularity across the pole and smoothly through density zeros. Perturbing the same singular reference as an everywhere radial obstacle instead gives a globally $C^{1,1}$ residual, zero near the pole, with open free regions of zero measure (theorem 15.15.2, corollary 15.15.3). Fixed-support bounded $C^2$ obstacle families satisfy the derivative stability of theorem 15.15.4, and proposition 15.15.5 transfers the full envelope to any fixed positive Hermitian product factor. These explicit ordinary-type regimes do not cover general singular references or ideal weighted envelopes.

33.1.4 Uniqueness and selection

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Where the density vanishes, the linearized equation degenerates, and the zero set can separate regions where it is elliptic. The question is whether the difference of two solutions can vary from one such region to another. Throughout this subsection $\omega $ is a Hermitian form on $X$, $f$ is smooth and nonnegative, and potentials are normalized by $\sup _ Xu=0$.

On $\mathbb P^3$, theorem 11.6.1 gives a nonclosed Hermitian form and a smooth nonnegative density with two distinct smooth normalized solutions; there the density vanishes on an open set. The construction of theorem 11.7.1 has uncountably many smooth normalized solutions for a density that is positive off two disjoint real hypersurfaces, and after a modification of the background, theorem 11.8.1 achieves exactly quadratic vanishing on those hypersurfaces. Thus neither a zero set of zero volume nor vanishing of finite order guarantees uniqueness. In all these examples the background is a Hermitian form with bounded mass; the degeneracy responsible for nonuniqueness lies in the density and in the solution forms.

These examples restrict any geometric use of the equation. A construction assigning a canonical potential to arbitrary nonnegative data must specify a selection procedure and prove that it is well defined; existence in the minimal ordinary singularity type does not provide one. The scalar, which is unique, and for strictly positive data the corresponding variational volume, remain meaningful without uniqueness of the potential. In low dimension uniqueness holds under additional conditions. On a compact Hermitian surface, 11.3.2 proves uniqueness for $C^2$ potentials when the common density is continuous and positive on a dense set, with no differential condition on the metric; in dimension three, 11.3.1 uses instead a sign condition on $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega $. The two arguments rest on different integration identities: the cofactor identity (11.20) and a signed estimate for the quadratic difference.

When $\{ f>0\} $ is connected and dense, theorem 11.4.1 shows that two bounded normalized solutions with the same positive scalar coincide; the theorem applies to the measure $cf\, \mathrm{d}V$ after the smooth positive ratio $\mathrm{d}V/\omega ^ n$ is absorbed into the density. In all the counterexamples above whose zero set consists of hypersurfaces, including the real-analytic example of 11.9.1, the positivity set has three connected components. None of them contradicts 33.6.1, which concerns strictly positive densities $\mathrm{e}^ F$ with $F$ continuous.

For a fixed nonzero density $f\in L^ p(\mathrm{d}V)$, $p>1$, the exponential equation does select a solution. By theorem 15.12.1, a weakly compact set of norming probabilities determines the limit: as the exponent tends to zero, the suitably shifted exponential solutions converge to the greatest subsolution of the critical equation that satisfies the integral normalizations given by these probabilities. In a Hermitian background the convergence is uniform; in an arbitrary smooth big background it holds in $L^1(X)$ and locally uniformly off the pole set of the strict barrier. The selected limit inherits the symmetries of the data (proposition 14.19.1), and in the projective example theorem 14.19.3 identifies it with the explicit symmetric $C^{1,1}$ solution of proposition 11.6.2. Selection thus coexists with nonuniqueness of the normalized critical equation. For the merely integrable radial densities on zero-lower-mass products, theorem 14.35.2 determines the whole supremum-normalized limit, even with joint variation of the density parameter and exponent. Scalar centering has a finite limit exactly below the moment boundary of proposition 14.35.3; at the boundary and above it diverges. For logarithmic product models with positive residual, theorem 15.13.3 also gives selection under independent variation of the reference coefficients, background, density and exponent; centering follows each varying critical scalar. Selection for arbitrary integrable data and general varying singular reference types remains open.

Dual attainment gives a different criterion. In dimension at least two, suppose that $f\neq 0$, that $b_\omega (f)>0$, and that $D_{f^{1/n}}$ has a smooth strictly positive maximizing Gauduchon current. Then the normalized threshold solution is unique, and the whole family of normalized solutions with positive exponent converges uniformly to it (corollary 26.9.6, corollary 26.9.7). The density of example 26.9.9 satisfies this condition although it vanishes on an open set. The size of the zero set alone therefore does not decide uniqueness; the dual condition also depends on the background.

For prescribed radial nonlogarithmic types, theorem 15.14.2 gives a different kind of regularity: a relative density error $O(r^\delta )$ controls the residual after the singularity is subtracted. The critical $C^1$ and $C^2$ thresholds are attained and are sharp by proposition 15.14.4. Positive cumulative central mass allows smooth crossing of density zeros, and proposition 15.14.5 gives an open zero annulus with a globally smooth residual. The product construction corollary 15.14.6 transfers this regularity under a zero-lower-mass factor hypothesis. These are radial criteria; they do not imply regularity or uniqueness for general nonsymmetric data.

33.2 Positive Gauduchon volume and bigness

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Fix a smooth strictly positive probability form $\mu $ on $X$. For a pseudoeffective smooth real $(1,1)$-form $\theta $, the Gauduchon variational volume $\mathcal V_\mu (\theta )=\lambda _\mu (\theta )^ n$ of definition 26.1.1 is an infimum over Gauduchon metrics, whereas bigness asks for one potential $u$ making $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ strictly positive. Bigness implies $\mathcal V_\mu (\theta )>0$ (corollary 26.1.4). The converse fails for nef nonclosed backgrounds: for every $n\geq 3$, theorem 26.13.2 constructs a smooth semipositive background $\theta $ on $\mathbb P^{n-1}\times \mathbb P^1$ with $\mathcal V_\mu (\theta )=n$ that is not big: no current $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ is bounded below by a positive multiple of a Hermitian form. In this example a radial potential compensates the determinant, while a closed test current carried by a degenerate fiber detects the missing strict positivity.

Positivity of $\mathcal V_\mu $ does not depend on the choice of $\mu $ (proposition 26.14.1), but the infimum over all such $\mu $ is a different invariant: by theorem 26.14.3 it is the lower mass, and it vanishes for every Hermitian form on the threefold of theorem 26.18.1, although Hermitian forms are big. In dimension one, positive volume and bigness are equivalent, since the volume is $\int _ X\theta $. For a closed nef class on a compact Kähler manifold, $\mathcal V_\mu $ is the top self-intersection, and the equivalence is the nef-and-big criterion recalled in [SGPT]. On a compact Kähler manifold, theorem 26.11.1 also gives bigness when a closed nef core of positive volume is supplemented by a semipositive form. Whether positive volume implies bigness for general nef nonclosed backgrounds on surfaces is open. In higher dimension, any such criterion requires geometric hypotheses that exclude the radial obstruction.

33.2.1 Collapse of normalized Gauduchon tests

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

The root volume and the bigness threshold divide the same pairing by different quantities. Positive volume can fail to imply bigness only if these denominators separate along tests approaching the threshold, and the following calculation isolates this degeneration. For the radial background of theorem 26.13.2, the normalized $(n-1)$-st powers of a family of balanced test metrics (definition 4.1.1) converge weakly to the integration current of the degenerate fiber (proposition 26.13.4). The volume-minimizing family approaches the bigness threshold only in dimension three, by proposition 26.13.3.

Fix a Hermitian form $h$ on $X$, and let $\theta $ be a smooth pseudoeffective real $(1,1)$-form. For a Gauduchon metric $\gamma $, abbreviate the pairings by

\[ A_\theta (\gamma )=\int _ X\theta \wedge \gamma ^{n-1}, \qquad A_ h(\gamma )=\int _ Xh\wedge \gamma ^{n-1}. \]

The threshold $\kappa _ h(\theta )$ of corollary 4.3.2 is the infimum of the quotient $A_\theta /A_ h$ over Gauduchon metrics, and it is positive exactly when $\theta $ is big. The root volume $\lambda _\mu (\theta )$ is the infimum of $A_\theta /D_\mu $, with the determinant denominator $D_\mu (\gamma )$ of definition 26.1.1.

Proposition 33.2.1. Let $h$, $\theta $ and $\mu $ be as above, and let $(\gamma _ j)$ be a sequence of Gauduchon metrics.

  1. Suppose $n\geq 2$, $\lambda _\mu (\theta )>0$, and $\theta $ is not big. If

    \[ A_ h(\gamma _ j)=1,\qquad A_\theta (\gamma _ j)\longrightarrow 0, \]

    then $D_\mu (\gamma _ j)\to 0$. Such sequences exist.

  2. More generally, fix a real number $\delta >0$. If $A_ h(\gamma _ j)=1$ and

    \[ A_\theta (\gamma _ j)\longrightarrow \kappa _ h(\theta ), \qquad \liminf _ jD_\mu (\gamma _ j)\geq \delta >0, \]

    then

    \[ \kappa _ h(\theta )\geq \delta \lambda _\mu (\theta ). \]

    In particular, positive root volume then implies bigness.

Proof. Suppose first that $\theta $ is not big. Since $\theta $ is pseudoeffective, the threshold criterion of corollary 4.3.2 gives $\kappa _ h(\theta )=0$, so there are Gauduchon metrics whose quotients $A_\theta /A_ h$ tend to zero. Multiplying a Gauduchon metric by a positive constant multiplies $A_\theta $, $A_ h$ and $D_\mu $ by the same factor; for $n\geq 2$ this factor can be chosen to make $A_ h=1$, without changing either quotient. This gives the sequences in the first assertion. By the definition of the root volume, every Gauduchon metric satisfies

33.1
\begin{equation} \lambda _\mu (\theta )D_\mu (\gamma )\leq A_\theta (\gamma ). \end{equation}

Dividing by the positive root volume proves the first assertion. Applying the same inequality to the sequence in the second assertion and taking lower limits gives $\delta \lambda _\mu (\theta )\leq \kappa _ h(\theta )$. If the root volume is positive, then $\kappa _ h(\theta )>0$, and the threshold criterion gives bigness. $\square$

The quotient $D_\mu /A_ h$ is always bounded above. Indeed, the smooth equation for $h$, or (33.1) with $\theta =h$, gives

\[ D_\mu (\gamma )\leq \lambda _\mu (h)^{-1}A_ h(\gamma ). \]

What is missing for the converse direction is a lower bound for $D_\mu $ along suitable sequences minimizing the threshold. Compactness of normalized potentials does not give compactness of these Gauduchon metrics.

In dimension one this normalization issue does not arise: the root volume is $\int _ X\theta $, and $\kappa _ h(\theta )=\int _ X\theta /\int _ Xh$. Positive volume and bigness are therefore equivalent by the same threshold criterion.

One way to rule out this collapse is to deform the test metrics. Solving a Gauduchon equation replaces the volume density of a test metric while keeping the Aeppli class of $\gamma ^{n-1}$. When $\theta $ is closed, this leaves the numerator $A_\theta $ unchanged. For nonclosed $\theta $, integration by parts leaves derivatives of the background, so such a deformation must also control the change of the pairing; this is the additional estimate needed to use the smooth equation against collapse.

33.3 Closed positivity and geometric equations

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

A closed Kähler current places the manifold in Fujiki's class $\mathcal C$, whereas a strictly positive current $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ in a nonclosed background keeps the torsion $\mathrm{d}\theta $ of that background. This distinction links the passage from positive volume to bigness with the geometry of the manifold, and it limits what a scalar potential equation can prove. Throughout this section $\mu $ is a smooth strictly positive probability form on $X$, and we use balanced and pluriclosed metrics in the sense of definition 4.1.1. The geometric results from the literature used below are stated, with their precise hypotheses and sources, in section 34.20.

33.3.1 Nef classes, bigness and the Fujiki class

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

A compact complex manifold belongs to Fujiki's class $\mathcal C$ exactly when it carries a closed Kähler current, that is, a closed positive $(1,1)$-current bounded below by a positive multiple of a Hermitian form. The transcendental Grauert–Riemenschneider conjecture asks whether a closed nef real $(1,1)$-class $\alpha $ with

\[ \int _ X\alpha ^ n>0 \]

is big. For such a class, bigness is equivalent to $X\in \mathcal C$; the precise statements and sources are given in section 34.20.

The obstruction can be expressed through Monge–Ampère mass. Under the same hypotheses on $\alpha $, one has

\[ \alpha \text{ is big}\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad v_+(h)<\infty \]

for a Hermitian metric $h$ on $X$, where $v_+(h)$ is the upper mass of definition 6.5.1. There is also a characterization of $\mathcal C$ by bounded mass together with positivity of an upper volume of some closed Bott–Chern class; that volume is different from the Gauduchon variational volume $\mathcal V_\mu $. Both results are stated in section 34.20.

The Hopf threefold fails bounded mass, but it is not a counterexample to the conjecture, since a counterexample would also need a closed nef class with positive top self-intersection. A proof of the conjecture must explain why this closed positivity forces bounded mass.

The elliptic threefold of theorem 26.18.1 is the complementary test case: it has bounded mass but no big closed class. By proposition 28.9.2, its nef cone is the nonnegative span of the classes of two smooth semipositive forms of rank one, and every top self-intersection vanishes. This example of zero lower mass therefore does not bear on the conjecture either. The explicit boundary calculation of proposition 28.9.5 describes its degeneration without identifying a nonclosed metric with a closed class.

The Gauduchon variational volume of definition 26.1.1 splits the conjecture into two possible steps:

33.2
\begin{equation} \int _ X\alpha ^ n>0 \quad \stackrel{?}{\Longrightarrow }\quad \mathcal V_\mu (\alpha )>0 \quad \stackrel{?}{\Longrightarrow }\quad \alpha \text{ is big}. \end{equation}

Neither implication is known on arbitrary compact complex manifolds. The second concerns closed nef backgrounds; the projective counterexample of theorem 26.13.2 has a nonclosed background and does not decide it. On a compact Kähler manifold both implications follow from the usual volume formula for nef classes and the classical nef-and-big criterion. Without closedness, positivity of $\mathcal V_\mu $ cannot characterize $\mathcal C$, since every Hermitian metric $h$ on every compact complex manifold satisfies $\mathcal V_\mu (h)>0$.

Problem 33.3.1. Let $\alpha $ be a smooth closed real $(1,1)$-form on $X$ whose class is nef with $\int _ X\alpha ^ n>0$, and let $h$ be a Hermitian form on $X$. Prove a positive lower bound for the root volume $\lambda _\mu (\alpha )$ of definition 26.1.1, and rule out the collapse of proposition 33.2.1 along a sequence of Gauduchon metrics minimizing the bigness threshold $\kappa _ h(\alpha )$.

The test metrics can be normalized by $\int _ Xh\wedge \gamma ^{n-1}=1$. By proposition 33.2.1, a positive lower bound for $D_\mu (\gamma )$ along one sequence minimizing the threshold would give the second implication. Solving a Gauduchon equation changes the volume density of a test metric while keeping the Aeppli class of $\gamma ^{n-1}$; since $\alpha $ is closed, it also keeps the pairing with $\alpha $. The missing estimate is control of the trace normalization and of the determinant denominator after this change. Prescribing a volume density alone does not give this estimate.

33.3.2 Positive lower mass and concentration

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Positive lower mass is an integrability property of the Gauduchon tests at an endpoint exponent. It is independent of finite upper mass: the threefold of theorem 26.18.1 has $v_-(h)=0$ for every Hermitian metric and carries a balanced metric of finite upper mass, while the normalized Hopf metric has $v_-=6$ and $v_+=\infty $.

Let $n\geq 2$, let $h$ be a Hermitian form and $\mathrm{d}V$ a smooth positive volume form. Recall the set $\mathscr G_ h$ of normalized Gauduchon currents (section 4.4) and the determinant measure $\Gamma _0$ of (26.39), and write

\[ \Gamma _0(T)=k_ T\, \mathrm{d}V,\qquad T\in \mathscr G_ h. \]

By theorem 26.8.2,

33.3
\begin{equation} v_-(h)>0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \sup _{T\in \mathscr G_ h} \| k_ T\| _{L^{n/(n-1)}(\mathrm{d}V)}<\infty , \end{equation}

and the exact value of the supremum is $v_-(h)^{-1/n}$, including the value $+\infty $. Uniform estimates for every smaller exponent $1<q<n/(n-1)$ hold without either mass property. In the example of zero lower mass, the endpoint estimate fails already for smooth strictly positive tests. Positivity, the condition $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T=0$ and the trace normalization alone therefore do not control concentration at the endpoint exponent.

Problem 33.3.2. Let $n\geq 2$ and let $h$ be a Hermitian form on $X$. Find geometric conditions, beyond the metric identities in corollary 26.17.1, that imply the endpoint estimate in (33.3), and explain how they exclude the degeneration of zero lower mass constructed in section 26.18.

Corollary 26.17.1 proves the endpoint bound when some Hermitian metric $g$ satisfies $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}g=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(g^2)=0$, which includes all compact Kähler manifolds and all compact complex surfaces. For a fixed smooth strictly positive density, theorem 26.6.2 gives a smooth maximizing current with bounded determinant density. However, proposition 26.17.2 produces maximizing sequences in fixed Kähler classes whose moments diverge at every exponent above the endpoint. Exact maximizers and maximizing sequences therefore require different estimates.

For principal elliptic threefolds over Kähler surfaces, theorem 26.16.3 gives a geometric sufficient condition: the sum of the squares of the two real curvature forms is nonnegative and not identically zero. A bounded cubic identity then gives positive lower mass, and the determinant bound transfers to every Hermitian background. These manifolds admit no pluriclosed metric; proposition 26.16.5, proposition 26.16.6 give explicit families. A primitive pair of curvature forms has the opposite sign, by proposition 26.16.4, so this criterion leaves the general problem open.

The balanced condition and bounded upper mass do not suffice, even together, as the threefold of theorem 26.18.1 shows. On this elliptic fibration, the identity (26.114) relates the failure of the lower bound to a vertical Dirichlet integral, and the horizontal curvature forms $\alpha ,\beta $ of section 26.18 have a positive exact product $\alpha \wedge \beta $, which excludes pluriclosed metrics and closed Kähler currents. These calculations give geometric information about this example, but not a classification of all degenerations of the lower mass.

For equations the consequence is exact: in a Hermitian background, corollary 14.26.4 characterizes positive lower mass by solvability of the normalized equation for all finite non-pluripolar measures. When the lower mass vanishes, even a positive density in $L^1$ bounded away from zero can admit no positive scaled subsolution. The remaining questions about equations concern specified measures or additional geometric hypotheses, and, separately, the effect of the exponential weight.

33.3.3 Primitive elliptic bundles

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

On a primitive elliptic bundle, the variation of the top-degree mass is a vertical Dirichlet integral. This gives the exact upper mass and a positive lower bound for separated potentials. The lower mass over all potentials remains a geometric question, since the collapse construction over a product of curves does not transfer directly to a base without curves.

Let $(B,\eta )$ be a connected compact Kähler surface and let $L\to B$ be a holomorphic line bundle such that

\[ \int _ Bc_1(L)\wedge \eta =0. \]

Choose a smooth Hermitian metric on $L$ whose curvature is primitive with respect to $\eta $. Such a metric exists: for an arbitrary metric, the trace of the curvature with respect to $\eta $ has mean zero by the condition above, and the scalar Poisson equation gives a smooth change of metric that cancels this trace. Let $c$ be the resulting smooth closed curvature representative of $-c_1(L)$, so that $\eta \wedge c=0$. Write $\pi :L\to B$ for the bundle projection and $w$ for a nonzero vector in a fiber, with norm taken in the chosen metric. On the complement $L^\times $ of the zero section, put

\[ t=\log |w|^2,\qquad \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}t=\pi ^*c. \]

For $q>1$, form the quotient

\[ X=L^\times /\langle w\mapsto qw\rangle , \qquad \pi :X\longrightarrow B, \qquad T=2\log q, \qquad A=\int _ B\eta ^2, \]

where we use the same symbol $\pi $ for the induced projection. The action increases $t$ by $T$. It is free and properly discontinuous, and the compact annulus $1\leq |w|\leq q$ meets every orbit. Hence $X$ is a connected compact complex threefold; in this subsection it replaces the general manifold of the chapter. Over a trivializing open set $U\subset B$, a local holomorphic frame of $L$ identifies the fibration with $U\times (\mathbb C^*/q^{\mathbb Z})$. Since $\mathrm{d}t$ and $\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}t$ are invariant under the action, they descend to $X$, and we set

\[ D=\mathrm{d}t\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}t,\qquad \omega =\pi ^*\eta +D. \]

The form $D$ has rank one; it is semipositive and positive on the vertical line, and its kernel projects isomorphically onto the tangent space of $B$. Hence $\omega $ is Hermitian.

Proposition 33.3.3. The metric $\omega $ is balanced and satisfies

\[ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega =-\pi ^*(c^2)\geq 0,\qquad \int _ X\omega ^3=3TA. \]

For every smooth real $u$,

33.4
\begin{equation} \int _ X(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^3 =3TA-3\int _ X\mathrm{d}u\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}u\wedge \bigl (-\pi ^*(c^2)\bigr ). \end{equation}

In particular $v_+(\omega )=3TA$, and the canonical bundles satisfy $K_ X\simeq \pi ^*K_ B$. If $c_1(L)\ne 0$ in real cohomology, $X$ admits neither a pluriclosed metric nor a closed Kähler current. If $c_1(L)=0$ in real cohomology, the chosen $\omega $ is Kähler.

Proof. Write $a=\mathrm{d}t$ and $b=\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}t$. Then $\mathrm{d}a=0$, $\mathrm{d}b=\pi ^*c$, $\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}a=-\pi ^*c$ and $\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}b=0$. Consequently

\[ D^2=0,\qquad \mathrm{d}D=-a\wedge \pi ^*c, \qquad \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}D=-\pi ^*(c^2), \]
\[ \mathrm{d}(\omega ^2)=-2\pi ^*(\eta \wedge c)\wedge a=0, \]

so $\omega $ is balanced. Since $c$ is primitive, its eigenvalues relative to $\eta $ are $\lambda $ and $-\lambda $ for a function $\lambda \geq 0$, so $c^2=-\lambda ^2\eta ^2$. This proves the asserted sign of $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega $.

To compute the volume, fix an elliptic fiber $E=\pi ^{-1}(b_0)$, where $b_0\in B$. In a logarithmic fiber coordinate $x+\mathrm{i}y$, the real periods are $\log q$ and $2\pi $, while $t$ equals $2x$ up to an additive constant and $D|_ E=\pi ^{-1}\mathrm{d}x\wedge \mathrm{d}y$. Thus $\int _ ED=T$. Since $\omega ^3=3\pi ^*(\eta ^2)\wedge D$, the volume formula follows.

For the mass identity, expand the cube in (33.4). The terms with one and with three factors $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ integrate to zero, because $\omega ^2$ is closed and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ is exact. For the remaining term, put $S=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega $. Integration by parts gives

\[ \int _ X(\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^2\wedge \omega =\int _ Xu\, \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\wedge S =-\int _ X\mathrm{d}u\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}u\wedge S, \]

where the last equality uses $\mathrm{d}S=0$. This proves the identity. Its last integral is nonnegative, and $u=0$ attains the upper bound $3TA$. Since smooth and bounded potentials have the same mass extrema, by theorem 26.14.3, this proves $v_+(\omega )=3TA$.

If $c_1(L)=0$ in real cohomology, then $c$ is exact and Stokes' theorem gives $\int _ Bc^2=0$. The pointwise identity $c^2=-\lambda ^2\eta ^2$ then forces $c=0$, and $\omega $ is closed. Otherwise $S=-\pi ^*(c^2)$ is nonzero, smooth and semipositive. For a pluriclosed metric $\gamma $, Stokes' theorem would give

\[ 0<\int _ X\gamma \wedge S =\int _ X\omega \wedge \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\gamma =0, \]

a contradiction. Likewise, a closed positive current $R\geq \varepsilon \omega $ with $\varepsilon >0$ would give $0<\int _ XR\wedge S=\int _ X\omega \wedge \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}R=0$. All these pairings are pairings of currents with smooth forms.

Finally, let $w$ be a local holomorphic fiber coordinate given by a frame of $L$, and let $\sigma $ be a local holomorphic two-form on $B$. The form $\pi ^*\sigma \wedge \mathrm{d}w/w$ is invariant under the quotient action. Changing the frame of $L$ adds to $\mathrm{d}w/w$ a horizontal one-form, whose wedge product with $\pi ^*\sigma $ vanishes. These local sections of $K_ X$ therefore have the transition functions of $\pi ^*K_ B$, which proves $K_ X\simeq \pi ^*K_ B$. $\square$

For separated potentials, positivity bounds both the amplitude and the downward slope of the vertical derivative. The sharp variance bound under these two constraints determines how much mass such a potential can lose. For $M,T>0$, set

\[ F(M,T)= \begin{cases} T^2/12,& T\leq 2M,\\ M^2-4M^3/(3T),& T\geq 2M. \end{cases} \]

Lemma 33.3.4. If a smooth real $T$-periodic function $p$ has mean zero, $|p|<M$ and $p'>-1$, then

\[ \frac1T\int _0^ T p^2\, \mathrm{d}s<F(M,T). \]

The supremum of the left side over this class is $F(M,T)$.

Proof. For $p=0$ the inequality holds because $F(M,T)>0$. Otherwise set $a=\max p>0$, $-b=\min p<0$ and $r=a+b$. Integrating $-p'<1$ over a forward interval $I$ from a maximum to the next minimum gives $r<|I|\leq T$; also $r<2M$, since $|p|<M$. The function $G(s)=(a-s)(s+b)$ is nonnegative on the range of $p$. Since $p$ has mean zero and $-p'<1$, integration over $I$ gives

\[ abT-\int _0^ T p^2\, \mathrm{d}s =\int _0^ T G(p)\, \mathrm{d}s \geq -\int _ I G(p)p'\, \mathrm{d}s =\frac{r^3}{6}. \]

As $ab\leq r^2/4$, the average of $p^2$ is at most $r^2/4-r^3/(6T)$. This expression increases strictly for $0<r<T$, and its value at $r=\min \{ 2M,T\} $ is $F(M,T)$. Since $r<\min \{ 2M,T\} $, the strict inequality follows.

For sharpness, consider the following $T$-periodic profiles $p_0$. When $T\geq 2M$, let $p_0$ be constant equal to $M$ and to $-M$ on two intervals of length $(T-2M)/2$ each, joined by a descent of slope $-1$ and length $2M$, and close the period by an upward jump. When $T\leq 2M$, let $p_0(s)=T/2-s$ for $0\leq s<T$, with its upward jump at the end of the period. In both cases $p_0$ has mean zero, $|p_0|\leq M$, $p_0'\geq -1$ in the sense of distributions, and the average of $p_0^2$ is $F(M,T)$; both constructions apply when $T=2M$. Convolution with a nonnegative periodic approximate identity preserves the mean and the bound on the distributional derivative, and multiplication by $1-\varepsilon $ makes both inequalities strict. As the convolution scale and $\varepsilon $ tend to zero, the resulting smooth functions converge to $p_0$ in $L^2$, which proves that the supremum is $F(M,T)$. $\square$

Proposition 33.3.5. Suppose $c_1(L)\ne 0$ in real cohomology, and put

\[ \kappa =-\frac{\int _ Bc^2}{A}>0. \]

Every smooth potential $u=f(t)+\pi ^*v$ with $f$ of period $T$, $v\in C^\infty (B,\mathbb R)$ and $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u>0$ satisfies

\[ \int _ X(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^3 >3TA\bigl (1-\kappa F(\kappa ^{-1/2},T)\bigr )>0. \]

Let $\lambda \geq 0$ be the nonnegative eigenvalue function of $c$ relative to $\eta $, so $c^2=-\lambda ^2\eta ^2$, and put $M_0=1/\max _ B\lambda $. For the subclass $v=0$, the exact mass infimum is

\[ 3TA\bigl (1-\kappa F(M_0,T)\bigr )>0. \]

It is approached by smooth potentials $f(t)$ with $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f(t)>0$, and it is not attained by such potentials.

Proof. Put $p=f'$. The chain rule gives

\[ \omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u =\pi ^*(\eta +pc+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v)+(1+p')D. \]

Positivity on the vertical line implies $p'>-1$. Over every point of $B$, the function $t$ takes every value modulo $T$, so restriction to $\ker \partial t$ shows that $\eta +pc+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v$ is a Kähler form on $B$ for every value of $p$. Its square has integral

\[ \int _ B(\eta +pc+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v)^2=A(1-\kappa p^2)>0, \]

since primitivity removes the mixed term and Stokes' theorem removes the exact terms. Hence $|p|<\kappa ^{-1/2}$.

Only the vertical derivative enters (33.4), because $-\pi ^*(c^2)$ has top degree in the base directions. For a $T$-periodic function $F_0$, integration over a fiber gives $\int _ E F_0(t)D=\int _0^ T F_0(s)\, \mathrm{d}s$, and therefore

\[ \int _ X(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^3 =3TA\left(1-\frac\kappa T\int _0^ T p^2\, \mathrm{d}s\right). \]

Now apply lemma 33.3.4 with $M=\kappa ^{-1/2}$. The formula for $F$ gives $F(M,T)<M^2$, which proves positivity of the bound.

For $v=0$, diagonalizing $c$ with respect to $\eta $ shows that the horizontal positivity condition is exactly $|p|<M_0$; together with $p'>-1$ it also suffices for positivity of the full form. Moreover $\kappa $ is the $\eta ^2$-average of $\lambda ^2$, so $\kappa \leq M_0^{-2}$. Lemma 33.3.4 with $M=M_0$ therefore proves the claimed positive lower bound and its nonattainment. The smooth approximating functions in its proof have mean zero and hence periodic primitives $f_\varepsilon $, and the potentials $f_\varepsilon (t)$ prove that the bound is sharp. $\square$

The proof of the first bound does not show that it is sharp for separated potentials over an arbitrary base: positivity of the classes $[\eta ]+p[c]$ alone does not provide one function $v$ that makes all the representatives $\eta +pc+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v$ positive. For the threefold over $\mathbb P^1\times \mathbb P^1$ of section 26.18, where $A=2$, $\kappa =1$ and $T=4\pi $, the bound is $8$ and it is sharp.

A base without curves rules out the averages of horizontal curves used in the known collapse example. The following construction uses the period theorem for K3 surfaces and the description of their Kähler cone [Theorems 7.4.1 and 8.5.2 of the online version, HuyK3].

Example 33.3.6. There is a K3 surface $B$ with $\operatorname {Pic}(B)=\mathbb Z[L]$, $c_1(L)^2=-4$, and a Kähler form $\eta $ orthogonal to $c_1(L)$. It has no compact curves. The associated $X$ has trivial canonical bundle, a balanced metric of finite upper mass, and no pluriclosed metric or closed Kähler current. It has no divisors, and every compact irreducible curve is an elliptic fiber.

Proof. In a hyperbolic plane of the K3 lattice, choose a basis $e,f$ with $e^2=f^2=0$ and $e\cdot f=1$, and put $\ell =e-2f$. This vector is primitive and has square $-4$. Choose a period in $\ell ^\perp $ outside the hyperplanes perpendicular to integral vectors not in $\mathbb Z\ell $. Such a period exists by Baire's theorem: the period domain in $\ell ^\perp $, of signature $(3,18)$, is nonempty and each excluded hyperplane is proper. The period theorem and the description of integral $(1,1)$-classes give the asserted Picard group. An irreducible curve would have nonzero class, because its Kähler area is positive, and hence square $-4m^2<-2$ for some integer $m\neq 0$, contradicting adjunction. Thus there are no curves.

The orthogonal complement of $\ell $ in $H^{1,1}(B,\mathbb R)$ has signature $(1,18)$. Choose a class of positive square there, with sign chosen so that it lies in the component of the positive cone containing the Kähler classes. Since there are no rational curves, the Kähler-cone theorem shows that this class is Kähler; choose $\eta $ in it. Proposition 33.3.3 then gives the assertions about the metric and the canonical bundle.

If a divisor $Z\subset X$ dominated $B$, the push-forward of its integration current would be $\pi _*[Z]=m$ for a positive generic degree $m$. Stokes' theorem and the projection formula would then give

\[ 0=\int _ X[Z]\wedge \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega =m\int _ B(-c^2)>0. \]

Thus no divisor dominates $B$. The image of any other irreducible divisor would be a curve or a point; the base has no curves, and a fiber has dimension one, so both alternatives are impossible. Likewise, the image of an irreducible compact curve is a point, and the curve is therefore an entire elliptic fiber. $\square$

Put $V=3TA$. The root volumes of complementary tests can be computed explicitly for one radial family. For smooth real $T$-periodic functions $a,g$, consider

\[ \Gamma =a(t)\eta ^2+g(t)\eta \wedge D. \]

Proposition 33.3.7. The form $\Gamma $ is strictly positive and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$-closed exactly when $a$ is a positive constant and $g>0$. For such a form put $\bar g=T^{-1}\int _0^ Tg(s)\, \mathrm ds$ and $d_\Gamma =\int _ X\omega \wedge \Gamma $. Its positive square root and volume are

\[ \gamma =\sqrt a\, \eta +\frac{g(t)}{2\sqrt a}D, \qquad \int _ X\gamma ^3=\frac32TA\sqrt a\, \bar g \leq \frac{d_\Gamma ^{3/2}}{\sqrt V}. \]

Equality holds exactly when $\bar g=2a$, without requiring $g$ to be constant. In particular every degree-one test in this family has root volume at most $V^{-1/2}$.

Proof. Since $\eta \wedge c=0$, the form $g\eta \wedge D$ is closed. The chain rule gives

\[ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(a\eta ^2)=a''D\wedge \eta ^2, \]

the $a'c\wedge \eta ^2$ term having excess base degree. Thus $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\Gamma =0$ forces $a''=0$, and periodicity makes $a$ constant. In a positive coframe with $\eta =\sigma _1+\sigma _2$, $D=\sigma _3$, the complementary coefficient matrix of $\Gamma $ is $\operatorname {diag}(g,g,2a)$. Hence positivity is exactly $a,g>0$. The displayed $\gamma $ is positive and squares to $\Gamma $. Multiplication and fiber integration give

\[ d_\Gamma =TA(a+\bar g),\qquad \gamma ^3=\tfrac 32\sqrt a\, g\eta ^2\wedge D. \]

For fixed $d_\Gamma >0$ set $s=d_\Gamma /(TA)$. Then $0<a<s$, $\bar g=s-a$, and $\sqrt a(s-a)$ has its unique maximum at $a=s/3$. Substitution proves the bound and its equality condition. $\square$

For the divisor-free threefold of example 33.3.6, theorem 4.5.1 gives divergent transverse pairings against normalized Gauduchon tests. The radial family above has bounded root volume at fixed degree, even when $g$ concentrates arbitrarily strongly. Large transverse pairings do not by themselves give unbounded root volumes or a sequence of potentials with masses tending to zero.

33.3.4 Null loci and the singularities of the Chern–Ricci flow

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Return to the general manifold $X$ of dimension $n$. Let $\omega _0$ be a Hermitian metric, taken as the initial metric of the Chern–Ricci flow, and suppose that the flow has a finite maximal time $\tau $. With the Ricci convention of corollary 26.20.4, the limiting background $\theta _\tau =\omega _0-\tau \operatorname {Ric}(\omega _0)$ is nef. To identify the region where the flow converges smoothly as the complement of a null locus, one first needs bigness of $\theta _\tau $. The dual currents detect the failure of strict positivity, but the degeneration of their determinants does not by itself locate an analytic exceptional set.

For a big smooth background $\theta $, the non-Hermitian locus $E_{\mathrm{nH}}(\theta )$ is the intersection of the singular sets of the strict currents $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$, those bounded below by a positive multiple of a Hermitian form, whose potentials have almost analytic singularities; one such current attains the intersection. Here a potential has almost analytic singularities if it is locally a positive multiple of the logarithm of the norm of an ideal up to a bounded remainder, and this remainder becomes smooth after subtracting the divisorial logarithms on a suitable log resolution. This is the class of singularities used in the theorem stated in section 34.20.

For nef and big $\theta $, define

\[ \operatorname {Null}(\theta ) =\bigcup _{\substack {V\subset X\text{ irreducible analytic}\\ \dim V>0,\ \theta |_ V\text{ not big}}}V, \]

where bigness on a singular subvariety has its intrinsic analytic meaning. By the theorem recalled in section 34.20, the equality $E_{\mathrm{nH}}(\theta )=\operatorname {Null}(\theta )$ holds for nef and big smooth backgrounds on arbitrary compact complex manifolds. The numerical criterion recalled there has additional hypotheses: bounded mass on $X$ and positive lower volume on its positive-dimensional submanifolds.

The flow theorem recalled in section 34.20 identifies the finite-time singularities of the Chern–Ricci flow with the null locus under positive limiting lower volume, for arbitrary initial metrics in dimension two and for Guan–Li initial metrics in higher dimensions, that is, those with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega _0=0$ and $\mathrm{d}\omega _0\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}\omega _0=0$. Positivity of $\mathcal V_\mu $ cannot replace these hypotheses, as the example below shows.

The calculation with currents in corollary 26.20.4 applies to arbitrary initial metrics. When $\tau <\infty $, there are normalized Gauduchon currents $T$ annihilating $\theta _\tau $:

\[ \int _ X\theta _\tau \wedge T=0. \]

If $\mathcal V_\mu (\theta _\tau )>0$, the determinant-weighted measures of all such currents vanish, by proposition 26.20.2. This is a precise statement of degeneracy, but it does not imply that their traces concentrate on analytic sets, since a smooth positive semidefinite matrix can have deficient rank on a whole open set.

Theorem 26.20.5 shows that positivity of the variational volume does not imply bigness at the end of the flow: in every dimension at least three, there is a Chern–Ricci flow with finite maximal time whose limiting background is nef, not big, and has positive $\mathcal V_\mu $. Thus being the endpoint of a flow does not exclude the radial obstruction. In this model, proposition 26.20.6 shows that every normalized annihilating current has its trace supported on the degenerate fiber, with a vertical coefficient matrix of rank one. This support follows from the explicit semipositive representative, not from the vanishing of determinants alone.

If $\theta _\tau $ is big, whether by assumption or under additional hypotheses, the parabolic theorem recalled in section 34.20 gives convergence as currents and smooth local convergence off $E_{\mathrm{nH}}(\theta _\tau )$. Combined with nefness and the null-locus theorem, this identifies the analytic exceptional set. The example above does not contradict this conditional theorem, but any further numerical or geometric criterion must impose enough structure to exclude its nonbig endpoint. The local continuity of envelopes and the homogeneous replacement arguments of theorem 14.7.1 provide an elliptic foundation away from the poles of a strict barrier; the parabolic estimates are a separate input.

Completeness of the limiting metric requires an additional estimate. In theorem 28.14.1, the metric defined by the unique finite-energy solution of a divisorial exponential equation in a big closed class is incomplete on every nonempty connected open subset of its ample locus, because its minimal divisorial singularity cancels the prescribed pole. Nefness alone does not help: theorem 28.14.2 keeps a nonintegrable pole with smooth positive coefficient and a complete cusp, but has a disjoint null curve at finite distance; its solution has zero Lelong numbers and finite energy, but is not of minimal ordinary type. The construction of theorem 28.14.4 with compatible initial data also gives an incomplete auxiliary metric, with the exact identities for the resolution and the prescribed volume, but its class is not nef. Simultaneous nefness and compatibility remain a separate question, and none of these elliptic examples decides whether the limits of an evolving flow are complete; see section 34.17.

For integrable poles weakened by logarithmic factors, corollary 28.15.2 gives a sufficient condition for completeness in terms of the density, the potential and the tangential determinant; the proof controls normal lengths and applies in arbitrary Hermitian backgrounds. The torus models of example 28.15.4, example 28.15.5 attain its critical exponent and show that it is sharp, even for a Kähler background and full mass. Their pole coefficients need not extend smoothly and positively across the divisor, so the geometric smoothing question remains separate.

The regularity of the residual also matters. By theorem 1.18.1, a Hölder residual on a compact manifold that defines a smooth positive metric off its analytic singular set puts that set at finite distance. Continuity alone does not suffice: the prescribed radial densities of theorem 28.12.2 have bounded continuous minimal remainders and complete regular ends for the range $n<d\leq 2n$ of the exponent $d$ in that theorem. These examples concern the specified densities; they say nothing about compatibility with an evolving Chern–Ricci flow.

33.3.5 Balanced and pluriclosed metrics

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

The balanced–pluriclosed conjecture asks whether a compact complex manifold carrying both a balanced metric $\beta $ and a pluriclosed metric $\omega $ must be Kähler. The two metrics may differ; a single metric satisfying both conditions is already Kähler by lemma 33.3.10. The source of the conjecture and its formulation through a flow are given in section 34.20.

A manifold in Fujiki's class $\mathcal C$ that carries a pluriclosed metric is Kähler, by the result recalled there. It would therefore suffice to construct a closed Kähler current from the two metrics. The scalar potential equation alone cannot produce the required closedness.

Indeed, for every distributional potential $u$,

\[ \mathrm{d}(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)=\mathrm{d}\omega . \]

Thus if the pluriclosed metric is not closed, no potential in its background makes it Kähler; the finite-volume equation, including its smooth solution, does not change this differential datum. Similarly, the Gauduchon metric in a conformal class need not be balanced. Positivity and a scalar normalization cannot replace either missing closedness condition.

The closed form $\beta ^{n-1}$ pairs trivially with exact real two-forms, so it can give a conserved trace along a flow. When $c_1(X)=0$ in real cohomology, the flow conjecture recalled in section 34.20 proposes that, in the presence of a balanced metric, the pluriclosed flow exists for all time and converges to a Kähler metric. The invariant examples cited there use the conserved trace. For general metrics, the analytic difficulty is to turn this integral into a pointwise bound.

Parallel torsion gives a setting in which the integral constraint of a balanced trace becomes pointwise. Suppose $n\geq 2$, write $J$ for the complex structure of $X$, let $g$ be a Hermitian metric on $X$, and let $D$ be a real connection on $TX$ that preserves $g$ and $J$ and has parallel torsion $T^ D$:

\[ Dg=0,\qquad DJ=0,\qquad DT^ D=0, \qquad T^ D(U,V)=D_ UV-D_ VU-[U,V]. \]

The balanced and pluriclosed metrics considered below need not equal $g$. Write

\[ E_ D^ k=\{ \alpha \in \Omega ^ k(X,\mathbb R):D\alpha =0\} \]

for the space of $D$-parallel real $k$-forms. Since $X$ is connected, evaluation at one point is injective on this space, so it is finite-dimensional. Its complexification inherits the decomposition by bidegree because $DJ=0$.

Lemma 33.3.9. There is a linear projection $S:\Omega ^\bullet (X,\mathbb R)\to E_ D^\bullet $ with $S\mathrm d=\mathrm dS$. Its complexification preserves bidegree and commutes with $\partial $ and $\bar\partial $. It preserves strict positivity in bidegrees $(1,1)$ and $(n-1,n-1)$.

Proof. Step 1: the projection. For a parallel form $\alpha $, the formula for exterior differentiation in terms of a connection expresses $\mathrm d\alpha $ as an algebraic contraction of $T^ D$ with $\alpha $. Since $DT^ D=0$, it follows that $\mathrm dE_ D^ k\subseteq E_ D^{k+1}$. The Hodge star of $g$ is also parallel and maps $E_ D^ k$ onto $E_ D^{2n-k}$. Consequently the pairing

\[ E_ D^ k\times E_ D^{2n-k}\longrightarrow \mathbb R, \qquad (\alpha ,\beta )\longmapsto \int _ X\alpha \wedge \beta \]

is nondegenerate: pairing a nonzero $\alpha $ with $*_ g\alpha $ gives its squared $L^2$ norm, which is positive. Hence every smooth real $k$-form $\alpha $ determines a unique $S_ k\alpha \in E_ D^ k$ such that

33.7
\begin{equation} \int _ X S_ k\alpha \wedge \beta =\int _ X\alpha \wedge \beta \qquad (\beta \in E_ D^{2n-k}). \end{equation}

This defines a projection onto $E_ D^ k$. For $\gamma \in E_ D^{2n-k-1}$ we have $\mathrm d\gamma \in E_ D^{2n-k}$, so Stokes' theorem gives

\[ \int _ X\mathrm dS_ k\alpha \wedge \gamma =(-1)^{k+1}\int _ X\alpha \wedge \mathrm d\gamma =\int _ X S_{k+1}(\mathrm d\alpha )\wedge \gamma . \]

Both forms being compared are parallel, so nondegeneracy proves $\mathrm dS_ k\alpha =S_{k+1}\mathrm d\alpha $.

Step 2: positivity. Fix $p\in X$. Let $H$ be the closure of the full $D$-holonomy group at $p$, a compact subgroup of the unitary group, with probability Haar measure $m_ H$. For a path from $p$ to $x$, write $P_ x:T_ pX\to T_ xX$ for parallel transport along it. The average

\[ R_\alpha (x)=\int _ H h^*(P_ x^*\alpha _ x)\, \mathrm dm_ H(h) \]

is independent of the path, since a change of path acts by an element of $H$. It is an $H$-invariant tensor at $p$ depending smoothly on $x$, because paths can be chosen smoothly on each coordinate neighborhood. Average it over $X$:

\[ a_ p=\frac1{\operatorname {Vol}_ g(X)} \int _ X R_\alpha (x)\, \mathrm dV_ g(x). \]

Being $H$-invariant, the tensor $a_ p$ extends uniquely to a global parallel form $\widetilde S_ k\alpha $. For parallel $\beta $, write $\alpha \wedge \beta =f\, \mathrm dV_ g$. Since parallel transport is unitary and $\beta $ is invariant under holonomy, averaging gives

\[ R_\alpha (x)\wedge \beta _ p=f(x)\, \mathrm dV_{g,p}. \]

Integrating this equality shows that $\widetilde S_ k\alpha $ satisfies (33.7); hence $\widetilde S_ k=S_ k$.

Every parallel transport and every holonomy element preserves reality, bidegree and the positive cones. A smooth strictly positive $(q,q)$-form, for $q=1$ or $n-1$, is bounded below by a positive multiple of the $q$th power of the fundamental form of $g$, uniformly on the compact manifold, and both probability averages preserve that lower bound. Therefore $S$ preserves strict positivity and bidegree. Comparing bidegrees in $S\mathrm d=\mathrm dS$ proves that $S$ commutes with $\partial $ and $\bar\partial $. $\square$

The last step of the construction uses the fact, mentioned above, that a metric which is both balanced and pluriclosed is Kähler.

Lemma 33.3.10. If a Hermitian form $\zeta $ satisfies $\mathrm d\zeta ^{n-1}=0$ and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\zeta =0$, then $\mathrm d\zeta =0$.

Proof. For $n=2$, the balanced condition is the assertion $\mathrm d\zeta =0$. Suppose $n\geq 3$ and set $\alpha =\partial \zeta $. Since $\zeta $ is balanced, $\alpha \wedge \zeta ^{n-2}=0$, so $\alpha $ is a primitive $(2,1)$-form. Applying $\partial \bar\partial $ to the balanced power gives

\[ 0=\partial \bar\partial \zeta ^{n-1} =(n-1)\partial \bar\partial \zeta \wedge \zeta ^{n-2} +(n-1)(n-2)\alpha \wedge \bar\alpha \wedge \zeta ^{n-3}. \]

The first term vanishes because $\zeta $ is pluriclosed. For a primitive $(2,1)$-form, pointwise Hermitian linear algebra gives

\[ -\mathrm i\, \alpha \wedge \bar\alpha \wedge \frac{\zeta ^{n-3}}{(n-3)!} =|\alpha |_\zeta ^2\frac{\zeta ^ n}{n!}. \]

Thus $\alpha =0$, and since $\zeta $ is real, also $\bar\partial \zeta =0$, so $\mathrm d\zeta =0$. $\square$

Theorem 33.3.11. Let $D$ be a connection on $TX$ as above, preserving $g$ and $J$ and with parallel torsion. If $X$ admits a balanced metric and a pluriclosed metric, then it admits a $D$-parallel Kähler metric.

Proof. Let $\beta $ be a balanced metric and $\omega $ a pluriclosed metric. By lemma 33.3.9, $S(\beta ^{n-1})$ is strictly positive, parallel and closed. It has a unique smooth positive $(n-1)$-st root $B$. Indeed, after the fixed normalization of wedge powers, the corresponding map on matrices is $G\mapsto C=(\det G)G^{-1}$, whose inverse is

\[ G=(\det C)^{1/(n-1)}C^{-1}. \]

By uniqueness the root is invariant under parallel transport. Thus $DB=0$ and $\mathrm dB^{n-1}=0$. Similarly, $P=S\omega $ is a parallel positive pluriclosed form.

Consider the finite-dimensional space of real parallel directions of exact type

\[ \mathcal A_ D=\{ (\mathrm d\xi )^{1,1}:\xi \in E_ D^1\} . \]

Every such direction is $\partial \bar\partial $-closed, since

\[ (\mathrm d\xi )^{1,1}=\partial \xi ^{0,1}+\bar\partial \xi ^{1,0}. \]

For a parallel positive form $Q$, the volume functional has derivative

33.8
\begin{equation} \left.\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\right|_{t=0} \frac1{n!}\int _ X(Q+t(\mathrm d\xi )^{1,1})^ n =\frac1{(n-1)!}\int _ X\xi \wedge \mathrm dQ^{n-1}, \end{equation}

because the $(2,0)$ and $(0,2)$ parts of $\mathrm d\xi $ pair to zero with $Q^{n-1}$. Since $\mathrm dQ^{n-1}$ is parallel, nondegeneracy of the parallel pairing shows that all these derivatives vanish exactly when $Q$ is balanced.

Apply this to the balanced form $B$. For $a\in \mathcal A_ D$, the function $\operatorname {tr}_ B a$ is parallel and hence constant. Its integral against $B^ n$ vanishes by (33.8), so it vanishes identically. Therefore every form in the positive affine slice

\[ \mathcal P=\{ Q\in P+\mathcal A_ D:Q>0\} \]

has the same pointwise trace $\operatorname {tr}_ B Q=\operatorname {tr}_ B P=:C>0$.

Evaluate the parallel forms at one point. The positive eigenvalues of $Q\in \mathcal P$ relative to $B$ sum to $C$, so they are bounded above by $C$. Thus $\mathcal P$ is bounded in its finite-dimensional affine space. Its closure is

\[ \overline{\mathcal P}=\{ Q\in P+\mathcal A_ D:Q\geq 0\} , \]

because $(1-t)Q+tP$ is positive for $0<t\leq 1$ whenever $Q$ is semipositive; in particular, this closure is compact. Since both forms are parallel, the relative eigenvalues and the relative determinant are constant on $X$. Hence

\[ \frac1{n!}\int _ XQ^ n =\det (B^{-1}Q)\frac1{n!}\int _ XB^ n. \]

At a relative boundary point of $\overline{\mathcal P}$ the semipositive matrix is singular, so this volume is zero, whereas it is positive at $P$. A maximum of the volume on the compact set $\overline{\mathcal P}$ therefore lies in $\mathcal P$.

At this interior maximum all the derivatives in (33.8) vanish, so the maximizing form is balanced; it is pluriclosed because it lies in $P+\mathcal A_ D$. By lemma 33.3.10 it is closed. Since it is positive and $D$-parallel, this proves the theorem. $\square$

The trace constraint in this argument is pointwise because the metrics and the directions of variation are parallel. This additional structure makes the positive slice compact and the volume vanish on its boundary; for a general family, the conserved balanced trace gives no such control.

33.3.6 Pluriclosed metrics under deformation

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Pluriclosed metrics persist under small deformations of the complex structure when the Aeppli number $h_ A^{1,1}$ stays constant. Let $M$ be a compact smooth manifold of real dimension $2n$, $n\geq 1$, and let $J_ t$, $t\in U\subset \mathbb R^\ell $, be a smooth family of integrable complex structures, where $U$ is an open neighborhood of $0$. Write $\Omega _ t^{p,q}$ for the smooth complex forms of type $(p,q)$ for $J_ t$. A pluriclosed form belongs to the kernel of $\partial _ t\bar\partial _ t$, and the corresponding Aeppli group is

\[ H_ A^{1,1}(M,J_ t) =\frac{\ker (\partial _ t\bar\partial _ t:\Omega _ t^{1,1}\to \Omega _ t^{2,2})}{\partial _ t\Omega _ t^{0,1}+\bar\partial _ t\Omega _ t^{1,0}}, \qquad h_ A^{1,1}(M,J_ t)=\dim _{\mathbb C}H_ A^{1,1}(M,J_ t). \]

Constancy of this dimension gives a smooth family of projections onto the forms satisfying the closedness constraint, and positivity then persists near a given strictly positive form.

Lemma 33.3.13. The number $h_ A^{1,1}(M,J_ t)$ is finite. If it is constant near $0$, then, after shrinking $U$, the orthogonal projections

\[ \mathcal P_ t:\Omega _ t^{1,1}\longrightarrow \ker (\partial _ t\bar\partial _ t) \]

for any smooth family of auxiliary Hermitian metrics vary smoothly with $t$ on smooth forms and preserve reality.

Proof. Step 1: an elliptic operator. Fix $t$ and suppress it from the notation; the auxiliary metric defines the adjoints. Put $C=\partial \bar\partial $ and $E=\bar\partial \partial ^*$. The nonnegative self-adjoint operator

33.9
\begin{equation} \mathcal L=C^*C+CC^*+E^*E+EE^* +\partial \partial ^*+\bar\partial \bar\partial ^* \end{equation}

is elliptic of order four. Indeed, at a nonzero real covector $\xi $, the principal symbols of $\partial $ and $\bar\partial $ are, up to the common factor $\mathrm i$, exterior multiplication by the $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ parts of $\xi $. These parts are orthogonal and have the same norm $r>0$. Split the exterior algebra according to which of these two directions are present. The four terms of order four act on the four resulting summands respectively, and the sum of their symbols is $r^4\operatorname {Id}$. The terms of lower order do not affect ellipticity.

The quadratic form of $\mathcal L$ is a sum of squared norms, which gives the inclusion $\subseteq $ in

\[ \ker \mathcal L=\ker C\cap \ker \partial ^*\cap \ker \bar\partial ^*. \]

For the reverse inclusion, note that $C^*=\bar\partial ^*\partial ^*$, $E=\bar\partial \partial ^*$ and $E^*=\partial \bar\partial ^*$ all vanish under these three conditions. Elliptic theory on the compact manifold gives a finite-dimensional space of smooth forms $\ker \mathcal L$, the orthogonal projection $\mathcal H$ onto it and the Green operator $\mathcal G$, with $\mathcal L\mathcal G=\operatorname {Id}-\mathcal H$.

Step 2: the projection. Set $R=\mathcal L-C^*C$. Each term of $R$ has image in $\operatorname {im}\partial +\operatorname {im}\bar\partial $. Thus every smooth $(1,1)$-form $\alpha $ has the decomposition

33.10
\begin{equation} \alpha =\mathcal H\alpha +R\mathcal G\alpha +C^*C\mathcal G\alpha . \end{equation}

The first two terms lie in $\ker C$, since $C\partial =C\bar\partial =0$, and the last is orthogonal to $\ker C$. Consequently

33.11
\begin{equation} \mathcal P=\operatorname {Id}-C^*C\mathcal G \end{equation}

is the orthogonal projection onto $\ker C$. If $C\alpha =0$, the last term in (33.10) vanishes, so the Aeppli class of $\alpha $ is represented by $\mathcal H\alpha $. A harmonic form in $\operatorname {im}\partial +\operatorname {im}\bar\partial $ is zero, by orthogonality. This identifies $H_ A^{1,1}$ with $\ker \mathcal L$ and proves finiteness.

Step 3: dependence on $t$. Now identify the varying type bundles and their $L^2$ spaces smoothly with those at $t=0$, using a unitary identification for the auxiliary metrics. The coefficients of $\mathcal L_ t$ then vary smoothly. Choose a circle $|z|=\varepsilon $ enclosing no nonzero eigenvalue of $\mathcal L_0$. The resolvents of $\mathcal L_0$ on this circle are bounded from $H^ s$ to $H^{s+4}$. The Neumann series for their perturbations gives smooth resolvents on the circle for small $t$, and the contour projections have constant rank. Constancy of $h_ A^{1,1}$ makes that rank equal to $\dim \ker \mathcal L_ t$. Hence the contour projections are exactly $\mathcal H_ t$, and $\mathcal L_ t$ has no additional small eigenvalues.

The operators

\[ \mathcal G_ t=(\mathcal L_ t+\mathcal H_ t)^{-1}-\mathcal H_ t \]

vary smoothly on every Sobolev scale, by the same inverse argument and elliptic regularity. Formula (33.11) therefore gives the asserted smooth family $\mathcal P_ t$. Complex conjugation sends $C_ t$ to $-C_ t$, exchanges $E_ t$ with $E_ t^*$, and exchanges the last two terms of (33.9). Thus $\mathcal L_ t$, $\mathcal H_ t$, $\mathcal G_ t$ and $\mathcal P_ t$ commute with conjugation. $\square$

Theorem 33.3.14. Suppose that $(M,J_0)$ carries a pluriclosed Hermitian form $\omega _0$ and that $h_ A^{1,1}(M,J_ t)$ is constant near $0$. Then for small $t$ there is a smooth family of pluriclosed Hermitian forms $\omega _ t$ on $(M,J_ t)$ with the prescribed value $\omega _0$ at $t=0$.

Proof. Let $\pi _ t^{1,1}$ denote the projection onto forms of type $(1,1)$ for $J_ t$, and use the operators of lemma 33.3.13. Define

\[ \omega _ t=\mathcal P_ t\bigl (\pi _ t^{1,1}\omega _0\bigr ). \]

These are smooth real $(1,1)$-forms satisfying $\partial _ t\bar\partial _ t\omega _ t=0$. At $t=0$ the projection fixes $\omega _0$, since $\omega _0$ already satisfies this equation. The tensors $\omega _ t(\cdot ,J_ t\cdot )$ converge uniformly to the positive definite tensor $\omega _0(\cdot ,J_0\cdot )$, which has a uniform positive lower bound by compactness. Hence they remain positive definite after shrinking $U$. $\square$

The construction projects onto $\partial _ t\bar\partial _ t$-closed forms and obtains positivity by continuity near $\omega _0$; closedness and conditions on the curvature would impose further equations. Thus the theorem provides pluriclosed metrics along a given deformation, while the balanced metric required in 33.3.12 remains a separate geometric condition.

33.3.7 Morse inequalities and the choice of volume

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

In the closed Kähler theory, subtracting a nef class has a controlled cost in volume. For closed nef classes $\alpha ,\beta $ on a compact Kähler manifold, the transcendental Morse inequality states that

\[ \operatorname {Vol}(\alpha -\beta ) \geq \int _ X\alpha ^ n-n\int _ X\alpha ^{n-1}\wedge \beta , \]

where $\operatorname {Vol}$ is the usual volume of a class. Its source, and the differentiability of the volume and the duality of cones that follow from it, are recalled in section 34.20. To formulate an analogue on an arbitrary compact complex manifold, one must first choose the volume to be estimated.

For nonclosed backgrounds, the intersection numbers on the right-hand side are in general not invariant under a change of representatives. Moreover, the upper volume of a class and $\mathcal V_\mu $ have different definitions and can behave differently with respect to finiteness. A Hermitian analogue must therefore specify which invariant it estimates.

The first-variation formula gives a finite quantity to study. Fix smooth real $(1,1)$-forms $\theta ,\eta $ and $t>0$. Suppose that $\theta _ s=\theta -s\eta $ has a smooth positive representative for $0\leq s\leq t$, and let $\gamma _ s$ be a minimizing Gauduchon metric for its smooth equation. By proposition 26.2.2 and the fundamental theorem of calculus,

33.12
\begin{equation} \lambda _\mu (\theta -t\eta ) =\lambda _\mu (\theta ) -\int _0^ t \frac{\int _ X\eta \wedge \gamma _ s^{n-1}}{D_\mu (\gamma _ s)}\, \mathrm{d}s. \end{equation}

The quotient in the integrand does not change when the minimizer is rescaled. This identity holds on the positive segment $0\leq s\leq t$ and uses no bound on the upper Monge–Ampère mass. A uniform upper bound for its integrand gives a lower bound for the remaining root volume. On a compact Kähler manifold, theorem 26.11.1 extends the identity up to a nef endpoint that has no smooth positive representative, and bounds the resulting improper integral, when a closed nef core of positive volume is supplemented by a semipositive form $B$. Its test metrics keep the prescribed density, and (26.69) isolates the correction involving $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}B$. There the endpoint is big because of the closed core; example 26.11.3 gives a nonclosed example with an explicit strict current at the endpoint. Positive volume at the endpoint alone cannot provide such a current, by theorem 26.13.2, so a criterion going beyond the closed core must exclude that obstruction.

For the warped products of section 26.12, theorem 26.12.1 computes the finite improper cost for every choice of minimizing metric, and proposition 26.12.2 gives its rate from the codimension and vanishing order of the fiber coefficient. These paths can end with positive root volume but no big representative, and proposition 26.12.3 excludes every closed nef positive-volume core at a zero of that coefficient. Thus exact control of the subtraction cost in this product family does not supply a general bigness criterion.

Problem 33.3.15. In the setting of (33.12), find geometric hypotheses that control the integral on the right-hand side, the cost of the subtraction, up to a nef endpoint, beyond the regime of a closed nef core treated in theorem 26.11.1. For closed backgrounds, compare the resulting estimate with the classical Morse inequalities; for nonclosed backgrounds, the estimate should keep track of the differential data and of the dependence on $\mu $.

The singularity and divisorial constructions of this book become relevant here: they measure how much positivity can be spent on imposed poles while the residual Gauduchon volume stays finite. They do not yet give the moving intersection product or the birational volume formula of the closed theory. For the nonanalytic ruled type of example 26.21.6, the improper subtraction cost remains finite as the variational volume vanishes, while the upper ordinary-type volume has a positive limit. This family makes the choice of invariant explicit even for an imposed type with infinite first moment. Recovering a general moving product or birational formula remains a further goal.

33.3.8 Oeljeklaus–Toma manifolds

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Oeljeklaus–Toma manifolds are not Kähler, yet they carry a closed semipositive form $\theta $ whose potentials are all continuous (theorem 28.19.2). On a threefold of type $(2,1)$, the natural Gauduchon metric $\omega =\theta +y_1y_2\, \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}|z|^2$ has an exactly quadratic mass functional, indefinite in sign, and its masses on fiber-invariant potentials are bounded above and below by positive constants (proposition 28.19.5). The known curvature criteria for finite upper and positive lower mass in dimension three require a Hermitian metric whose $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$ has a sign, and these threefolds admit no such metric (proposition 28.19.6; see section 34.16 for the criteria). By (28.71), the remaining difficulty lies entirely in the fluctuation of a potential around its fiber average.

Problem 33.3.16. Let $X$ be an Oeljeklaus–Toma threefold of type $(2,1)$, with the Hermitian form $\omega $ of proposition 28.19.5. Does $X$ have the bounded mass property, that is, is $v_+(\omega )<+\infty $? Is the lower mass $v_-(\omega )$ positive? Equivalently, writing $F(u)$ for the fluctuation term in (28.71), is $F$ bounded above on smooth $\omega $-plurisubharmonic functions $u$, and is $\inf _ u\bigl (\mathrm M_\omega (Au)+F(u)\bigr )>0$? The same questions can be asked for Oeljeklaus–Toma manifolds of any type $(s,t)$ with $s\geq 2$.

By proposition 6.5.2, the answer to the first question does not depend on the choice of Hermitian form. A negative answer would require potentials whose fluctuations make $\operatorname {Re}(\partial _{w_1}v\, \overline{\partial _{w_2}v})$ large, while keeping $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$. By lemma 28.19.1, every nonzero Fourier mode on the fiber tori depends on $z$, so for a single mode $c(y)\cos (\cdots )$ the positivity of the $z$-coefficient of $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ bounds $|c|$ by a multiple of $y_1y_2$ divided by the square of the $z$-frequency; the frequencies in the $x$-directions are not controlled pointwise. Whether compactness and the automorphy under the units bound the fluctuation term is the content of the problem. Unlike the Hopf threefold of chapter 8, the manifold contains no curves along which heat potentials could be concentrated, by the theorem of Ornea and Verbitsky recalled before corollary 28.19.4.

The rigidity in theorem 28.19.2 comes from two features of the null foliation of $\theta $: its leaves carry no nonconstant plurisubharmonic functions bounded above, and their closures are real tori on which translations act transitively. For the pullback of a Kähler form under a holomorphic fibration, the null leaves are the compact fibers, and $\theta $-plurisubharmonic functions may have poles.

33.4 Closed and twisted classes

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

A volume attached to a closed Bott–Chern class must not depend on the representative, while a volume equation for a locally conformally Kähler (LCK) form must preserve its Lee class and its twisted differential. The examples below show where ordinary Hermitian perturbations fail to respect these requirements. We use the LCK notions of definition 29.1.1: for a closed one-form $\lambda $, $D_\lambda $ is the twisted Hessian of (29.8), and $j_ X\colon H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(X,\mathbb R)\to H^{1,1}_{\mathrm{BC}}(X,\mathbb R)$ is the Lee map (29.7); the associated Lee–Gauduchon cone is studied in section 29.6.

The non-nef and non-Kähler loci of chapter 28 separate closed currents with small negative errors from strictly positive closed currents. Any numerical restricted volume proposed for closed classes on general compact complex manifolds must be compatible with proposition 28.5.1: if a Hermitian form $\omega $ fails the bounded mass property, as on the Hopf threefold, then for every $\varepsilon >0$ the supremum of $\int _ X(T+\varepsilon \omega )^ n$ over smooth closed representatives $T\geq -\varepsilon \omega $ of the zero class is already infinite. Since the failure occurs at each fixed error size, letting the error tend to zero afterwards cannot turn this expression into a class invariant.

For $n\geq 2$, the subsolution threshold also constrains divisorial singularities. Let $\omega $ be a Hermitian form, $\mathrm{d}V$ a smooth positive probability volume form and $0\leq f\in L^1(\mathrm{d}V)$ a nonzero density with $b=b_\omega (f)>0$, and put $p=f^{1/n}$. If a subsolution at the threshold $b$ dominates the current of integration of an effective divisor $E$, then by proposition 28.8.1 the weak limits of every smooth maximizing Gauduchon sequence annihilate the class of every component of $E$. If one such sequence has uniformly integrable determinant-weighted densities, corollary 28.8.3 excludes divisors whose classes have suitable smooth semipositive representatives, for instance the hyperplane divisor on the Hopf threefold, and corollary 28.8.4 shows that a component with nef class has zero variational volume for every smooth density. Both conclusions hold under the root-tail condition $p\in \mathcal D_\omega ^0$. Characterizing the components that can occur would require a realization theorem; note that the weak closure of smooth tests can be smaller than the set of all normalized Gauduchon currents (example 28.8.2).

For a prescribed logarithmic divisor with a positive Hermitian residual, theorem 15.9.4 identifies the threshold on the entire one-sided class with that of the residual background. The ordinary class and relative envelope class have the same attained coefficient for smooth strictly positive data. A nonzero divisor then makes it strictly smaller than the global coefficient, and corollary 15.9.5 excludes divisorial components at the smooth global threshold. The projective product in example 15.9.6 computes this loss explicitly. These positive-residual results do not give a converse from an annihilating face for general integrable data.

For closed classes on manifolds in Fujiki's class $\mathcal C$, section 28.6 defines a finite restricted invariant on each subvariety, independent of the Kähler resolution used to compute it. It is positive exactly when the class is big on the resolved subvariety, and for a nef class on $X$ the union of the subvarieties where it vanishes is the null locus. For a specified divisorial trace with bounded remainder, section 28.7 computes the mass of the trace itself and turns positive residual mass into a strict current, more singular than the trace. The nonclosed ruled family of section 19.10 goes beyond bounded residuals: theorem 19.10.2 computes both masses over the whole ordinary type, and proposition 19.10.3 identifies its ambient ideal orders, although the unbounded residual is invisible to those ideals. Positive lower volume gives a strict current in that ordinary type downstairs. It is preserved under pure pullback, while proposition 19.10.5 excludes a strict current in the pulled-back ordinary type and still allows a one-sided strict representative. The same obstruction holds after every nonempty finite composition of point blowups, including centers at poles. More generally, theorem 16.7.3 gives an exact ordinary-type strictness criterion for a finite logarithmic divisor plus a residual with arbitrarily small negative curvature. Its ambient ideals and, in the normal crossing case, all intrinsic traces are given by proposition 19.2.2, even when the naive residual restriction is identically $-\infty $. Thus a general restricted invariant must specify which singularity relation its strictness criterion uses. Its construction for arbitrary nonclosed backgrounds, and its relation to intrinsic multiplier-ideal trace data outside these families, remain open.

The LCK setting raises separate questions about equations. By corollary 29.2.2, a compact strictly LCK manifold has no big ordinary Bott–Chern class. Its LCK form belongs instead to a twisted Bott–Chern class, whose representatives are described by the twisted Hessian $D_\lambda $. Prescribing the volume form of a Gauduchon LCK metric in such a class leads to the coupled system (29.11), whose second equation is the additional conformal condition imposed by the Gauduchon condition. 29.4.1 asks whether this system has at most one solution when the Lee class and the twisted class are fixed; the nonuniqueness examples for ordinary potentials do not decide it.

On scalar Hopf manifolds the radial case is solved: for every smooth positive radial volume form and every negative Lee period, theorem 29.4.6 gives exactly one Gauduchon metric in the full positive radial family. By proposition 29.4.7, this metric has a positive potential in its own Lee gauge, so its twisted class is zero, and the coupled system determines it among radial candidates. For nonconstant radial densities these metrics are not Vaisman (proposition 29.4.8). Uniqueness among arbitrary LCK candidates is open.

Two geometric conjectures use the Lee form in different ways. 29.2.4 asserts that the class $j_ X([\lambda ])$, for the Lee form $\lambda $ of a strictly LCK metric, is pseudoeffective, that is, pairs nonnegatively with every Gauduchon metric; the LCK metric itself provides one positive pairing. 29.5.3 asks whether a compact complex manifold with vanishing first Bott–Chern class that admits a strictly LCK metric also admits a Vaisman metric. The rigidity theorem theorem 29.5.1 gives this when a single strictly LCK metric is both Chern–Ricci flat and Gauduchon; the remaining existence problem is to arrange these two conditions simultaneously.

There is a pointwise sufficient condition for the first conjecture. If the least Lee-curvature eigenvalue relative to the chosen strictly LCK metric is constant, 29.2.6 makes it zero and gives a smooth semipositive representative of the nonzero ordinary Bott–Chern class. In particular this class is nef, and the balanced obstruction applies; compact invariant LCK quotients satisfy the criterion. The volume polynomial 29.2.5 supplies the argument. The two Hopf families show its scope: 29.2.8 has constant minimum but varying remaining spectrum, whereas 29.2.7 has indefinite Lee curvature and a smooth semipositive correction. In the latter family the first pointwise degeneracy retains positive total volume. A general LCK metric need not have constant minimum, so the unrestricted pseudoeffectivity conjecture stays open.

The Lee–Gauduchon cone of section 29.6 makes part of this geometry invariant under bimeromorphic maps. Its dual is the cone of classes in $H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(X,\mathbb R)$ whose image under $j_ X$ is pseudoeffective, and the strict inequalities defining it exclude $\ker j_ X$; the duality and its behavior under modifications are proved in that section. A nonzero pseudoeffective class in the image of $j_ X$ obstructs balanced metrics on every manifold bimeromorphic to $X$. On the Hopf threefold, the classes in $H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(X,\mathbb R)$ whose image under $j_ X$ is pseudoeffective form a positive half-ray, although the Monge–Ampère masses there are unbounded. This cohomological invariant thus captures a geometric obstruction, while the nonlinear estimates for the scalar equation remain a separate task. The converse of its consequence for balanced metrics fails: the threefold of example 29.7.4 has zero Lee–Gauduchon cone but no strongly Gauduchon metric.

33.5 Degeneration orders and finite-distance boundaries

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Approximating a nef background by positive ones yields two kinds of boundary data: the rate at which the variational volume vanishes, and the distance traveled in the Hessian metric of section 26.5. These need not detect the same boundary points. Throughout this section $\mu $ is a smooth strictly positive probability form on $X$.

For a nef background $\theta $ and a Hermitian form $h$, the lower and upper degeneration orders $\underline a(\theta )\leq \overline a(\theta )$ of proposition 26.19.1 are the lower and upper limits of $\log \mathcal V_\mu (\theta +\epsilon h)/\log \epsilon $ as $\epsilon \downarrow 0$. They do not depend on $\mu $ or on the perturbation direction $h$, but they need not coincide. On a fixed product of two elliptic curves, proposition 26.19.5 realizes every pair $(1+\alpha ,1+\beta )$ with $0<\alpha <\beta <1$, as well as irrational common orders, and proposition 26.19.4 gives zero boundary volume with decay slower than every positive power of $\epsilon $. Equality of the two orders and their integrality therefore require additional geometry, as in the closed Kähler case, where the order is governed by the numerical dimension.

Pulling back by a modification can only increase both orders (proposition 26.19.8), and a single blowup can increase them strictly, or remove positive limiting volume while both orders remain zero. Subtracting a positive current gives the same one-sided comparison when the residual stays nef. The perturbative minimal multiplicities of a nef background vanish, whereas in corollary 26.19.12 the boundary class contains a unique positive current, the current of integration along a divisor; it has minimal singularities, and removing it changes the order. The remaining questions, collected in 26.19.49, concern equality of the orders and geometric restrictions on these behaviors.

The Hessian metric gives boundary information of a different kind. Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional space of smooth real $(1,1)$-forms containing no nonzero form $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f$ with $f$ smooth, and let $\mathscr C$ be the open cone of elements of $V$ that admit a smooth positive representative. By proposition 26.5.1, the metric $-D^2\log \lambda _\mu $ on $\mathscr C$ splits isometrically as the product of the logarithmic volume direction with the unit-volume hypersurface $\mathscr S=\{ \lambda _\mu =1\} $; consequently every path along which the volume tends to zero or to infinity has infinite length. Nevertheless, the blowup example of example 26.5.2 has boundary points at finite distance, represented by nef classes of positive volume. Completing the metric cone therefore cannot mean simply adjoining all nef classes, nor declaring every nef boundary point infinitely far away.

Problem 33.5.1. Let $\mu $ be a smooth strictly positive probability form on $X$, and let $\mathscr C$ be a cone of backgrounds in a specified finite-dimensional space $V$ as in section 26.5, with the Hessian metric $-D^2\log \lambda _\mu $. Describe the metric completion of its unit-volume hypersurface $\mathscr S=\{ \theta \in \mathscr C:\lambda _\mu (\theta )=1\} $. Which boundary points admit a current representative, and which estimates for the minimizing Gauduchon metrics distinguish boundary points at finite distance from those at infinite distance?

On a compact quotient of a complex Lie group, with $\mu $ the normalized invariant probability, theorem 26.5.5 answers the question for every fixed linear space of invariant backgrounds containing a positive form: the unit-volume hypersurface is complete, and its Cauchy sequences are exactly those whose coefficient matrices converge to a positive matrix. After normalization, nonzero singular limits of the coefficients lie at infinite distance. These assertions depend on the invariant probability and do not classify arbitrary cones of backgrounds or reference densities.

On the Iwasawa manifold, theorem 26.5.9 extends the same coefficient metric and completion to every smooth reference density that is invariant along the central elliptic fibers; these are exactly the probabilities that maximize the variational volume of each invariant positive background. The minimizing forms need not be invariant, but their coefficient means still satisfy the spectral distance bounds. A reference density that varies along the fibers gives smaller volume, and the completion of its Hessian metric is a separate question.

For the warped product cones of section 26.5.3, on the product of a compact Kähler manifold with a curve, theorem 26.5.13 shows that the completion of the unit-volume hypersurface is a closed strip, a closed half-plane or the whole plane. Which ends lie at finite distance is decided by two ratios of real codimension to vanishing order, attached to the minimum set and the maximum set of the warping coefficient. Every end at finite distance is represented by a smooth semipositive, nef, nonbig form of volume one. The length estimates of lemma 26.5.12 are bounds for the variance of the traces of the balanced minimizers; they use the product probability and extreme sets consisting of finitely many disjoint strata with power-type vanishing. Other densities and other geometries of the extreme sets remain to be studied.

The cone must be specified in 33.5.1, since allowing additional directions can shorten distances along paths. The finite-dimensional calculations above do not define a distance on the whole infinite-dimensional quotient of backgrounds modulo $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$ of smooth functions.

33.6 Weak Gauduchon adjoints

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Let $\theta $ be a background with a model reference $\varphi $ and $\mathrm{d}V$ a smooth positive volume form, as in chapter 15. For a bounded logarithmic density $F$, the normalized equation $\mathrm{MA}^{\mathrm{ext}}_\theta (u)=c_\varphi (F)\mathrm{e}^ F\, \mathrm{d}V$ in the ordinary type of $\varphi $ determines a unique scalar, and $B_\varphi (F)=\log c_\varphi (F)$ is a concave functional of $F$ (section 15.1). For smooth data, differentiating the smooth equation produces a Gauduchon adjoint probability. At a continuous logarithmic density $F$, the supporting probabilities of $B_\varphi $, which form the set $\mathcal A_\varphi (F)$ of (15.5), provide a weak replacement. In the regimes of background and reference considered in chapter 15, $\mathcal A_\varphi (F)$ is nonempty, convex and weakly compact, and its members are absolutely continuous (theorem 15.2.1). If this set consisted of a single probability, the scalar functional would be differentiable at $F$ even where no classical linearized equation is available.

Conjecture 33.6.1. Let $\omega $ be a Hermitian form on $X$ and $\mathrm{d}V$ a smooth positive volume form, and take the nonsingular reference $\varphi =0$. For every real $F\in C(X)$, the set $\mathcal A_0(F)$ of supporting probabilities of the normalization functional $B_0$ at $F$ consists of a single probability measure.

Equivalently, by proposition 15.2.3, the concave functional $B_0$ is Gâteaux differentiable at every continuous logarithmic density. The conjecture holds at smooth $F$, by the linearized equation, and in the closed Kähler setting, where the normalization is explicit. By proposition 15.2.2, it would imply that for any smooth approximations of $F$ the Gauduchon adjoint probabilities have a unique weak limit.

A further sufficient condition is a bounded solution with continuous, everywhere positive complex Hessian. Then theorem 15.2.6 identifies a unique stationary supporting probability and the Gâteaux derivative. Proposition 15.2.7 gives its formula on $\mathbb P^1$ times any compact Hermitian factor for every positive continuous density of the stated product form; example 15.2.8 includes a non-Dini density on a non-Kähler threefold. The criterion does not prove that an arbitrary continuous density has a solution with such a Hessian.

For bounded potentials there is a direct result. Suppose that $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v$ is Gauduchon for every smooth $v$ with $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v>0$; this holds for every Gauduchon form on a compact surface. Then, by proposition 4.10.1, the normalized Monge–Ampère measures of bounded $\omega $-plurisubharmonic functions extend the smooth adjoint probabilities and are continuous under convergence in capacity of uniformly bounded potentials. This defines no weak linearized operator, and it does not settle approximation by unbounded potentials in a general background.

A Baire category argument gives a partial result. In every regime of background and reference considered in chapter 15, theorem 15.2.4 shows that $\mathcal A_\varphi (F)$ is a single probability for $F$ in a dense $G_\delta $ subset of $C(X)$, and that at such $F$ all choices of supporting probabilities along uniform approximations of $F$ have the same weak limit. The assertion is generic for the uniform topology and does not exclude exceptional continuous densities.

For $n\geq 2$, the compact formulation with currents gives a more precise target. By theorem 26.7.2, the supporting probabilities at $F$ are exactly the normalized determinant-weighted measures of the currents $T\in \mathscr G_\omega $ maximizing the functional $\mathfrak D_ F$ of (26.39). The conjecture thus asks whether all maximizers have the same weighted measure. Corollary 26.8.3 bounds the densities of these measures in $L^ q$, for every $1<q<n/(n-1)$, uniformly as $F$ ranges over a bounded subset of $C(X)$; the endpoint $q=n/(n-1)$ is included when $v_-(\omega )>0$. For $n\geq 3$, theorem 26.8.5 gives uniform $L^\infty $ bounds for both the adjoint density and the determinant density for smooth logarithmic densities in a small $C^{2,\alpha }$ ball around the logarithmic density of $\omega ^ n$ with respect to $\mathrm{d}V$, even when the lower mass vanishes, and by corollary 26.8.6 this ball contains distinct data with distinct exact maximizing currents. The controlled-Hessian family of theorem 26.8.9 gives a larger sufficient condition: if the smooth solution forms are uniformly comparable to the background, both densities have uniform two-sided bounds depending only on that comparison and fixed geometry. The divergence of the cofactor uses only the fixed torsion, so derivatives of the varying Hessian do not enter the constants. Example 26.8.10 gives such a smooth family whose densities have unbounded $C^1$ norms and no common modulus of continuity. Arbitrary bounded families of continuous data remain outside these hypotheses. These estimates alone give compactness but not uniqueness at continuous data. For a fixed smooth density the maximizing current is unique, smooth and strictly positive; nevertheless, proposition 26.17.2 shows that a sequence approaching the maximum can have unbounded determinant moments above the critical exponent. Another approach to the conjecture would be to show directly that the upper and lower directional derivatives of $B_0$ agree.

More is known about the functional itself. Relative inequalities between positive residual forms give uniform convergence of the whole scalar functional, by the explicit bound (15.29) of theorem 15.8.1. Under $L^1$ convergence of tests with a common $L^\infty $ bound, corollary 15.8.2 shows that the supporting probabilities have uniformly integrable densities whose weak $L^1$ cluster limits lie in the limiting supporting set. The adjoint probabilities at smooth tests, together with the value of the functional at zero, determine the whole functional by (15.30). None of these statements decides the conjectured uniqueness at continuous tests.

For a singular reference or a degenerate background, the same uniqueness question makes sense under the hypotheses of chapter 15. A geometric interpretation of the supporting probabilities, however, requires a well-defined weak linearization. For a smooth background $\theta $ and $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, write $\theta _ u=\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$, and let $\rho $ be a measurable weight. The expression $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(\rho \theta _ u^{n-1})$ is not a definition until multiplication of the product $\theta _ u^{n-1}$ by $\rho $ has been justified.

An explicit divisorial model shows what can go wrong. For the admissible reference of lemma 15.2.9, the supporting set is a single probability and the residual linearization is well defined (proposition 15.2.10). Nevertheless, theorem 15.2.11 gives decreasing smooth approximations, with positive forms, whose classical adjoint probabilities have different weak limits, each carrying mass one half on the divisor. These potentials converge in capacity and their logarithmic densities converge in every finite $L^ p$, but their exponential densities concentrate and their smooth mass exceeds the relative mass. Within this fixed divisorial type, corollary 15.2.12 proves recovery, with an explicit estimate, under convergence in capacity and a uniform bound on the residual potentials. On the non-Gauduchon product surface of 15.13.4, theorem 15.13.6 constructs a weak linearization for a logarithmic solution with continuous positive residual and identifies its full supporting set as a singleton. Uniform convergence of the positive residual forms, with the divisor fixed, gives convergence of both the weighted currents and their probabilities in proposition 15.13.7; pole-filling smoothings still retain unwanted divisor mass (example 15.13.8). For general singular references, adjoints that do not depend on the approximation still require control of the singularity type, of the mass and of the conformal multiplier.

33.7 Topologies on singularity types

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

A topology on singularity types must keep information that weak convergence of currents can lose. Deficits of upper volume achieve this when bounded mass and positive lower volume provide uniform control, and on curves polar curvature gives a complete description even at zero volume. The problem is to extend such control to degenerations in higher dimension and to backgrounds with unbounded mass. Throughout this section, for a smooth real $(1,1)$-form $\theta $ and $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, $P_\theta [u]$ is the ordinary envelope of definition 1.19.4 and $P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}$ the multiplier-ideal envelope of definition 16.2.1. An ordinary model is a potential $u$ with $P_\theta [u]=u$, an ideal model is one with $P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}=u$, and an analytic model is an ordinary model with analytic singularities. The lower and upper singularity volumes $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)\leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)$ are the infimum and the supremum of the non-pluripolar masses over the ordinary singularity type of $u$ (definition 13.3.1).

33.7.1 Weak convergence and singularity data

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Weak convergence alone keeps little singularity information. The complete Gauduchon trace metric of corollary 5.2.1 induces the $L^1$ topology on normalized potentials, and the ideal metric of proposition 16.4.1 is the $L^1$ distance between the canonical ideal representatives $P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}$. The completion of the ideal metric can acquire points that are not ideal envelopes of their own classes: on a curve, theorem 16.4.2 approximates every normalized potential by ideal envelopes with finitely many logarithmic poles and zero non-pluripolar mass. In particular, such envelopes can converge to a smooth potential with strictly positive curvature. This convergence does not hold in capacity, since damped continuity would then preserve their zero measures.

A positive lower bound on the mass does not repair this. In proposition 16.4.3, analytic ideal envelopes of one fixed positive mass converge in the ideal metric to the smooth model $0$, whose mass is larger. Nor can the normalization functional recover the lost data: on the closed model domain of theorem 15.10.1, it records exactly the mass. The examples with positive residual in example 15.10.2, example 15.10.3 have identical functionals and supporting sets but different poles, different multiplier ideals and different limits of normalized potentials, also for nonclosed backgrounds.

An order bound gives more control than weak convergence. Let $h$ be a Hermitian form, let $\epsilon _ j\geq 0$ tend to zero, and let $a_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta +\epsilon _ jh)$ satisfy the one-sided bound $u\preceq _{\mathcal I}a_ j$ in the ideal order of section 1.12, that is, $\mathcal I(ku)\subseteq \mathcal I(ka_ j)$ for all $k>0$. Then proposition 16.5.1 identifies the limit of the ideal envelopes from the convergence of divisorial Lelong numbers, and it shows that along such ordered analytic approximations, including dyadic regularization, the ordinary envelopes converge to $P_\theta [u]$ exactly when $P_\theta [u]=P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}$. Inclusions of multiplier ideals at fixed weights, as in corollary 16.5.2, give the required convergence of divisorial numbers. Without the order bound, $L^1$ convergence together with convergence of every divisorial number is not enough, by example 16.5.3. These tests make sense even when the upper volume is infinite. In contrast, in a Hermitian background analytic classes are dense for the ideal metric in the whole space (corollary 16.5.6), so density for that metric cannot characterize equality of the envelopes.

The finite damped measures of chapter 7 control nonlinear limits throughout the space of potentials, including potentials of infinite mass. Since their weak continuity already follows from convergence in capacity, requiring it in addition imposes no further condition on multiplier ideals.

For a Hermitian form $\omega $, the uniform topology $\tau _{\omega ,0}$ of section 16.6 on ordinary singularity types combines both envelopes with controlled inclusions of multiplier ideals and the total variation of divisorial orders on each fixed compact modification. By theorem 16.6.3, the closure and the sequential closure of the analytic classes are both the set of ordinary classes with $P_\omega [u]=P_\omega [u]_{\mathcal I}$. This characterizes the analytic closure in every Hermitian background, including classes represented by potentials of infinite mass. The topology is not $T_0$ on ordinary classes. Transport by a smooth gauge change of the background is exact when the obstacle changes with the background, but with the zero obstacle kept fixed it is discontinuous already on curves, even within a cutoff of positive mass (proposition 16.6.6).

The uniformity of 16.6.4, generated by the envelopes $P_{\omega ,F}[u]$ and $P_{\omega ,F}[u]_{\mathcal I}$ below all smooth obstacles $F$ (definition 1.19.4), is invariant under smooth changes of background, and its analytic closure consists of the classes for which the two envelopes agree below every smooth obstacle (theorem 16.6.9). On curves this is the same analytic locus, but by proposition 30.12.12 the topology is incomplete and strictly weaker than polar total variation. In higher dimension, membership of $u$ in the relative envelope class $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\omega ,P_\omega [u]_{\mathcal I})$ suffices for equality below every obstacle (proposition 16.6.8), and under bounded mass and positive lower volume of the ideal envelope, equality at the zero obstacle is already sufficient (proposition 30.8.2).

The fixed stronger potential in theorem 16.6.11 gives an explicit family in the sufficient relative envelope class on every positive Hermitian background. Its poles may contain a prescribed countable dense set, while its multiplier ideals are trivial at every positive weight. By proposition 16.6.12, both envelopes equal the unrestricted envelope below every continuous obstacle. The stretching property is supplied by the construction rather than deduced from equality below zero.

Several geometric cases are known without the volume condition. For every admissible smooth background on a curve, corollary 30.13.2 shows that equality at the zero obstacle, equality at all smooth obstacles and membership in the relative envelope class of the ideal envelope are equivalent. On products with separated backgrounds, proposition 16.6.30 computes both envelopes coordinatewise below separated smooth obstacles, also for curve backgrounds of zero area, and proposition 16.6.40 identifies equality at the zero obstacle with equality at every smooth obstacle, including obstacles that mix the coordinates and factors of zero area. This equivalence persists after adding a nonclosed auxiliary coefficient and a unit divisor (corollary 16.6.41), and for arbitrary compact complex auxiliary factors with a semipositive vertical term, without any mass bound on the ambient manifold (corollary 16.6.44). For divisorial fibrations over curves, corollary 16.6.20 gives the same equivalence at zero model volume, even with poles along whole fibers or with disconnected fibers, by reducing the obstacles to the cover formed by the fiber components. In example 16.6.18 the ambient background fails bounded mass, while the base potential belongs to the relative envelope class required for equality at all obstacles. Whether equality at the zero obstacle implies equality at all obstacles at zero lower volume in general, including backgrounds with unbounded mass, is unresolved.

33.7.2 Volume comparison and analytic approximation

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

In this subsection $X$ has bounded mass and $\theta $ is a smooth big background. For $u,v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, the comparison $d_\theta (u,v)$ of definition 30.6.1 is the sum of the deficits of the upper volumes of $u$ and $v$ relative to the upper volume of $\max (u,v)$, and $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ denotes the set of ordinary models with lower volume at least $\delta >0$. A common positive lower bound for the volumes converts a small deficit into a stretching estimate. The resulting quantitative $L^1$ bound, together with a modulus for three points that replaces the triangle inequality, gives separation and Cauchy completeness on $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ (theorem 30.6.5); the cutoff must be closed, as example 30.6.6 shows. The positive lower bound is also what prevents the comparison from identifying distinct ordinary models: example 30.6.2 gives two distinct models at zero lower volume with comparison zero, in positive closed backgrounds in every dimension at least two and in positive nonclosed backgrounds in every dimension at least three. Rooftop continuity gives monotone lower bounds, which yield convergence in capacity (proposition 30.7.2, theorem 30.7.3).

Analytic approximation detects exactly the ideal models. Theorem 30.8.3 corrects analytic regularizations so that they stay in the fixed background; their ordinary models converge for $d_\theta $ to the ideal envelope, and their comparison with the original potential tends to the excess of the upper volume of its ideal envelope over its own upper volume. Conversely, at positive lower volume, theorem 30.8.4 shows that ideal models are closed under this convergence. Although $d_\theta $ fails the triangle inequality even on analytic models on a projective manifold, theorem 30.11.3 constructs a complete metric on the ordinary models of positive lower volume with the same convergent sequences; its Cauchy criterion also controls the reciprocals of the lower volumes, which excludes approach to zero volume. By corollary 30.11.4, the ideal models form a closed complete subspace, which is the completion of the analytic models of positive volume.

Monotone limits show where positivity enters. For decreasing ordinary models in backgrounds that decrease uniformly to a smooth big form, theorem 30.5.2 gives continuity of the lower volume even when the limiting volume is zero; if the limiting lower volume is positive, the upper volumes and the Lelong numbers on every fixed modification are continuous as well. For increasing sequences in a fixed background, theorem 30.5.1 gives continuity of the ordinary envelopes and of both volumes under the same positivity condition. For arbitrary decreasing potentials whose limit has positive lower volume, proposition 30.5.3 shows that the ordinary envelopes converge exactly when the upper volumes do. Likewise, when the ideal envelope has positive lower volume, proposition 30.8.2 shows that the ordinary and ideal envelopes of a potential agree exactly when the potential and its ideal envelope have the same upper volume.

There is an explicit divisorial positivity test on $\mathbb P^2$. Let $\kappa $ be the normalized Fubini–Study form, prescribe distinct hyperplanes $H_ j$ with summable coefficients $a_ j>0$, and put $A=\sum _ ja_ j$. If $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\theta =0$ and $\theta -A\kappa >0$, equality with the profile $\sum _ ja_ j\operatorname {ord}_ E(\pi ^*H_ j)$ on every smooth compact modification specifies one ideal type. By 30.8.6, its ideal envelope is ordinarily equivalent to the logarithmic series, and every representative of this ordinary type has mass $\int _ X(\theta -A\kappa )^2$. The nonclosed example of 30.8.7 has infinitely many hyperplanes outside one pencil and positive lower volume. General positivity criteria from restricted ideals or divisorial orders, and the remaining zero-volume implications in 30.8.8, remain open.

The comparison is compatible with changes of background as long as these controls persist. A smooth gauge change, followed by ordinary projection, preserves it exactly (proposition 30.9.1). For a fixed potential and backgrounds decreasing to $\theta $, corollary 30.5.4 gives continuity of the lower volume and, at positive lower volume, of the ordinary envelope. The uniform estimate (30.25) allows the curvature errors of analytic regularization to tend to zero, as in theorem 30.9.4. For perturbations of the background in both directions, a convex correction produces admissible transported models, with a uniform comparison whose limiting notion of convergence does not depend on the chosen strict reference; this gives the analytic criterion of theorem 30.10.2, theorem 30.10.3. None of these arguments covers loss of bigness or the boundary at zero volume in higher dimension.

33.7.3 Polar curvature

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Let $Y$ be a compact connected Riemann surface with a smooth positive form $\theta $. For $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )$, the polar curvature $\mu _ u=\mathbf1_{\{ u=-\infty \} }(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)$ determines the ordinary model of $u$, and its atomic part determines the ideal model. By theorem 30.12.2, $d_\theta (u,v)$ is the total variation of $\mu _ u-\mu _ v$, also at zero non-pluripolar mass. By corollary 30.12.3, all normalized ordinary models form a complete geodesic space, and the ideal models form the closed subspace obtained by completing the analytic models of positive mass. On ideal models of positive mass this agrees with the topology of the source metric in theorem 19.5.3, corollary 19.5.4, for which this space is not totally bounded. Its completeness therefore has a different content from the compact completion obtained from weak convergence of traces.

Keeping only the atomic part of the polar curvature is a $1$-Lipschitz retraction onto the ideal models and selects the unique nearest ideal model; by proposition 30.12.6, the distance to the analytic closure is exactly the mass of the diffuse part of the polar curvature. The model of zero mass in example 30.12.7 has trivial multiplier ideals but positive distance from that closure. Convergence in total variation implies convergence in $L^1$ and in capacity, even at zero mass (theorem 30.12.4), whereas the moving pole of example 30.12.5 shows that convergence in capacity does not imply convergence in total variation.

Degeneration of the background is also explicit on curves. Between smooth backgrounds of positive area, theorem 30.13.3 gives correspondences of the model spaces that rescale by the ratio of the areas and are compatible with ideal projection, also at models of zero mass. Under uniform convergence of the backgrounds, the corresponding normalized representatives vary uniformly in $L^1$ and in capacity (corollary 30.13.5). When the limiting area is zero, theorem 1.8.15 shows that all normalized potentials converge to the unique limiting potential, uniformly over the admissible families, and the unscaled diameters of the model spaces tend to zero.

The joint space of theorem 30.14.1 treats the smooth background as a further coordinate. It is complete for the combination of smooth convergence of backgrounds and total variation of polar curvature, its analytic closure is exactly the ideal locus, even at zero area, and on each fixed background it restricts to the curve topology described above. In higher dimension, the realization of compatible singularity data and a replacement for the comparison of finite upper volumes are unresolved.

Finite curve maps give another compatibility within the curve theory. For $r:C'\to C$ of degree $d$, a smooth base form $\beta $ of nonnegative area and $\sigma =r^*\beta $, 16.6.53 commutes both envelopes with pullback below every pulled-back smooth obstacle. The polar metric scales by $d$, and 16.6.54 gives a $1$-Lipschitz retraction with polar measure $d^{-1}r^*r_*\mu $, preserving polar mass and commuting with ideal projection. By 16.6.55, this describes the closed complete subspace of models that descend to the original base of a compact curve map, with its original-base normalization. The cubic example shows that this averaging need not select a nearest model. These conclusions do not supply a complete topology for general higher-rank backgrounds.

In dimension at least two the polar part of the first curvature current loses information. For $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\omega )$, write $\operatorname {Pol}_1(u)=\mathbf1_{\{ u=-\infty \} }(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)$, and $\operatorname {Pol}_\pi (u)$ for the corresponding current of $u\circ \pi $ on a modification $\pi $ of $X$. By proposition 16.6.58, distinct analytic ideal models with isolated logarithmic poles, whose multiplier ideals differ at suitable weights, all have $\operatorname {Pol}_1=0$. The blowup at the pole detects the coefficient on the exceptional divisor (corollary 16.6.59), but every fixed countable family of modifications misses some such models (corollary 16.6.60).

Tracing polar curvature against Gauduchon forms gives a uniform normalization over modifications. Fix a family $\mathscr R$ of smooth modifications $\pi \colon Y_\pi \to X$ and Gauduchon forms $\eta _\pi $ on $Y_\pi $. The comparison $\delta ^{\mathrm{pol}}_\omega $ of (16.50) is the supremum over $\pi \in \mathscr R$ of the total variation of the polar curvature of $u\circ \pi $ traced against $\eta _\pi ^{n-1}$, normalized by the trace of $\pi ^*\omega $. By proposition 16.6.61, it separates analytic models and, on curves, recovers the complete curve topology up to a constant factor. Bounded equivalence preserves polar currents (proposition 1.25.3), so transport by a smooth gauge change, followed by ordinary projection, preserves the comparison (corollary 16.6.63). Since polar curvature is closed for every pole set (proposition 1.25.6), equality of the scalar traces recovers the full polar currents on each modification (corollary 16.6.64). Arbitrary independent choices of the Gauduchon forms can nevertheless fail to induce the positive-volume topology, even for analytic ideal models on $\mathbb P^2$ (proposition 16.6.66). The space of polar trace coordinates is complete (proposition 16.6.62), and for sequences that are Cauchy for the comparison, proposition 16.6.65 gives compatible weak limits of the polar currents, all carried by the pole set of a single $\omega $-plurisubharmonic function. Whether these limiting currents are realized by an ordinary model is a further problem.

Even all modifications together do not make the first polar comparison separate models or give the right analytic closure. On every compact Kähler manifold of dimension at least two, proposition 16.6.69 constructs a nonzero ordinary model whose polar currents vanish on every modification, whose non-pluripolar volume is positive but strictly smaller than that of the zero model, and whose multiplier ideals are all trivial. More precisely, corollary 16.6.70 gives a complete ordered interval of such models with volumes $A-Mt^ n$ and mutual distances $M|s^ n-t^ n|$, where $A=\int _ X\omega ^ n$ and $M>0$ is a constant of the construction; for every $0<\delta <A$, the interval can be chosen inside $\mathcal M_\delta (\omega )$. Every first polar coordinate vanishes on this interval, and only its zero endpoint is ideal. Consequently every choice of Gauduchon forms identifies distinct models, and the closure of the analytic models for the polar comparison contains nonideal models.

The same diffuse cutoff can be placed in a Hermitian background. Proposition 16.6.71 separates the loss $Mt^ n$ in the mass of the potential from lower-degree terms coming from the background. If $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(\omega ^ q)=0$ for $1\leq q<n$, the mass is the same for all bounded-equivalent representatives, and corollary 16.6.72 extends the whole interval of models to this Hermitian background; in particular the obstruction occurs on every compact complex surface with a Gauduchon form. For an arbitrary Hermitian background, proposition 16.6.73 computes the mass of each representative from finitely many torsion coefficients and the same loss $Mt^ n$. On a surface, corollary 16.6.74 shows that the mass is constant over the bounded-equivalence class exactly when $\omega $ is Gauduchon; otherwise even admissible smooth bounded perturbations change it, and the exact values would require optimizing these terms, which depend on the representative.

Under bounded mass and positive lower mass, proposition 16.6.75 still gives positive lower volume and a strict gap in upper volume for the diffuse ordinary models, while their first polar currents vanish on every modification. By the same proposition, this obstruction holds for every Hermitian metric on every compact complex surface. The theorem on preservation of residuals, theorem 30.3.5, distinguishes the diffuse parameters after ordinary projection. Consequently, in every background with both mass properties, corollary 16.6.76 gives a complete ordered interval of models throughout each range $0\leq t\leq t_0<1/C$, with $C$ the constant of proposition 16.6.71. Its upper-volume coordinate is strictly decreasing and Lipschitz, although the torsion terms prevent the explicit power formula from being inferred, and every first polar coordinate still vanishes. The same theorem preserves the entire residual measure, so its location matters: in proposition 16.6.77, two models carry residual mass $r$ in disjoint regions, have zero first polar data, and are at distance at least $2r$; when $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(\omega ^ q)=0$ for $1\leq q<n$, their two singularity volumes also coincide. A complete extension must therefore distinguish this spatial residual information.

Even the full residual measure on the base is not enough. When $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(\omega ^ q)=0$ for $1\leq q<n$, proposition 16.6.78 uses anisotropic maxima on one diffuse pole set to build a compact metric family of ordinary models indexed by positive weight vectors. Fixing the product of the weights gives complete subfamilies of real dimension $n-1$ whose members have the same residual measure, the same two singularity volumes, trivial multiplier ideals and zero first polar currents on every modification. The comparison distance nevertheless separates them, through the symmetric difference of the associated coordinate rectangles. A complete extension must therefore also detect singularity information lost by the full base residual. The proposition does not assert that the full residual products agree on every modification.

Return to a curve $Y$ with a smooth positive form $\theta $. On ideal models, the ordinary-type topology $\tau _{\theta ,0}$ is the topology of total variation of the atomic data, and its canonical metric includes the boundary of zero mass (theorem 30.12.8). On general ordinary models it is weaker than polar total variation: diffuse polar curvature can concentrate into an atom in the limit of the envelopes, which produces the Cauchy sequence without an ordinary-type limit in proposition 30.12.9. Characterizing the analytic closure on the whole ordinary space and extending the model topology to a complete one are therefore separate requirements.

Problem 33.7.1. For a smooth big background $\theta $ on a manifold with bounded mass, theorem 30.11.3 gives a complete metrizable topology on the ordinary models of positive lower volume, in which the closure of the analytic models is exactly the set of ideal models. Extend this topology to ordinary models beyond bounded mass and positive lower volume, keeping the closure of the analytic models exactly the set of ideal models. The extension should keep the compatibility with smooth gauge changes and with two-sided perturbations of the background established in section 30.9, section 30.10, and it should treat limits of backgrounds that lose bigness or a uniform positive lower bound for the volume. On curves it should recover the complete topology of polar total variation of corollary 30.12.3 on all models. Determine whether the extension in higher dimension is completely metrizable.

Any such extension must keep the singularity information that weak limits lose. Potentials of infinite mass are already covered by $\tau _{\omega ,0}$: for instance, the potential of proposition 16.3.3 has infinite mass, while its canonical envelopes, the ordinary and the ideal one, both equal zero. On curves, zero volume does not prevent completeness, but in higher dimension the stretching argument still needs a positive lower bound for the volume. There is nevertheless a restricted locus where a complete metric is known at zero volume. On the divisorial product locus of proposition 16.6.26, polar curvature on the curve factor gives a complete metric whose analytic closure is exactly the set of ideal models; the top-degree volume comparison vanishes identically on this locus and therefore cannot produce this metric. The same restricted metric and analytic closure exist in a positive nonclosed background on a projective manifold (corollary 16.6.27), and the locus, the metric and the ideal projection persist when the second curve coefficient vanishes and the limiting background is not big (corollary 16.6.28). Proposition 16.6.31 gives a complete sum metric, with the same properties of ideal projection and analytic closure, on the locus induced by several independent curve models, and corollary 16.6.32 preserves this locus and metric through a nonclosed auxiliary coefficient and its nonbig limit at zero. When the curve coefficients vary, proposition 16.6.34 gives compatible weighted metrics, discarding vanishing coordinates, with uniform $L^1$ collapse when all curve areas vanish, and proposition 16.6.35 compares these metrics quantitatively across different sets of active coordinates, with the exact distance for ordered coefficients. Proposition 16.6.37, Corollary 16.6.38 extend the construction to arbitrary smooth admissible curve backgrounds and identify the limiting singleton, and proposition 16.6.43 preserves the restricted complete metric through arbitrary compact complex auxiliary factors with semipositive vertical terms, independently of bounded mass on the ambient manifold.

33.8 Trace realization and recovery

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Prescribing divisorial lower bounds and taking an envelope may produce larger orders than those prescribed. Let $u$ be a quasi-plurisubharmonic function on $X$ and $Y\subset X$ a connected compact submanifold of positive dimension along which $u$ has zero generic Lelong number, $\nu (u,Y)=0$. As in chapter 19, the trace data $\tau _{Y,u}(E)$ of (19.1) are the asymptotic vanishing orders of the restrictions to $Y$ of the multiplier ideals $\mathcal I(tu)$, along prime divisors $E$ on smooth modifications $\pi \colon Z\to Y$. For a smooth real $(1,1)$-form $\Omega $ on $Y$, the trace envelope $\mathcal T_\Omega (u;Y)$ of (19.7) is the upper envelope of the nonpositive $\Omega $-plurisubharmonic functions $a$ with $\nu (a\circ \pi ,E)\geq \tau _{Y,u}(E)$ for all such $E$. The realization problem asks when these inequalities are equalities. Theorem 19.6.2 constructs the envelope in the restricted background itself and identifies it as the $L^1$ limit of the envelopes of the restricted analytic approximants, and theorem 19.6.3 shows that it pulls back exactly to every compact modification and is there the limit of the envelopes for vanishing Hermitian perturbations. When $Y$ has bounded mass and carries a Hermitian form of positive lower mass, corollary 30.5.5 proves equality in all divisorial inequalities for sufficiently large backgrounds $\Omega $; when $Y$ is Kähler, equality also follows directly from the closed theory (theorem 19.6.4). Ambient potentials with analytic singularities have exact ordinary restrictions. In higher dimension, theorem 19.7.1 realizes all orders when the data reduce to a problem on an ambient curve, with an ordinary type independent of a sufficiently large background. For general data, theorem 19.6.5 shows that in a larger Hermitian background the envelope has positive finite mass.

Problem 33.8.1. Let $u$ and $Y$ be as above, and suppose that $Y$ fails at least one of the two mass properties used in corollary 30.5.5: bounded mass, and the existence of a Hermitian form of positive lower mass. Can the Hermitian background $\Omega $ on $Y$ in theorem 19.6.5 be chosen so that

\[ \nu \bigl (\mathcal T_\Omega (u;Y)\circ \pi ,E\bigr ) =\tau _{Y,u}(E) \]

for every prime divisor $E$ on every smooth modification $\pi $ of $Y$? If so, determine an ordinary or ideal singularity class of this realization that is compatible with changes of background, and prove its addition and approximation properties.

The equality is required on all modifications, which is stronger than realizing the divisorial part of the data on one model. For that part, corollary 1.17.5 gives a canonical ordinary type by combining a Gauduchon bound for the mass of the divisorial current with a smooth decomposition of currents; it does not determine the trace data of higher codimension, which appear on further modifications. The exceptional divisor of theorem 23.8.1 describes the discrepancy between the canonical types on two models, together with a cocycle relation and approximation in linear mass. By example 23.8.3, a nonzero exceptional term is compatible with an exact trace potential: the term records information absent from the divisorial part on the original model. Enlarging the background provides a competitor with controlled curvature, but it does not automatically exclude additional divisorial orders. In the proof for curve fibrations, every order on a further model reduces to a multiplicity in one variable. Under the two mass properties, theorem 30.5.2 controls Lelong numbers through the deficit of upper volume and a positive lower volume. The missing step is to extend such control beyond these mass conditions.

Even exact realization does not control the mass of all representatives of the ideal class. In proposition 19.9.2, on a product with the Hopf threefold, the realizing potential has finite extended mass, while another potential with the same multiplier ideals and the same trace numbers has infinite mass. A proposed characterization by volumes or by continuity must specify which representative, or which class of masses, it uses.

Theorem 19.8.2 answers the realization question for the Hopf pair $(H_{n+1},H_ n)$, $n\geq 3$, when the ambient potential is pulled back from $\mathbb P^ n$ and has zero generic Lelong number along the projective hyperplane. Every Hermitian metric on $H_ n$ fails bounded mass, yet the same pulled-back potential realizes the trace data on all compact models. Within the invariant backgrounds $a\alpha +b\beta $, corollary 19.8.5, proposition 19.8.6 give an ideal class compatible with changes of background, addition, positive scaling and decreasing analytic approximation. Exactness persists in every larger Hermitian background. This settles the projective pullback case; general ambient data and background compatibility outside this family remain part of the problem.

33.8.1 Recovery of compatible divisor systems

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Prescribing finitely many divisorial poles leaves a smooth residual background. When the residual has a smooth positive representative, theorem 26.21.2 solves the residual equation for a smooth strictly positive probability measure, and every subsolution at the same scalar whose Lelong numbers along the prescribed divisors are at least the prescribed coefficients differs from this solution by a constant. Consequently a solution in the prescribed ordinary type has the same scalar and a smooth remainder. Passing to compatible data on all modifications requires keeping this positivity and this exact singularity information uniformly.

Passing to all modifications raises two further issues. First, a smooth positive probability measure pulls back to a density that vanishes on the exceptional divisor, and the smooth dual formula cannot be applied to such a density until a degenerate extension has been proved. Second, the residual classes on two models are related explicitly: corollary 24.2.2 subtracts the Bott–Chern class of the effective exceptional divisor while keeping the prescribed exterior derivative. The approximating coefficients converge in linear trace mass, but this formula gives no transformation rule for the nonlinear volume. Exact pullback preserves singularity volumes by proposition 13.3.2; subtracting an exceptional divisorial class changes the background and is a different operation. Assigning an intersection number to a nonclosed form resolves neither the degenerate equation nor the question of volume.

The envelope systems of chapter 25 are compatible families, over the positive Hermitian perturbations of a background, that record representatives of one multiplier-ideal class (definition 25.2.2). Their construction gives an order adjunction and an exact criterion for recovery: the limit as the perturbation vanishes must keep the prescribed ideal type. Under bounded mass, for a big background, theorem 30.5.6 gives a sufficient condition involving only the initial slices: their lower singularity volumes must have a positive limit as the perturbation vanishes. Concavity then gives positive lower volume at every finite parameter, and stretching preserves all local divisorial orders. The ruled-surface example of that chapter has zero residual volume and shows why recovery fails without such a condition.

Problem 33.8.2. Let $X$ fail the bounded mass property, and let $\theta $ be a smooth big background on $X$. Find a condition on a compatible Hermitian envelope system over $\theta $ (definition 25.2.2) that forces recovery of its ideal type at every finite parameter, and that reduces to the criterion of theorem 30.5.6, a positive limiting lower volume of the initial slices, when bounded mass holds. Specify the invariant and the uniform estimates that replace the finite deficit of upper volume, and describe their behavior under modifications.

On the scalar Hopf threefold, theorem 25.6.3 gives a sufficient condition despite unbounded ambient upper mass. If every positive scalar slice is invariant under the elliptic fiber action and the initial transverse limiting model has positive lower volume on $\mathbb P^2$, the system is recovered at every finite parameter and every positive Hermitian perturbation. The symmetry constructs the full transverse system rather than assuming survival of the limiting ideals. Example 25.6.4 supplies nonanalytic ideal data with exact positive transverse volumes. The recovery identity and transverse volumes survive modifications in the exact pulled backgrounds, and smooth gauge transport preserves recovery. A criterion for general nonsymmetric systems and arbitrary new Hermitian backgrounds on modifications remains unresolved.

For a fixed finite divisorial support, proposition 26.21.3 settles the case where the residual stays in the positive cone: if the coefficients converge and the limiting residual background still has a smooth positive representative, the normalized smooth remainders converge smoothly, the singular solutions converge in capacity, their measures converge in total variation, and their logarithmic coefficients remain exact on every modification. Multiplier ideals can nevertheless jump at an integer coefficient. For divisors of sections moving in fixed finite-dimensional spaces, positive limiting weights and residuals give convergence of the full potentials (theorem 15.9.2), in each of the two regimes stated there. Recovery beyond the regime of bounded mass and positive volume, degeneration of residual positivity, and estimates uniform over infinitely many models all require further arguments.

33.9 Sections, kernels and the dimension of growth

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

A Hermitian form is the curvature of a holomorphic line bundle only if it is closed, so the algebraic part of the theory must keep the closed curvature data separate from the Hermitian background. The weighted spaces of sections of chapter 20, their multiplicative variant and their intrinsic flag bodies do this, and their dimension of growth can be strictly smaller than the complex dimension of the manifold.

For the partial Bergman kernels of chapter 27, the upper bound by the envelope is proved in general; a matching lower bound is known in the explicit regimes below, and the general problem is open. Let $L\to X$ be a holomorphic line bundle with a smooth Hermitian metric of curvature $\theta $. If $X$ admits a modification from a compact Kähler manifold and $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ has positive non-pluripolar mass, theorem 27.6.3 proves convergence of the kernel measures normalized in full dimension. The proof keeps track of the relative canonical bundle, whose Jacobian factor corrects both the integrability condition of the ideals and the Hilbert norm. This geometric assumption implies the bounded mass property, although the birational transformation identities themselves do not require it. On a holomorphic submersion with descended data, the equilibrium theorem on the base gives a different exact limit on the total space. On the Hopf $n$-fold, the dimension of the selected space of sections of the $m$th tensor power grows like $m^{n-1}$, so normalizing by $m^ n$ would erase the limit; adding the vertical Hermitian form recovers a nonzero measure, with the factor computed in (27.24).

Isolated point poles that do not descend are treated in theorem 27.8.3: their projected jet conditions differ from the multiplier ideals on the base by only finitely many orders. For a single point pole on the Hopf manifold, theorem 27.9.2 proves the lower asymptotics of the potentials as well as convergence of the kernel measures in total variation. Moving the pole along its fiber leaves every selected space unchanged, so these limits recover the projected equilibrium, while the multiplier-ideal type of the original Hermitian weight can be different.

Theorem 27.11.1 gives a further regime: a logarithmic pole along a section over a hyperplane, with arbitrary compact connected fibers of positive dimension. The growth degree is the dimension of the base. The partial potentials attain the domination envelope of the original singularity on the total space, and the kernel measures converge in total variation for every smooth positive volume used in the norm. The limit on the base is determined by the metric of the line bundle, and its lift keeps exactly the conditional probabilities on the fibers.

For an interior rational Newton polytope with integral affine offset, theorem 27.16.2, theorem 27.16.3 prove a lower bound by the envelope and a positive limit of the measures in the relative growth dimension. Here the smooth weight and the sampling probability are invariant under the torus, the logarithmic weight is strictly convex, and the target is the specified envelope of Newton growth. In lower dimension the limit lies on a real moment shell and differs from every mixed measure formed from the curvature of the envelope and a smooth Hermitian form on the ambient manifold. These results do not identify an arbitrary multiplier-ideal envelope.

The multiplier-ideal family of theorem 27.17.1 has positive growth exponent and potentials converging to its ideal envelope, yet its kernel measures have two different limits: rounding at two critical divisors makes the leading term of the section count depend on the parity of the degree. Convergence of the normalized dimensions is therefore an additional necessary condition for a finite limit of the measures. This selecting weight has zero top non-pluripolar mass, so it lies outside the positive-mass regime above.

For the two isolated base points of a pencil, theorem 27.18.3, theorem 27.18.5 identify the full ideal envelope and prove a global lower bound for the potentials and a positive limit of the measures. The selected spaces consist of three neighboring bands of monomials and have dimension exactly $3m+1$, although their class of competitors at all weights equals the upper ordinary type class. The specified compact logarithmic box and Gaussian norm give intrinsic mass three. Locally uniform convergence away from the poles, convergence in every finite $L^ p$ and uniform convergence after exponentiation coexist with an unbounded additive error at each fixed degree. These spaces are not multiplicative, and this selecting weight also has zero top non-pluripolar mass.

Problem 33.9.1. A Bergman upper bound gives neither. On a manifold that is not Moishezon, normalization by the ambient degree may already force every kernel measure to vanish, for reasons independent of singularity theory.

Locality of Hölder regularity, and regularity of weighted extremal functions under a uniform density condition in capacity, give special cases of the regularity problem; see section 34.19. On a fibration, the comparison of spaces of finite codimension in lemma 27.8.1 suggests a more specific question, which asks for an analytic description of the constraints on sections after descent.

Problem 33.9.2. Let $\pi :X\to B$ be a surjective holomorphic submersion of connected compact complex manifolds with connected fibers, where $B$ is Kähler of dimension $q\geq 1$. Thus a semipositive reference of positive volume does not force the local gap between orders to be absorbed by fixed divisors. Whether such conditions are necessary in general is open.

Fix an integer $r\geq 1$ and, for each positive integer $m$, a nonnegative integer $q_ m$. The model of section 27.12 on $\mathbb C$ has the smooth weight $|z|^{2r}$ and the selecting ideals $(z^{q_ m})$. Problem 33.9.3. Let $C$ be a connected compact Riemann surface, let $x\in C$, and let $(L,h)$ be a smooth Hermitian holomorphic line bundle whose curvature is semipositive and strictly positive on $C\setminus \{ x\} $. Fix an integer $r\geq 1$. Suppose that in a local coordinate $z$ centered at $x$ and a holomorphic frame $e$ one has

\[ |e|_ h^2=\mathrm{e}^{-\phi },\qquad \phi (z)=|z|^{2r}+O(|z|^{2r+1}). \]

Let $\nu $ be a smooth positive volume form with local expression $a(z)\, \mathrm{d}A$ and $a(0)=1$, where $\mathrm{d}A$ is Euclidean area. For positive integers $m$, choose integers $q_ m\to \infty $ with $q_ m=o(m)$. Give

\[ V_ m=H^0(C,L^ m\otimes \mathfrak m_ x^{q_ m}) \]

the norm $\| s\| ^2=\int _ C|s|_{h^ m}^2\, \mathrm{d}\nu $, where $\mathfrak m_ x$ is the ideal sheaf of $x$. Write $B_ m=\sum _ j|s_{m,j}|_{h^ m}^2$ for an orthonormal basis $(s_{m,j})$ of $V_ m$, with value zero when $V_ m=0$. Determine the growth ranges of $q_ m$ for which, for each fixed real $\tau $, every sequence of points $x_ m$ in the coordinate neighborhood satisfying

\[ m|z(x_ m)|^{2r}=q_ m/r+\tau \sqrt{q_ m/r} \]

obeys

\[ \frac{\pi B_ m(x_ m)}{mr^2|z(x_ m)|^{2r-2}} \longrightarrow \Phi (\tau ):=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi }}\int _{-\infty }^{\tau } \mathrm{e}^{-s^2/2}\, \mathrm{d}s. \]

When this transition law fails, determine how further terms of the local weight change its center and scale.

For the data of this problem, theorem 27.13.5 shows that the law holds in the optimal universal range $q_ m=o(m^{1/(r+1)})$, uniformly in the direction of observation; outside that range, a compact example obtained by a holomorphic change of coordinate violates the uncorrected law along a subsequence. For a fixed weight, the exact criterion is (27.102).

In the whole range $q_ m\to \infty $, $q_ m=o(m)$, theorem 27.13.4 centers the transition on the boundary of the curvature quadrature domain and uses the normal length scale $1/(2\sqrt{m\rho })$, where $\rho $ is the density of the curvature. The first effective Taylor coefficient and the ranges of the corrections of finite order are computed in section 27.13.4; a flat perturbation there shows that no fixed finite Taylor jet of the weight determines the center to within the width of the transition for every sublinear sequence. This resolves the problem on compact curves as stated.

In higher dimension, for ordinary point jets of growing total order on the explicit anisotropic product of section 27.15, theorem 27.15.1 proves a Gaussian transition throughout the sublinear range of orders. The correct radial center includes the nonzero remainder terms of the weight, whereas proposition 27.15.2 gives the sharp, smaller range in which the homogeneous center can be used in this class. Theorem 27.15.3 transfers these laws through a submersion with compact connected fibers, with a pulled-back metric and a matched smooth volume; there the isolated selecting weight varies with the tensor power. Mixed or angular remainders, observation near the coordinate faces, other bases and general transfers through fiber selectors remain open directions.

33.10 Equations on singular spaces

On a resolution of a singular space, a pulled-back Hermitian form degenerates, and the pulled-back density can vanish or become singular. Existence on the resolution must therefore be combined with an estimate that allows the solution to descend. A develops potentials, envelopes, ideal equivalence and extended Monge–Ampère measures on reduced irreducible compact unibranch spaces, keeping the original nonclosed background. On projective cones, theorem A.6.1, theorem A.6.2 solve explicit equations, for prescribed angular and radial data, in full ordinary logarithmic classes and in the bounded class of the closed background. The logarithmic construction includes nonclosed deformations that preserve the determinant, and its full solution descends even when the smooth residual is nonconstant on the exceptional fiber. The continuity results discussed in section 34.19 use additional geometric and integrability hypotheses, notably log terminal singularities.

On the regular locus of a compact reduced irreducible Hermitian space of dimension at least two there is a conformal Gauduchon metric uniformly comparable to the original metric; see section 34.19. This existence theorem does not by itself give the nonlinear equations asked for below.

In dimension one, theorem A.7.2 gives a different sufficient condition: the normalization is a torus, the prescribed ordinary type has finitely many logarithmic poles of total weight smaller than the mass of the background, and the measure has a nonnegative $L^ p$ density, $p>1$, with respect to the flat area on the normalization. Convolution with a Green function compensates for the degeneracy of the pulled-back background. The atoms at the poles determine the mass of every potential in the prescribed type and give uniqueness even among solutions that are a priori rough, and the residual descends continuously. The projective cusp of example A.7.3 has an unbounded density on the curve itself although the data on the normalization are smooth. Every background on a curve is closed, so this construction gives no equation with a nonclosed background in higher dimension.

The family of logarithmic densities in theorem A.7.4 makes the integrability issue precise. Densities on the normalization with local form $C_0|z|^{-2}(\log (1/|z|))^{-\gamma }$ are integrable for $\gamma >1$, but they give a solution in the prescribed ordinary type exactly when $\gamma >2$. For $1<\gamma \leq 2$, the constructed solution keeps all multiplier ideals and the same non-pluripolar mass, but loses the lower bound defining the type. Multiplier-ideal equivalence alone therefore does not give a bounded residual. A criterion for general integrable data remains to be found.

Problem 33.10.1. Consider reduced irreducible compact unibranch complex spaces with a Hermitian form, as in A. Beyond the projective cones of section A.6, determine geometric conditions on such spaces and conditions on the measure that give existence and uniqueness of solutions in specified singularity classes. Identify estimates ensuring finite mass, descent from resolutions and continuity, and describe their behavior under common refinements of resolutions.

In the cone examples, the pulled-back density vanishes on the exceptional divisor, and the logarithmic remainder solves an equation in a strictly positive background; the bounded equation for the closed background uses instead an energy that is coercive off that divisor. More general spaces of higher dimension require a replacement for these two controls. The curve construction uses a Green operator on the normalization; a linear construction by traces on a singular space needs further estimates across the singular locus.
