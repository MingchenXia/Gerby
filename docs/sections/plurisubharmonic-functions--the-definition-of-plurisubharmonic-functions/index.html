5.13 The definition of plurisubharmonic functions92A4FA45DFE1

92A4FA45DFE1

In this section, we recall the notion of plurisubharmonic functions. We will also take care of the $0$-dimensional case, which makes a number of induction arguments easier to carry out. None of our references treats the $0$-dimensional case, but the reader can easily verify that the results in this section hold in this exceptional case.

5.13.1 The $1$-dimensional case600FCCB417C9

Let $\Omega $ be a domain (a connected open subset) in $\mathbb {C}$.

Definition 5.13.2.C3781B231934 A subharmonic function on $\Omega $ is a function $\varphi \colon \Omega \rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ satisfying the following three conditions:

  1. 06D657B81863

    $\varphi \not\equiv -\infty $;

  2. 2BAC570CEF93

    $\varphi $ is upper semicontinuous;

  3. 249F615C259B

    $\varphi $ satisfies the sub-mean value inequality: For any $a\in \Omega $ and $r>0$ such that $\overline{B_1(a,r)}\subseteq \Omega $, we have

    \[ \varphi (a)\leq \frac{1}{2\pi }\int _0^{2\pi } \varphi (a+r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta })\, \mathrm{d}\theta .\footnotemark \]
Note. Condition 2 guarantees that $\varphi $ is measurable and locally bounded from above, and hence the integral in Condition 3 makes sense.

We will denote the set of subharmonic functions on $\Omega $ by $\mathrm{SH}(\Omega )$.

Here, $B_1(a,r)\coloneqq \{ z\in \mathbb {C}:|z-a|<r\} $ denotes the open ball with center $a$ and radius $r$.

In fact, for each $a\in \Omega $, in 3, it suffices to require the sub-mean value inequality for all small enough $r>0$.

Intuitively, at a specific point $a\in \Omega $, Condition 2 says $\varphi (a)$ is no smaller than the upper limit of nearby values, while Condition 3 says $\varphi (a)$ is no larger than the mean. This intuition leads to the following rigidity theorem:

Theorem 5.13.3.FDD1E63B4457 Let $\varphi \colon \Omega \rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ be a measurable function. Then the following are equivalent:

  1. E03504FC9B0D

    $\varphi $ is locally integrable and $\Delta \varphi \geq 0$.

  2. D517D85A39B6

    $\varphi $ coincides almost everywhere with a subharmonic function $\psi $ on $\Omega $.

Moreover, the subharmonic function $\psi $ in 2 is unique.

Proof. Suppose first that $\varphi\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$ and $\Delta\varphi\geq0$. Apply the distributional characterization of subharmonic functions to the distribution defined by $\varphi$. It gives a subharmonic representative $\psi$ of this distribution. Equality of the distributions defined by two locally integrable functions is equality almost everywhere, so $\varphi=\psi$ almost everywhere.

Conversely, a subharmonic function on the connected domain $\Omega$ is locally integrable and has positive distributional Laplacian by the same theorem. Replacing it on a null set changes neither its distribution nor its local integrability. Finally, two subharmonic representatives that agree almost everywhere agree at every point: their values are the limits of their averages over shrinking balls. This also proves uniqueness. Multiplying the usual planar Laplacian by a fixed positive normalization constant does not change positivity.

Here in Condition 1, $\Delta \varphi =2\mathrm{i}\partial \bar\partial \varphi $ is the Laplacian in the sense of currents. This is a special case of theorem 5.13.8 below.

This theorem gives a very useful way of constructing subharmonic functions.

5.13.4 The higher dimensional caseEB9F157B42F2

We will fix $n\in \mathbb {N}$ and a domain $\Omega $ (a connected open subset) in $\mathbb {C}^ n$.

See Definition 5.8.1.

A plurisubharmonic function is also called a psh function for short. The relevant notations are indicated in 5.13.5.
Note. Recall that most figures in this book are somewhat misleading: We usually draw a complex dimension as a real dimension. The figures should not be read literally!

Example 5.13.6.DD77B8A4B7A7 When $n=0$, we have a canonical bijection $\mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )\cong \mathbb {R}$.

Example 5.13.7.1C9711BBE591When $n=1$, we have $\mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )=\mathrm{SH}(\Omega )$.

Similar to theorem 5.13.3, we have a rigidity theorem for plurisubharmonic functions as well.

Theorem 5.13.8.2CB6F65A67EE Let $\varphi \colon \Omega \rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ be a measurable function. Then the following are equivalent:

  1. AD9F1D874ACA

    $\varphi $ is locally integrable and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\varphi \geq 0$;

  2. B21823D3C183

    $\varphi $ coincides almost everywhere with a plurisubharmonic function $\psi $ on $\Omega $.

Moreover, the plurisubharmonic function $\psi $ is unique.

Proof. In positive dimension, regard a locally integrable $\varphi$ as a real distribution. If $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm c}\varphi\geq0$, the plurisubharmonic representative theorem supplies a plurisubharmonic function $\psi$ representing this distribution, hence equal to $\varphi$ almost everywhere. Conversely, that theorem proves local integrability and positivity of $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm c}\psi$ for every plurisubharmonic $\psi$; these properties are unchanged by almost-everywhere equality.

Uniqueness follows from the shrinking-ball formula for the subharmonic representative. This is essential: an arbitrary pointwise representative of a positive distribution need not itself be plurisubharmonic. In dimension zero the domain is a point, with its counting measure; local integrability is finiteness, so both conditions simply say that the value is finite.

Here, the operator $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$ is normalized so that
\[ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi }\partial \overline{\partial }. \]

For the proof, we refer to [Proposition 1.43, GZ17].

Plurisubharmonic functions have nice functorialities:

Proposition 5.13.9.5DCB508EA9AD Let $n'\in \mathbb {N}$ and $\Omega '\subseteq \mathbb {C}^{n'}$ be a domain. Given any holomorphic map $f\colon \Omega \rightarrow \Omega '$ and any $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega ')$, exactly one of the following cases occurs:

  1. 11DBE4FA0035

    $f^*\varphi \equiv -\infty $;

  2. EEDAC90E09ED

    $f^*\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$.

Proof. For positive source and target dimensions, apply the holomorphic pullback theorem. Its conclusion is precisely that $\varphi\circ f$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty$ on each connected component. Here the source is connected, so there is just one alternative to consider. They are disjoint because the definition of plurisubharmonicity excludes the function identically $-\infty$.

If the source has dimension zero, it is a point: the pullback is either a finite number or $-\infty$. If the target has dimension zero, $\varphi$ is finite and constant, and so is its pullback. Thus these cases satisfy the same assertion.

We refer to [Proposition 1.44, GZ17] for the proof
Note. Recall that the statement of [Proposition 1.44, GZ17] is flawed. One has to reduce to the case where Case 1 does not occur before following their proof.
.

For each $n\in \mathbb {N}$, $a\in \mathbb {C}^ n$ and $r>0$, we write

7D0D660F11F8 5.13.10
\begin{equation} B_ n(a,r)=\left\{ z\in \mathbb {C}^ n: |z-a|<r \right\} . \end{equation}

Proposition 5.13.11.2FF7CC0E8132 Let $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(B_ n(a,r_0))$ for some $r_0>0$. Then the function

\[ (-\infty ,\log r_0)\rightarrow \mathbb {R},\quad \log r\mapsto \sup _{B_ n(a,r)}\varphi \]

is convex and increasing.

Proof. Write $M(r)=\sup_{B_n(a,r)}\varphi$. This is finite: upper semicontinuity bounds $\varphi$ above on the closure of each smaller ball, and a plurisubharmonic function not identically $-\infty$ cannot equal $-\infty$ on a ball. It is increasing by inclusion of balls. In dimension zero it is constant.

Assume $n\geq1$ and fix $0<r_1<r_2<r_0$. Let $A\log r+B$ interpolate $M(r_1)$ and $M(r_2)$. On every complex line through $a$, comparison with this harmonic function on the annulus gives \[\varphi(z)\leq A\log|z-a|+B\qquad(r_1\leq|z-a|\leq r_2).\] The boundary bounds also hold on the spheres: if its value at a sphere point exceeded the interior supremum by $\delta>0$, upper semicontinuity would bound nearby values by that value plus $\delta/4$. A sufficiently small centered circle has at least one third of its length on the interior side, so its average would be strictly below the center value, contradicting the submean inequality. For $|z-a|<r_1$, the bound $\varphi(z)\leq M(r_1)$ suffices, because $A\geq0$. Taking the supremum over $|z-a|<r$, where $r_1<r<r_2$, yields \[M(r)\leq \frac{\log r_2-\log r}{\log r_2-\log r_1}M(r_1)+\frac{\log r-\log r_1}{\log r_2-\log r_1}M(r_2).\] This is convexity in $\log r$.

See [Theorem 4.1.13, Hor07] for the case $n>1$ and [Corollary 2.4, Bou17] for the general case.

Proposition 5.13.12.D8BC10548372 Let $a<b$ be two real numbers. Let $f\colon (a,b)\rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ be a function. Define

\[ g\colon \{ z\in \mathbb {C}:a<\operatorname{Re}z<b\} \rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty ),\quad z\mapsto f(\operatorname{Re}z). \]

Suppose that $g$ is subharmonic. Then $f$ is convex. In particular, $f$ takes real values only.

Proof. Convolve $g$ on smaller strips with a nonnegative smooth radial approximate identity. The regularizations $g_\varepsilon$ are smooth and subharmonic and decrease pointwise to $g$ by subharmonic regularization. They are still invariant under imaginary translations, hence have the form $g_\varepsilon(z)=f_\varepsilon(\operatorname{Re}z)$. Therefore $f_\varepsilon''=\Delta g_\varepsilon\geq0$, and each $f_\varepsilon$ is convex on its interval of definition.

Passing to the decreasing limit in the convexity inequality proves convexity of $f$ in the extended sense. This limit is finite everywhere: it is bounded above locally because $g$ is subharmonic, and if it were $-\infty$ at an interior point, convexity with a point beyond any other given point would force it to be $-\infty$ throughout $(a,b)$. That would make $g\equiv-\infty$, which is excluded. Thus $f$ is a real-valued convex function.

See [Theorem 2.12, HK76] for a more general result.

5.13.13 The manifold case7BAC2CC7335C

Let $X$ be a complex manifold. In the whole book, complex manifolds are assumed to be paracompact, namely, all connected components have countable bases.

Definition 5.13.14.D072C98719AA A plurisubharmonic function on $X$ is a function $\varphi \colon X\rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ such that for any $x\in X$, there exists an open neighborhood $U$ of $x$ in $X$, an integer $n\in \mathbb {N}$, a domain $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb {C}^ n$ and a biholomorphic map $F\colon \Omega \rightarrow U$ such that $F^*(\varphi |_ U)\in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$.

The set of plurisubharmonic functions on $X$ is denoted by $\mathrm{PSH}(X)$.

Example 5.13.15.67A5152A77BC When $X$ is a domain in $\mathbb {C}^ n$, the notions of plurisubharmonic functions in definition 5.13.14 and in definition 5.8.1 coincide.

Example 5.13.16.399FC71A61E3 Write $\{ X_ i\} _{i\in I}$ for the set of connected components of $X$. Then we have a natural bijection

\[ \mathrm{PSH}(X) \cong \prod _{i\in I} \mathrm{PSH}(X_ i). \]

Here the product is in the category of sets. In particular, if $X=\varnothing $, then $\mathrm{PSH}(X)$ is a singleton.

This example allows us to reduce to the case of connected manifolds when studying general plurisubharmonic functions.

Proposition 5.13.17.726DDFDDA8F2 Let $Y$ be a complex manifold and $f\colon Y\rightarrow X$ be a holomorphic map. Then for any $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$, exactly one of the following cases occurs:

  1. CAAB826BFF14

    $f^*\varphi $ is identically $-\infty $ on some connected component of $Y$;

  2. 13566C9390E6

    $f^*\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(Y)$.

Proof. Work on one connected component $Y_0$ of $Y$. In coordinate neighborhoods of $Y$ and $X$, holomorphic pullback says that $\varphi\circ f$ is either plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty$. A plurisubharmonic function cannot be identically $-\infty$ on a nonempty open subset of a connected domain, by its local integrability. Thus a neighborhood on which the pullback is identically $-\infty$ cannot overlap a neighborhood on which it is plurisubharmonic. The two types form disjoint open subsets of $Y_0$; connectedness leaves only one type. If the first type never occurs on any component, the pullback is plurisubharmonic on $Y$. Zero-dimensional charts are covered by the finite-value convention.

This proposition follows from proposition 5.13.9 applied separately on each connected component of $Y$. theorem 5.13.8 implies immediately the general form of the rigidity theorem:

Theorem 5.13.18.FEFFC91695E0 Let $\varphi \colon X\rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ be a measurable function. Then the following are equivalent:

  1. 3BB9EAC495FC

    $\varphi $ is locally integrable and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\varphi \geq 0$;

  2. FB5BA344B4A3

    $\varphi $ coincides almost everywhere with a plurisubharmonic function $\psi $ on $X$.

Moreover, the plurisubharmonic function $\psi $ in 2 is unique.

Proof. Restrict a locally integrable $\varphi$ with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm c}\varphi\geq0$ to each coordinate chart and apply the local representative theorem. The resulting plurisubharmonic representatives agree almost everywhere on overlaps, hence everywhere there by the uniqueness part of the same theorem. They therefore glue to a plurisubharmonic function on $X$ representing $\varphi$.

Conversely, local integrability and positivity hold in every chart for a plurisubharmonic function and are preserved by almost-everywhere equality. The same overlap argument proves uniqueness globally. On a zero-dimensional component all assertions reduce to finiteness at each point.

See Definition 5.47.2.

When $X$ has dimension $1$, a pluripolar set is called a polar set. We say some property about objects on $X$ holds quasi-everywhere if it holds outside a pluripolar set.

Theorem 5.13.19 (Josefson's theorem).DB05CEAF06C1 Let $E\subseteq \mathbb {C}^ n$ be a pluripolar set. Then there is $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(\mathbb {C}^ n)$ such that $\varphi |_ E\equiv -\infty $.

Proof. Apply Josefson's theorem, proved earlier in the collection to $E$. It constructs a function $V\in\mathrm{PSH}(\mathbb C^n)$ with $E\subset\{V=-\infty\}$, in fact with the additional bound $V(z)\leq\log^+|z|$. Taking $\varphi=V$ gives the assertion. The earlier construction is a proof of this stronger statement, so no additional external theorem is being invoked here. In dimension zero a pluripolar set is empty and a finite constant suffices.

See [Corollary 4.41, GZ17] for the proof of a more general result. There is also a global version of Josefson's theorem:

Theorem 5.13.20.0ED3DB1BD7C2 Assume that $X$ is a compact complex manifold and $E\subseteq X$ is a pluripolar set. Then there is a quasi-plurisubharmonic function $\varphi $ on $X$ with $\varphi |_ E\equiv -\infty $.

For a proof, see [Vu19]. The notion of quasi-plurisubharmonic functions will be recalled in definition 5.17.1 below.
