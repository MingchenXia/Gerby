1.5 Introduction

Definitions, results, and proofs on this subject.

Introduction

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

A singular potential can be studied through its growth near its poles, through the holomorphic functions that remain integrable there, or through its Monge–Ampère measure. These descriptions lead to ordinary singularity types, multiplier ideals and curvature measures. Global pluripotential theory relates them and asks which singularities can be approximated by analytic ones. This book develops that theory for Hermitian backgrounds, starting from Singularities in global pluripotential theory [SGPT].

In a fixed Kähler class, all bounded potentials have the same Monge–Ampère mass. The non-pluripolar mass of a singular potential can therefore be compared with a fixed total mass, and this comparison connects analytic approximation, envelopes and energy. A nonclosed Hermitian background changes this relation at its first step: the mass can vary even among smooth potentials. Ordinary singularity type, which identifies potentials with bounded difference, no longer determines a single mass. Envelopes and multiplier ideals remain available, but their connection to mass requires different arguments.

This loss of closedness is distinct from the geometry of the manifold. A non-Kähler manifold can carry closed positive currents, and a Kähler manifold can be equipped with a nonclosed Hermitian form. Let $X$ be a connected compact complex manifold of dimension $n\geq 1$, and let $\theta $ be a smooth real $(1,1)$-form on $X$. A function $u$ is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic if it is locally the sum of a plurisubharmonic function and a smooth function and the current $\theta _ u=\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ is positive. If $\theta $ is closed, this current is locally a complex Hessian. For a general background, a smooth local correction still makes $u$ plurisubharmonic, but leaves a smooth remainder in the current. Compactness, regularization and multiplier ideals thus survive locally; the new difficulties arise when these constructions are related globally.

Closedness, mass and energy

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Let $\omega $ be a Hermitian form on $X$, that is, a smooth strictly positive $(1,1)$-form. If $\omega $ is Kähler, every smooth real potential $u$ with $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u>0$ satisfies

\[ \int _ X(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^ n=\int _ X\omega ^ n. \]

The identity extends to bounded $\omega $-plurisubharmonic potentials. For singular potentials, the non-pluripolar product can have smaller total mass, and the difference measures the mass excluded at the poles.

For a general Hermitian form, integration by parts gives instead

\[ \int _ X(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^ n-\int _ X\omega ^ n =\sum _{k=1}^{n-1}\binom nk \int _ X u(\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^{k-1}\wedge \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(\omega ^{n-k}) \]

for every smooth $u$. The derivatives of the background already affect the mass before singularities appear. Chapter 2 proves that mass invariance is equivalent to $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(\omega ^ j)=0$ for $1\leq j\leq n-1$. The usual global comparison inequality would force this invariance, so a comparison argument in an arbitrary Hermitian background must account for these derivatives.

Once mass varies, its upper and lower bounds become separate questions. The scalar Hopf manifolds of dimension at least three have unbounded masses of smooth potentials with positive perturbed form, while the standard Hopf threefold has a positive infimum of those masses. A different compact threefold carries a balanced metric with bounded masses whose infimum is zero (chapter 8, section 26.18). Neither bound follows from the other. The envelope and measure constructions below accommodate this variation; 9 develops volume comparison and equation theory under explicit mass bounds.

Mass conservation does not by itself restore the Kähler energy. At a smooth potential $u$ with $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u>0$, consider the one-form

\[ \alpha _ u(v)=\int _ X v(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^ n \]

on the space of such potentials, where $v$ is a smooth real tangent direction. For a Kähler form, it is the differential of the Monge–Ampère energy. In dimension at least two, a primitive even near the zero potential forces $\mathrm d\omega =0$ (theorem 3.1.3). On a non-Kähler surface, a Gauduchon metric conserves mass but has no such primitive. Perron interpolation and Legendre duality can still be constructed by envelopes; an interpretation through a metric energy needs a separate justification.

A Gauduchon metric supplies a conserved linear trace in place of the varying nonlinear mass. Its form $\gamma $ satisfies $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(\gamma ^{n-1})=0$, and hence, for every $\theta $-plurisubharmonic potential,

\[ \int _ X(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)\wedge \gamma ^{n-1} =\int _ X\theta \wedge \gamma ^{n-1}. \]

This trace measure determines $u$ up to an additive constant. In a fixed background, for a smooth positive volume form $\mathrm dV$ and the normalization $\int _ Xu\, \mathrm dV=0$, weak convergence of trace measures is equivalent to $L^1$ convergence of potentials (chapter 5). The trace stays finite even when the nonlinear Monge–Ampère mass is infinite. Extended non-pluripolar products and finite damped measures make it possible to study such potentials, while passage from damped to undamped measures requires control near the poles.

Singularity types and envelopes

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Multiplier ideals record the holomorphic functions locally square integrable against $\mathrm e^{-ku}$, for every $k>0$. This gives a second equivalence relation on potentials, coarser than ordinary singularity type. To compare the two, fix $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and take envelopes in the same background. The ordinary envelope $P_\theta [u]$ is the upper semicontinuous regularization of the supremum of the $\theta $-plurisubharmonic $h\leq 0$ with $h\leq u+C$ for some constant $C$. The multiplier-ideal envelope $P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}$ uses instead the $h\leq 0$ with the same multiplier ideals as $u$ at every positive weight.

In a closed big class on a compact Kähler manifold, for potentials of positive mass, the equality of these two envelopes characterizes $\mathcal I$-good singularities and is equivalent to a mass identity and approximation by analytic singularities. In the Hermitian setting, analytic approximation still relates the envelopes even when a mass identity is unavailable. Correcting the curvature errors keeps the dyadic approximants in a positive Hermitian background, and their envelopes decrease to the multiplier-ideal envelope. In such a positive background, comparing the two envelopes defines a uniform topology on ordinary classes in which their equality characterizes the closure of the analytic classes, including classes with infinite-mass representatives (theorem 16.6.3). This topology can identify distinct ordinary classes.

The obstacle in an envelope is part of this construction. A smooth change $\theta \mapsto \theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\chi $ transforms potentials by $u\mapsto u-\chi $ and transforms their obstacles as well. Keeping only the obstacle zero need not give an invariant topology; allowing all smooth obstacles does. Whether equality of the envelopes at zero implies equality at every smooth obstacle is therefore a substantive question. It holds in a smooth big background with bounded mass when the multiplier-ideal envelope has positive lower volume, as well as on curves and on certain product loci without those volume conditions (proposition 30.8.2, corollary 30.13.2, corollary 16.6.44). The general case at zero volume remains open.

Envelopes and mass give complementary ways to compare singularities. An ordinary model is a fixed point $P_\theta [u]=u$, and the infimum and supremum of mass over an ordinary type define its lower and upper singularity volumes. These volumes need not agree. In a smooth big background with bounded mass, volume comparison gives a completely metrizable topology on the models of positive lower volume. At zero volume, the comparison can fail to distinguish models, so an extension must retain information that mass has lost.

On curves, the polar curvature—the part of $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ carried by the pole set—determines a model. Total variation of this measure gives a complete metric that includes zero-volume models, with the ideal models as the closure of the analytic models. The construction extends to certain higher-dimensional product loci without an ambient mass bound (proposition 16.6.43). A general higher-dimensional model contains more information: residual top-degree mass may lie on a diffuse pole set although every polar curvature current on every modification vanishes. Distinct models can even have the same residual measure and both volumes, while their comparison distance is positive (proposition 16.6.78). Thus a classification by measures already has limitations in closed Kähler backgrounds.

Restriction raises a related question. A potential may be identically $-\infty $ on a submanifold even though its generic Lelong number there is zero. In this case, the restrictions of its ambient multiplier ideals define finite divisorial data on every modification. Trace envelopes turn this data into constraints on potentials of the submanifold. Exact realization is possible on curves and in sufficiently large backgrounds on Kähler targets, and projective pullback data also admit exact realizations on Hopf manifolds of unbounded mass (theorem 19.8.2). Thus failure of a mass bound does not itself prevent realization. Exact divisorial data nevertheless need not determine the Monge–Ampère mass, or a single ordinary type.

Equations with prescribed data

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

The varying mass also changes the normalization of an equation. Given a smooth real function $F$ on $X$, one seeks a smooth pair $(u,c)$ satisfying

\[ (\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^ n=c\, \mathrm e^ F\, \mathrm dV, \qquad \omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u>0,\qquad \sup _ Xu=0. \]

In a Kähler background, integration determines $c>0$ before solving for $u$. In a Hermitian background the left-hand mass depends on the unknown potential, so the constant must be found together with it. Such a smooth pair exists and is unique. The linearization gives an adjoint Gauduchon probability measure describing the variation of $\log c$ with the density, and leads to a variational volume defined by Gauduchon test forms. This volume remains finite even on manifolds with unbounded upper Monge–Ampère mass.

Positive variational volume, however, need not imply that a background is big, meaning that it admits a potential $\rho $ with $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho \geq \delta \omega $ for some $\delta >0$. A nef nonclosed background on a projective manifold has positive variational volume and fails this condition (theorem 26.13.2). Up to a smooth change of representative, the same example occurs at a finite maximal Chern–Ricci time (theorem 26.20.5). An argument for geometric convergence that needs bigness therefore cannot substitute positive variational volume for it.

For a nonnegative, nonzero $L^ p$ density, $p>1$, continuous solutions still exist and the positive constant is still unique, but uniqueness of the potential is a separate matter. A smooth density with zeros may have distinct normalized smooth solutions. For a continuous density whose positive set is connected and dense, bounded solutions are unique up to constants (theorem 11.4.1). Selection by approximation must also be distinguished from uniqueness. Moreover, when singularities are prescribed, distinct divisorial types can have the same normalization functional and supporting Gauduchon probabilities (chapter 15); the scalar response does not recover the full type.

Replacing the determinant by an intermediate elementary symmetric polynomial leads to the Hessian measure $H_ m(u)=(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^ m\wedge \omega ^{n-m}$, for $2\leq m<n$. Its admissible cone permits negative eigenvalues of the perturbed form, so bounded products require capacity estimates beyond the plurisubharmonic calculus. Derivatives of the background again obstruct the usual comparison inequality. Rescaling one potential and working on a sufficiently small sublevel set, however, allows the torsion error to be absorbed. The resulting modified comparison gives strict domination on an arbitrary Hermitian background. For example, for every $a>0$ and smooth real $F$, the equation $H_ m(u)=\mathrm e^{au+F}\omega ^ n$ has exactly one bounded admissible solution, and it is smooth (12.12.10). Thus the increasing dependence on the potential can recover uniqueness and regularity even when mass is not conserved.

To prescribe singularities as well as the measure, one fixes a reference potential and uses relative envelopes to control the solution. For general finite non-pluripolar measures, 9 proves existence in two regimes: semipositive big backgrounds of positive lower mass, with a model reference admitting a competitor in a smaller background, and arbitrary smooth big backgrounds of bounded mass, with a model reference of positive lower volume. These solutions belong to a relative envelope class. Membership in the prescribed ordinary type requires the stronger conclusion that their difference from the reference is bounded; additional estimates give this conclusion for suitable data.

Geometric constructions

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Logarithmic poles give concrete tests of the theory. On a resolution, removing a prescribed divisorial pole can leave a strictly positive residual form, where a bounded equation determines the remaining potential. A divisor forced by the background behaves differently: it belongs to the type of minimal singularities, and removing it can force the current to descend. The constructions in section 14.21, section 14.22 distinguish a freely prescribed pole from such a compulsory one.

The regularity of a potential also affects the geometry of its current. When that current is smooth and strictly positive off an analytic set, a Hölder potential places the analytic set at finite distance, making the regular metric incomplete if the set is nonempty (theorem 1.18.1). Boundedness alone is insufficient: radial examples in closed Kähler classes have continuous minimal residuals and complete regular ends. The integral controlling the potential differs from the one controlling path length. This distinction comes from regularity, even before closedness is lost.

Holomorphic sections link singularities to geometry through a closed line-bundle curvature, so counting sections cannot measure an arbitrary Hermitian Monge–Ampère mass. Gauduchon degrees control section growth and flag valuations, while fibrations can concentrate equilibrium measures on a lower-dimensional base. Partial Bergman kernels connect these section spaces to envelopes of singular weights. Closed Bott–Chern classes and locally conformally Kähler structures give further settings where the differential constraints can be used more precisely.

Reading the book

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

We assume familiarity with complex manifolds, differential forms and currents. Chapter 1 develops the plurisubharmonic tools used throughout. On a first reading, the maximum principle, normalized compactness and exponential integrability are the starting points; the longer approximation and descent arguments can be consulted when needed. The mass and energy calculations explain the obstructions above, and Gauduchon traces, non-pluripolar products and the Hopf construction complete the foundations.

The equation chapters develop smooth and weak estimates and comparison principles; the Hessian chapter examines how these arguments change when positivity is weakened. Envelopes and analytic approximation then develop the comparison of ordinary and ideal types; interpolation, restriction and holomorphic sections connect it to geometry. 9 gives the volume and equation theory under mass hypotheses. The appendix explains descent to compact unibranch spaces and solves equations on cones, normalized curves and cuspidal products. Open questions are collected in chapter 33, and the sources and precise dependencies in chapter 34; monograph references also appear where they are used.
