4.1 Potentials, energy and capacity765E6C8792CB

765E6C8792CB

Polar sets were introduced in subsection 3.4.25 as the sets that lie locally in the pole set of a nontrivial subharmonic function. Every polar set has Lebesgue measure zero, but the converse fails: a sphere is a Lebesgue null set, yet its normalized surface measure has a bounded potential (example 4.1.21), and we shall see that this prevents the sphere from being polar. In this chapter we introduce two quantities adapted to the Laplacian, the energy of a measure and the capacity of a set. They characterize the polar sets, and they show that the exceptional sets of the limit theorems of section 3.4 are polar.

The energy of a positive measure is the integral of its potential against the measure itself. The Newtonian kernel, and the logarithmic kernel on measures of total mass zero, define positive definite energies, and minimizing the energy over the probability measures on a compact set gives its equilibrium measure. Measures with finite continuous potentials give no mass to polar sets, and by the continuity principle (theorem 4.1.10) every compactly supported measure whose potential is finite almost everywhere with respect to the measure itself is a countable sum of such measures. We also construct a polar Cantor set carrying an atom-free probability measure (proposition 4.1.12); this measure reappears in example 13.4.11.

Capacity is defined by an extremal problem for subharmonic functions. For a compact set $K$ in a ball $B$, let $u_ K$ be the upper semicontinuous regularization of the supremum of all nonpositive subharmonic functions on $B$ that are at most $-1$ on $K$. The relative capacity $C_ B(K)$ is the total mass of the Riesz measure of $u_ K$. A compact set is nonpolar if and only if it carries a nonzero measure of finite energy, if and only if its relative capacity is positive (theorem 4.1.17). With Choquet's capacitability theorem this extends to arbitrary sets with compact closure in $B$: such a set is polar if and only if its outer capacity vanishes (corollary 4.1.24). The main application, theorem 4.1.26, has two parts. If subharmonic functions $v_ j$ converge to $v$ in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$, then the inequality $\varlimsup _ jv_ j\leq v$ of Hartogs' lemma (theorem 3.4.20) is an equality outside a polar set. Moreover, the upper envelope of a family of subharmonic functions that is locally uniformly bounded above agrees with its upper semicontinuous regularization outside a polar set, which sharpens theorem 3.4.23. The chapter closes with the Dirichlet energy of Green potentials and the quasi-continuity of subharmonic functions.

Throughout this chapter $d\geq 2$; in dimension one the empty set is the only polar set (subsection 3.4.25).

4.1.1 Kernel energies and equilibrium measuresFF9D3CA4D463

Recall the fundamental solution $E_ d$ of (3.1.14), the kernel $\Gamma _ d=-E_ d$ of $-\Delta $, and the area $s_{d-1}$ of the unit sphere. For energy estimates we need kernels that are nonnegative on the sets under consideration, so we fix a compact set $K\subset \mathbb R^ d$. For $d\geq 3$ we put

\[ k(x,y)=\Gamma _ d(x-y)=\frac{|x-y|^{2-d}}{(d-2)s_{d-1}}. \]

In dimension two we fix a scale $D>\operatorname {diam}K$ and put

\[ k_ D(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi }\log \frac{D}{|x-y|}=\Gamma _2(x-y)+\frac{\log D}{2\pi }. \]

Both kernels equal $+\infty $ on the diagonal and are nonnegative on $K\times K$.

By a measure on $K$ we mean a finite positive Borel measure on $\mathbb R^ d$ supported on $K$; it is a Radon measure by proposition 2.2.2. The potential and the energy of such a measure $\mu $ are

\[ U^\mu (x)=\int k(x,y)\, \mathrm d\mu (y),\qquad I(\mu )=\iint k(x,y)\, \mathrm d\mu (x)\, \mathrm d\mu (y), \]

with $k_ D$ in place of $k$ when $d=2$; when the scale must be specified, we write $I_ D$. We call $U^\mu $ and $I(\mu )$ the Newtonian potential and the Newtonian energy of $\mu $ if $d\geq 3$, and its logarithmic potential and logarithmic energy if $d=2$. For two measures $\mu $ and $\nu $ on $K$, their mutual energy is $I(\mu ,\nu )=\iint k(x,y)\, \mathrm d\mu (x)\, \mathrm d\nu (y)$. The same integrals define the energy and the mutual energy of finite signed measures on $K$ whenever the kernel is integrable for the product of the total variations.

The potential $U^\mu $ is lower semicontinuous on $\mathbb R^ d$: the kernel is a lower semicontinuous function of $x$ with values in $(-\infty ,\infty ]$, and it is bounded below for $x$ in a compact set and $y\in K$, so Fatou's lemma applies after adding a constant. Moreover, $U^\mu $ is related to the fundamental potential $E_ d*\mu $ of proposition 3.4.11 by $U^\mu =-E_ d*\mu $ if $d\geq 3$ and $U^\mu =-E_2*\mu +(2\pi )^{-1}\mu (K)\log D$ if $d=2$. Replacing $D$ by another scale $D'>\operatorname {diam}K$ adds $(2\pi )^{-1}\mu (K)^2\log (D'/D)$ to the energy; in particular, whether $\mu $ has finite energy does not depend on the scale.

Fundamental potentials satisfy a reciprocity law. Let $\mu $ and $\nu $ be finite positive measures with compact supports, and let $M$ be an upper bound of $E_ d$ on the compact set $\operatorname{Supp}\mu -\operatorname{Supp}\nu $. Applying Tonelli's theorem to the function $(x,y)\mapsto M-E_ d(x-y)$, which is nonnegative on $\operatorname{Supp}\mu \times \operatorname{Supp}\nu $, using $E_ d(-z)=E_ d(z)$ and subtracting the finite number $M\mu (\mathbb R^ d)\nu (\mathbb R^ d)$, we obtain

718E110735E0 4.1.2
\begin{equation} \int (E_ d*\nu )\, \mathrm d\mu =\int (E_ d*\mu )\, \mathrm d\nu , \end{equation}

where both sides lie in $[-\infty ,\infty )$.

The positivity of the energy rests on the Gaussian kernels $\mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}$, $t>0$: each of them is an $L^2$ inner product of two Gaussians, and both $k$ and $k_ D$ are obtained from them by integration in $t$.

Lemma 4.1.3.21858171114B Let $\sigma $ and $\tau $ be finite real signed measures on $\mathbb R^ d$, and for $t>0$ put

\[ Q_ t(\sigma ,\tau )=\iint \mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}\, \mathrm d\sigma (x)\, \mathrm d\tau (y),\qquad h_ t^\sigma (z)=\int \mathrm e^{-2t|z-x|^2}\, \mathrm d\sigma (x), \]

and $Q_ t(\sigma )=Q_ t(\sigma ,\sigma )$. Then $h_ t^\sigma $ is a bounded continuous function, and

FD55A94B512F 4.1.4
\begin{equation} Q_ t(\sigma ,\tau )=\left(\frac{4t}{\pi }\right)^{d/2}\int _{\mathbb R^ d}h_ t^\sigma (z)\, h_ t^\tau (z)\, \mathrm d z. \end{equation}

In particular $Q_ t(\sigma )\geq 0$. If $Q_ t(\sigma )=0$ for arbitrarily large $t$, then $\sigma =0$.

Proof. The function $h_ t^\sigma $ is bounded by $|\sigma |(\mathbb R^ d)$, and it is continuous by dominated convergence. Completing the square gives $|z-x|^2+|z-y|^2=2|z-\tfrac 12(x+y)|^2+\tfrac 12|x-y|^2$. Since $\int _{\mathbb R^ d}\mathrm e^{-4t|w|^2}\, \mathrm dw=(\pi /4t)^{d/2}$, it follows that

\[ \mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}=\left(\frac{4t}{\pi }\right)^{d/2}\int _{\mathbb R^ d}\mathrm e^{-2t|z-x|^2}\, \mathrm e^{-2t|z-y|^2}\, \mathrm d z. \]

We integrate this identity against $\sigma \otimes \tau $. Fubini's theorem applies, because the integral of the absolute value of the integrand with respect to $\mathrm dz\otimes |\sigma |\otimes |\tau |$ is at most $(\pi /4t)^{d/2}|\sigma |(\mathbb R^ d)|\tau |(\mathbb R^ d)$, and integrating first in $x$ and in $y$ gives (4.1.4). For $\tau =\sigma $ it shows that $Q_ t(\sigma )$ is a positive multiple of $\int (h_ t^\sigma )^2\, \mathrm dz$. Hence $Q_ t(\sigma )\geq 0$, and if $Q_ t(\sigma )=0$, then $h_ t^\sigma =0$ almost everywhere, hence everywhere, because $h_ t^\sigma $ is continuous.

Now suppose that $Q_{t_ j}(\sigma )=0$ for a sequence $t_ j\to \infty $. The functions $g_ t(z)=(2t/\pi )^{d/2}\mathrm e^{-2t|z|^2}$ are nonnegative and have integral one, and $\int _{|z|\geq \delta }g_ t\, \mathrm d z\to 0$ as $t\to \infty $ for every $\delta >0$. Hence $g_ t*f\to f$ uniformly on $\mathbb R^ d$ for every $f\in C_ c(\mathbb R^ d)$: in the integral defining $g_ t*f-f$ we use the uniform continuity of $f$ on the region $|z|<\delta $ and the smallness of the Gaussian tail on the region $|z|\geq \delta $. Since $g_{t_ j}*\sigma =(2t_ j/\pi )^{d/2}h_{t_ j}^\sigma =0$, Fubini's theorem and the symmetry of $g_ t$ give

\[ \int (g_{t_ j}*f)\, \mathrm d\sigma =\int f\, (g_{t_ j}*\sigma )\, \mathrm d z=0. \]

Letting $j\to \infty $, we get $\int f\, \mathrm d\sigma =0$ for every $f\in C_ c(\mathbb R^ d)$. Thus the positive and negative parts of $\sigma $ define the same positive linear functional on $C_ c(\mathbb R^ d)$, and the uniqueness assertion of theorem 2.3.3 shows that they are equal, that is, $\sigma =0$. $\square$

Proposition 4.1.5.09A0D05A868F Let $\mu $ and $\nu $ be measures on $K$ with finite energy.

  1. If $d\geq 3$, then $I(\mu ,\nu )$ is finite and $I(\mu ,\nu )^2\leq I(\mu )I(\nu )$. The energy is a positive definite quadratic form on the real linear span of the measures on $K$ with finite energy.

  2. If $d=2$, then $I_ D(\mu ,\nu )$ is finite. The energy $I_ D$ is a positive definite quadratic form on the space of finite signed measures on $K$ of total mass zero whose positive and negative parts have finite energy, and it satisfies the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on this space.

Proof. By (4.1.4), the expression $Q_ t(\sigma ,\tau )$ of lemma 4.1.3 is $(4t/\pi )^{d/2}$ times the $L^2$ inner product of $h_ t^\sigma $ and $h_ t^\tau $. Hence, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in $z$,

F2F2EC96C26E 4.1.6
\begin{equation} Q_ t(\mu ,\nu )\leq Q_ t(\mu )^{1/2}Q_ t(\nu )^{1/2} \end{equation}

for positive measures $\mu $ and $\nu $.

The case $d\geq 3$. Put $a=(d-2)/2>0$ and $c_ a=\int _0^\infty s^{a-1}\mathrm e^{-s}\, \mathrm d s\in (0,\infty )$. The substitution $s=tr^2$ gives

\[ r^{2-d}=\frac{1}{c_ a}\int _0^\infty t^{a-1}\mathrm e^{-t r^2}\, \mathrm d t,\qquad r>0, \]

and both sides are $+\infty $ for $r=0$. By Tonelli's theorem,

\[ I(\mu ,\nu )=\kappa \int _0^\infty t^{a-1}Q_ t(\mu ,\nu )\, \mathrm d t,\qquad \kappa =\frac{1}{(d-2)s_{d-1}c_ a}. \]

Applying (4.1.6) and then the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the measure $t^{a-1}\, \mathrm dt$, we obtain $I(\mu ,\nu )\leq I(\mu )^{1/2}I(\nu )^{1/2}$. Now let $\sigma $ be a real linear combination of measures on $K$ with finite energy. Its total variation is dominated by a combination of the same measures with nonnegative coefficients, so the inequality just proved shows that $\iint k\, \mathrm d|\sigma |\, \mathrm d|\sigma |<\infty $. Consequently Fubini's theorem applies to the integrand $t^{a-1}\mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}$, and

\[ I(\sigma )=\kappa \int _0^\infty t^{a-1}Q_ t(\sigma )\, \mathrm d t\geq 0 \]

by lemma 4.1.3. If $I(\sigma )=0$, then $Q_ t(\sigma )=0$ for almost every $t>0$, in particular for arbitrarily large $t$, and lemma 4.1.3 gives $\sigma =0$.

The case $d=2$. For $0<r\leq D$ we have

\[ \log \frac Dr=\frac12\int _0^\infty \frac{\mathrm e^{-t r^2}-\mathrm e^{-tD^2}}{t}\, \mathrm d t. \]

Indeed, the right side vanishes for $r=D$, and differentiation under the integral sign, which is legitimate for $r$ in a compact subinterval of $(0,\infty )$, shows that its derivative in $r$ is $-r\int _0^\infty \mathrm e^{-tr^2}\, \mathrm dt=-1/r$. The integrand is nonnegative, and since $|\mathrm e^{-t r^2}-\mathrm e^{-tD^2}|\leq tD^2$, its integral over $0<t<1$ is at most $D^2$. Since $|x-y|\leq \operatorname {diam}K<D$ for $x,y\in K$, Tonelli's theorem gives

\[ 4\pi I_ D(\mu )=\int _0^\infty \bigl (Q_ t(\mu )-\mu (K)^2\mathrm e^{-tD^2}\bigr )\, \frac{\mathrm d t}{t}, \]

with a nonnegative integrand. As $\int _1^\infty \mathrm e^{-tD^2}\, \mathrm dt/t<\infty $, it follows that $\int _1^\infty Q_ t(\mu )\, \mathrm dt/t<\infty $, and likewise for $\nu $. By (4.1.6) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the measure $\mathrm dt/t$ on $(1,\infty )$, also $\int _1^\infty Q_ t(\mu ,\nu )\, \mathrm dt/t<\infty $. Since the part of the integral over $0<t<1$ contributes at most $D^2\mu (K)\nu (K)$ to $4\pi I_ D(\mu ,\nu )$, the mutual energy $I_ D(\mu ,\nu )$ is finite.

Now let $\sigma $ be a signed measure on $K$ of total mass zero whose positive and negative parts $\sigma ^\pm $ have finite energy. Then

\[ \iint k_ D\, \mathrm d|\sigma |\, \mathrm d|\sigma |=I_ D(\sigma ^+)+2I_ D(\sigma ^+,\sigma ^-)+I_ D(\sigma ^-)<\infty , \]

so Fubini's theorem applies to the integrand $(\mathrm e^{-t|x-y|^2}-\mathrm e^{-tD^2})/t$ with respect to $\mathrm dt\otimes \sigma \otimes \sigma $. Since $\sigma (K)=0$, the term $\mathrm e^{-tD^2}$ integrates to zero, and

\[ I_ D(\sigma )=\frac1{4\pi }\int _0^\infty Q_ t(\sigma )\, \frac{\mathrm d t}{t}\geq 0. \]

In particular $I_ D(\sigma )$ does not depend on $D$. As in the case $d\geq 3$, $I_ D(\sigma )=0$ forces $\sigma =0$ by lemma 4.1.3.

It remains to prove the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Let $\mathcal E$ be the real linear span of the measures on $K$ with finite energy if $d\geq 3$, and the set of signed measures on $K$ of total mass zero whose positive and negative parts have finite energy if $d=2$. In the second case $\mathcal E$ is also a vector space. Indeed, let $\sigma ,\tau \in \mathcal E$ and $c\in \mathbb R$. Since $|\sigma +\tau |\leq |\sigma |+|\tau |$, we have $(\sigma +\tau )^\pm =\frac12\bigl (|\sigma +\tau |\pm (\sigma +\tau )\bigr )\leq \sigma ^\pm +\tau ^\pm $, while $(c\sigma )^\pm =c\sigma ^\pm $ if $c\geq 0$ and $(c\sigma )^\pm =|c|\sigma ^\mp $ if $c<0$. A positive measure on $K$ dominated by the sum of two measures with finite energy has finite energy, because the kernel is nonnegative on $K\times K$ and the mutual energy of two measures with finite energy is finite. Hence $\sigma +\tau $ and $c\sigma $ belong to $\mathcal E$.

In both cases, the total variation of an element of $\mathcal E$ is dominated by a sum of measures on $K$ with finite energy, so for $\sigma ,\tau \in \mathcal E$ the kernel is integrable with respect to $|\sigma |\otimes |\tau |$. Consequently $I(\sigma ,\tau )=\iint k\, \mathrm d\sigma \, \mathrm d\tau $ (with $k_ D$ in place of $k$ when $d=2$) is a symmetric bilinear form on $\mathcal E$, and $I(\sigma )=I(\sigma ,\sigma )\geq 0$ by what we have proved. Hence, for $\sigma ,\tau \in \mathcal E$, the quadratic polynomial $s\mapsto I(\sigma +s\tau )=I(\sigma )+2sI(\sigma ,\tau )+s^2I(\tau )$ is nonnegative on $\mathbb R$. Its discriminant is therefore nonpositive, which is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $I(\sigma ,\tau )^2\leq I(\sigma )I(\tau )$. $\square$

In dimension two, the restriction to measures of total mass zero in part (2) is what makes the logarithmic energy independent of the scale. For a positive measure, the sign of $I_ D$ depends on how the scale compares with the support. Identify $\mathbb R^2$ with $\mathbb C$, and let $\sigma _ r$ be the normalized arc length measure on the circle $|x|=r$. For $|x|=r$, the rotation invariance of $\sigma _ r$ gives

\[ \int \log |x-y|\, \mathrm d\sigma _ r(y)=\log r+\frac1{2\pi }\int _{-\pi }^{\pi }\log |1-\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }|\, \mathrm d\theta , \]

and the last integral vanishes. Indeed, for $0<a<1$ the function $z\mapsto \log |1-az|$ is harmonic on a neighborhood of the closed unit disc, so its mean over the unit circle equals its value $0$ at the origin. We may let $a\uparrow 1$ by dominated convergence, since $|\theta |/\pi \leq |1-a\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\theta }|\leq 2$ for $1/2\leq a<1$ and $|\theta |\leq \pi $. Hence, for every $D>0$, the function $\int k_ D(\cdot ,y)\, \mathrm d\sigma _ r(y)$ equals $(2\pi )^{-1}\log (D/r)$ on the circle, and $\iint k_ D\, \mathrm d\sigma _ r\, \mathrm d\sigma _ r=(2\pi )^{-1}\log (D/r)$. For a fixed scale $D$, this energy is negative as soon as $r>D$. Thus no single scale makes the logarithmic energy nonnegative on all positive measures with compact support.

Theorem 4.1.7.AA43DD93EFE4 Let $K\subset \mathbb R^ d$ be a compact set that carries a probability measure of finite energy; when $d=2$, the scale $D>\operatorname {diam}K$ is fixed. Then there is a unique probability measure $\mu _ K$ on $K$ of minimal energy, the equilibrium measure of $K$. Put $V(K)=I(\mu _ K)$, and $V_ D(K)=I_ D(\mu _ K)$ when $d=2$. Every probability measure $\nu $ on $K$ with finite energy satisfies

\[ I(\mu _ K,\nu )\geq V(K) \]

(with $I_ D$ and $V_ D(K)$ when $d=2$). If $d\geq 3$, then $V(K)>0$, and the Newtonian capacity of $K$ is defined to be $1/V(K)$. If $d=2$, the logarithmic capacity of $K$ is defined to be $D\mathrm e^{-2\pi V_ D(K)}$; neither $\mu _ K$ nor this number depends on $D$. If $K$ carries no probability measure of finite energy, in particular if $K=\varnothing $, its Newtonian or logarithmic capacity is defined to be zero.

Proof. Existence. Let $\mu _ j$ be probability measures on $K$ whose energies converge to the infimum $m$, which is finite by hypothesis. By theorem 2.6.5, after passing to a subsequence, $\mu _ j$ converges vaguely to a Radon measure $\mu $ on $\mathbb R^ d$. By proposition 2.6.2, applied to the open set $\mathbb R^ d\setminus K$, we have $\mu (\mathbb R^ d\setminus K)\leq \varliminf _ j\mu _ j(\mathbb R^ d\setminus K)=0$, so $\mu $ is carried by $K$; testing against a function in $C_ c(\mathbb R^ d)$ equal to one on $K$ then shows that $\mu (K)=1$. To prove that $I(\mu )\leq m$, we first show that

\[ \iint F\, \mathrm d\mu _ j\, \mathrm d\mu _ j\longrightarrow \iint F\, \mathrm d\mu \, \mathrm d\mu \]

for every continuous function $F$ on $\mathbb R^ d\times \mathbb R^ d$.

Given $\delta >0$, cover $K$ by finitely many balls $B(x_ i,\delta )$ with $x_ i\in K$. By lemma 2.5.2, there are functions $0\leq \varphi _ i\in C_ c^\infty (B(x_ i,\delta ))$ with $\sum _ i\varphi _ i=1$ on $K$. For $x,y\in K$, a term $\varphi _ i(x)\varphi _ l(y)$ is nonzero only if $|x-x_ i|<\delta $ and $|y-x_ l|<\delta $, so

\[ \Bigl |F(x,y)-\sum _{i,l}F(x_ i,x_ l)\varphi _ i(x)\varphi _ l(y)\Bigr |\leq \omega (2\delta ), \]

where $\omega $ is the modulus of continuity of $F$ on $K\times K$. The double integrals of the finite sums converge, since $\int \varphi _ i\, \mathrm d\mu _ j\to \int \varphi _ i\, \mathrm d\mu $. As all the measures are probability measures carried by $K$ and $\omega (2\delta )\to 0$ as $\delta \downarrow 0$, this proves the asserted convergence.

For $n>0$, the truncated kernel $\min \{ k,n\} $ is continuous on $\mathbb R^ d\times \mathbb R^ d$, including the diagonal, and it increases to $k$ as $n\to \infty $. Applying the convergence just proved to $F=\min \{ k,n\} $, we get

\[ \iint \min \{ k,n\} \, \mathrm d\mu \, \mathrm d\mu =\lim _ j\iint \min \{ k,n\} \, \mathrm d\mu _ j\, \mathrm d\mu _ j\leq \varliminf _ j I(\mu _ j)=m. \]

Since $k\geq 0$ on $K\times K$, monotone convergence in $n$ gives $I(\mu )\leq m$. Hence $\mu $ is a minimizer.

Uniqueness. Let $\mu $ and $\mu '$ be minimizers. The signed measure $\sigma =\mu -\mu '$ has total mass zero. Since $|\sigma |\leq \mu +\mu '$, its positive and negative parts satisfy $\sigma ^+=\frac12(|\sigma |+\sigma )\leq \mu $ and $\sigma ^-\leq \mu '$, so they have finite energy, the kernel being nonnegative on $K\times K$. All mutual energies are finite by proposition 4.1.5, so we may expand and obtain

\[ I\bigl (\tfrac 12(\mu +\mu ')\bigr )=\tfrac 12I(\mu )+\tfrac 12I(\mu ')-\tfrac 14I(\sigma )=m-\tfrac 14I(\sigma ). \]

Since $\frac12(\mu +\mu ')$ is a probability measure on $K$, this energy is at least $m$, so $I(\sigma )\leq 0$. By proposition 4.1.5, $\sigma =0$.

The inequality. Let $\nu $ be a probability measure on $K$ with finite energy. For $0<t\leq 1$ the probability measure $(1-t)\mu _ K+t\nu $ competes in the minimization, so

\[ V(K)\leq I\bigl ((1-t)\mu _ K+t\nu \bigr )=(1-t)^2V(K)+2t(1-t)I(\mu _ K,\nu )+t^2I(\nu ), \]

all terms being finite. Subtracting $V(K)$, dividing by $t$ and letting $t\downarrow 0$ gives $0\leq -2V(K)+2I(\mu _ K,\nu )$.

The capacities. Let $d\geq 3$. A point mass has infinite energy, so $K$ is not a single point and $\operatorname {diam}K>0$. The kernel is at least $((d-2)s_{d-1})^{-1}(\operatorname {diam}K)^{2-d}>0$ on $K\times K$, hence $V(K)>0$. Let $d=2$. Replacing $D$ by $D'>\operatorname {diam}K$ changes the energy of every probability measure on $K$ by the same constant $(2\pi )^{-1}\log (D'/D)$. Hence $\mu _ K$ does not depend on the scale, $V_{D'}(K)=V_ D(K)+(2\pi )^{-1}\log (D'/D)$, and $D'\mathrm e^{-2\pi V_{D'}(K)}=D\mathrm e^{-2\pi V_ D(K)}$. $\square$

4.1.8 Continuous potentials and polar sets12EDE2E332B0

To relate energy to polarity we need measures whose fundamental potentials $E_ d*\mu $, or equivalently whose potentials $U^\mu $, are finite and continuous. We show that such measures are plentiful: every compactly supported measure whose potential is finite almost everywhere with respect to the measure itself is a countable sum of them. We begin with a version of Lusin's theorem.

Lemma 4.1.9.468BD94D4D83 Let $\mu $ be a finite Radon measure on $\mathbb R^ d$, and let $f$ be a Borel function with values in $[-\infty ,\infty ]$ that is finite $\mu $-almost everywhere. Then for every $\varepsilon >0$ there is a compact set $L$ with $\mu (\mathbb R^ d\setminus L)<\varepsilon $ such that $f$ is finite on $L$ and the restriction $f|_ L$ is continuous.

Proof. The sets $\{ |f|>N\} $ decrease to the $\mu $-null set $\{ |f|=\infty \} $ as $N\to \infty $, and $\mu $ is finite, so we can choose $N$ with $\mu (\{ |f|>N\} )<\varepsilon /3$. The truncation $f_ N=\max \{ -N,\min \{ f,N\} \} $ is bounded, hence belongs to $L^1(\mu )$. By proposition 2.3.5 there are continuous functions $g_ j$, $j\geq 1$, with $\int |g_ j-f_ N|\, \mathrm d\mu <\varepsilon 2^{-2j}/3$. By Chebyshev's inequality, the set $E_ j=\{ |g_ j-f_ N|>2^{-j}\} $ satisfies $\mu (E_ j)<\varepsilon 2^{-j}/3$, so $\mu (\bigcup _ jE_ j)<\varepsilon /3$. On the Borel set $F=\mathbb R^ d\setminus (\{ |f|>N\} \cup \bigcup _ jE_ j)$ we have $f=f_ N$ and $|g_ j-f|\leq 2^{-j}$ for every $j$, so $g_ j\to f$ uniformly on $F$. Since $\mu (\mathbb R^ d\setminus F)<2\varepsilon /3$, inner regularity gives a compact set $L\subset F$ with $\mu (F\setminus L)<\varepsilon /3$. Then $\mu (\mathbb R^ d\setminus L)<\varepsilon $, and $f|_ L$ is finite and continuous as a uniform limit of continuous functions. $\square$

Theorem 4.1.10.872A6887585B Let $K\subset \mathbb R^ d$ be compact, let $\mu $ be a finite positive measure on $\mathbb R^ d$ with $\operatorname{Supp}\mu \subset K$, and put $p=E_ d*\mu $.

  1. If $x\in K$ and the restriction $p|_ K$ is continuous at $x$, then $p$ is continuous at $x$ as a function on $\mathbb R^ d$ with values in $[-\infty ,\infty )$.

  2. If $p$ is finite $\mu $-almost everywhere, then $\mu =\sum _ j\mu _ j$, where the $\mu _ j$ are the restrictions of $\mu $ to pairwise disjoint compact sets and every $E_ d*\mu _ j$ is finite and continuous on $\mathbb R^ d$.

Finally, $u_ K$ satisfies the hypotheses by proposition 4.1.16, and since $u_ K\geq -1$ and $\lambda _ K$ has total mass $C_ B(K)$, we get $\| \nabla u_ K\| _2^2=-\int u_ K\, \mathrm d\lambda _ K\leq C_ B(K)$. $\square$

Theorem 4.1.30.6B0925DDDD3A Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb R^ d$ be an open set with $\overline B\subset \Omega $, and let $u\in \mathrm{SH}(\Omega )$ be not identically $-\infty $ on $B$. Then $u$ is quasi-continuous: for every compact set $L\subset B$ and every $\eta >0$ there is an open set $O\Subset B$ with $C_ B(O)<\eta $ such that the restriction of $u$ to $L\setminus O$ is finite and continuous.

Proof. The case of a bounded Green potential. Suppose first that $u$ is a bounded subharmonic function on $B$ that tends to zero at $\partial B$ and whose Riesz measure $\mu $ has compact support in $B$. Let $Q\subset B$ and $u_\varepsilon $ be the compact set and the regularizations given by the last assertion of proposition 4.1.29. These regularizations are continuous on $\overline B$, decrease to $u$, equal $u$ on $B\setminus Q$, and $\nabla u_\varepsilon \to \nabla u$ in $L^2(B)$. For $\delta >0$, the set $O_{\delta ,\varepsilon }=\{ u_\varepsilon -u>\delta \} $ is open, because $u_\varepsilon -u$ is lower semicontinuous, and it is contained in $Q$, so it has compact closure in $B$. Let $K\subset O_{\delta ,\varepsilon }$ be compact. The function $u_\varepsilon -u$ is a difference of two functions satisfying the hypotheses of proposition 4.1.29, and $\lambda _ K$ is supported on $K$, where $u_\varepsilon -u>\delta $. By the bilinear identity of that proposition, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the bound $\| \nabla u_ K\| _2^2\leq C_ B(K)$,

\[ \delta C_ B(K)\leq \int (u_\varepsilon -u)\, \mathrm d\lambda _ K =-\int \nabla (u_\varepsilon -u)\mathbin {\cdot }\nabla u_ K\, \mathrm d x \leq \| \nabla (u_\varepsilon -u)\| _2\, C_ B(K)^{1/2}. \]

Hence $C_ B(K)\leq \delta ^{-2}\| \nabla (u_\varepsilon -u)\| _2^2$, and taking the supremum over $K$ gives $C_ B(O_{\delta ,\varepsilon })\leq \delta ^{-2}\| \nabla (u_\varepsilon -u)\| _2^2$.

Choose $\delta _ j\downarrow 0$ and then radii $\varepsilon _ j\downarrow 0$ such that $C_ B(O_{\delta _ j,\varepsilon _ j})<\eta 2^{-j}$, and let $O=\bigcup _ jO_{\delta _ j,\varepsilon _ j}$, which is open with compact closure in $B$. By countable subadditivity (theorem 4.1.19), $C_ B(O)<\eta $. Outside $O$ we have $0\leq u_{\varepsilon _ j}-u\leq \delta _ j$ for all $j$, so the continuous functions $u_{\varepsilon _ j}$ converge uniformly to $u$ on $B\setminus O$. Hence $u$ is finite and continuous on $B\setminus O$, in particular on $L\setminus O$.

The case of a function bounded near $\overline B$. Suppose next that $u$ is bounded on a neighborhood of $\overline B$; subtracting a constant, we may assume that $u\leq -1$ there. Choose $r<R$ with $L\subset B(0,r)$, and $M>0$ with $-M<\inf _ Bu$. Let $q=Mu_{\overline{B(0,r)}}$, where the extremal function $u_{\overline{B(0,r)}}$ was computed in example 4.1.21. Then $q$ is subharmonic on $B$, equals $-M$ on $\overline{B(0,r)}$, is harmonic on $r<|x|<R$, and tends to zero at $\partial B$. The function $w=\max \{ u,q\} $ is subharmonic and bounded on $B$; it equals $u$ on $B(0,r)$, and it equals $q$ near $\partial B$, where $q>-1\geq u$. Hence $w$ tends to zero at $\partial B$, and its Riesz measure has compact support in $B$. The first case, applied to $w$, gives the required open set, since $w=u$ near $L$.

The general case. The function $u$ is bounded above near $\overline B$, so the functions $u_ N=\max \{ u,-N\} $, $N\geq 1$, are subharmonic and bounded near $\overline B$. Fix an open set $W$ with $L\subset W\Subset B$. By the second case, there are open sets $O_ N\subset W$ with $C_ B(O_ N)<\eta 2^{-N-1}$ such that $u_ N$ is continuous on $L\setminus O_ N$ (intersecting with $W$ does not affect this). On $L\setminus \bigcup _ NO_ N$, every $u_ N$ is continuous, and $\mathrm e^{u_ N}$ converges uniformly to $\mathrm e^ u$, because $0\leq \mathrm e^{u_ N}-\mathrm e^ u\leq \mathrm e^{-N}$. Hence $u$ is continuous there as a function with values in $[-\infty ,\infty )$. Let $\Omega _0$ be the connected component of $\Omega $ that contains the connected set $\overline B$. Since $u$ is not identically $-\infty $ on $\Omega _0$, the set $\{ u=-\infty \} \cap \Omega _0$ is polar by the definition of polarity. By corollary 4.1.24, $L\cap \{ u=-\infty \} $ is contained in an open set $O'\Subset B$ with $C_ B(O')<\eta /2$. The open set $O=O'\cup \bigcup _ NO_ N$ has compact closure in $B$ and capacity less than $\eta $ by countable subadditivity, and the restriction of $u$ to $L\setminus O$ is finite and continuous. $\square$
