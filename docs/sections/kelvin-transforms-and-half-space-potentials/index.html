3.2 Kelvin transforms and half-space potentials9728BD930155

3.2.1 Inversion and the Kelvin transform9A02E6F8D636

Fix an integer $d\geq 2$. We use $\Delta =\sum _{j=1}^ d\partial _ j^2$ and write $s_{d-1}$ for the area of the unit sphere in $\mathbb R^ d$. For $a\in \mathbb R^ d$, $\rho >0$ and $x\neq a$, put

\[ T(x)=a+\frac{\rho ^2(x-a)}{|x-a|^2},\qquad (Ku)(x)=\left(\frac{\rho }{|x-a|}\right)^{d-2}u(T(x)). \]

The factor is $1$ when $d=2$.

Theorem 3.2.2 (Kelvin transform).97BA27062B9F Let $U\subset \mathbb R^ d\setminus \{ a\} $ be open and let $u\in C^2(U)$. Then $Ku\in C^2(T(U))$ and

0E9BCA99B831 3.2.3
\begin{equation} \Delta (Ku)(x)=\left(\frac{\rho }{|x-a|}\right)^{d+2} (\Delta u)(T(x)). \end{equation}

Moreover, $T^2$ and $K^2$ are the identity. Thus the Kelvin transform preserves harmonicity and nonnegativity in both directions.

Proof. Translate $a$ to $0$, and set $r=|x|$, $e=x/r$, $Q=I-2ee^{\mathsf T}$, $y=T(x)=\rho ^2x/r^2$ and $w(x)=\rho ^{d-2}r^{2-d}$. The matrix $Q$ is symmetric and orthogonal, and $Qx=-x$. Differentiating $y_ j=\rho ^2x_ jr^{-2}$ gives

\[ Dy=\rho ^2r^{-2}Q,\qquad \Delta y_ j=-2(d-2)\rho ^2x_ jr^{-4}. \]

For completeness, $\Delta r^\alpha =\alpha (\alpha +d-2)r^{\alpha -2}$ follows by differentiating $\partial _ jr^\alpha =\alpha x_ jr^{\alpha -2}$ and summing. Applying the product rule to $x_ jr^{-2}$ proves the second identity above. The same radial formula yields

\[ \nabla w=(2-d)\rho ^{d-2}r^{-d}x,\qquad \Delta w=0. \]

The chain rule and the orthogonality of $Q$ now give

\begin{align*} \Delta (u\circ T) & =\rho ^4r^{-4}(\Delta u)(y) -2(d-2)\rho ^2r^{-4}x\cdot \nabla u(y),\\ 2\nabla w\cdot \nabla (u\circ T) & =2(d-2)\rho ^ d r^{-d-2}x\cdot \nabla u(y). \end{align*}

In $\Delta (w(u\circ T))$, the terms containing $x\cdot \nabla u(y)$ therefore cancel, leaving $\rho ^{d+2}r^{-d-2}(\Delta u)(y)$. This proves (3.2.3), including $d=2$.

Since $|T(x)|=\rho ^2/|x|$, substitution gives $T(T(x))=x$ and $w(x)w(T(x))=1$. In particular $T$ is a smooth diffeomorphism of $\mathbb R^ d\setminus \{ 0\} $ onto itself, so $T(U)$ is open and $K^2u=u$. The positive weight $w$ preserves signs. The Laplacian identity and involutivity prove the assertion about harmonicity. $\square$

No assertion is made at the inversion center, which is outside both domains.

3.2.4 Nonnegative harmonic functions on a half-space5C12992087C2

Put $H=\{ x=(x',t)\in \mathbb R^{d-1}\times \mathbb R:t>0\} $ and identify $\partial H$ with $\mathbb R^{d-1}$. For $z\in \mathbb R^{d-1}$ set

21F1298C637C 3.2.5
\begin{equation} P_ H(x,z)=\frac{2}{s_{d-1}} \frac{t}{(|x'-z|^2+t^2)^{d/2}}. \end{equation}

Theorem 3.2.6 (Half-space representation).6BC86EEF03A9 Every nonnegative harmonic function $h$ on $H$ has a representation

F3ED29BBAD39 3.2.7
\begin{equation} h(x',t)=ct+\int _{\mathbb R^{d-1}}P_ H((x',t),z)\, d\nu (z), \end{equation}

where $c\geq 0$ and $\nu $ is a positive Radon measure satisfying

8F7C774A9D89 3.2.8
\begin{equation} \int _{\mathbb R^{d-1}}(1+|z|^2)^{-d/2}\, d\nu (z)<\infty . \end{equation}

Conversely, any such pair defines a nonnegative harmonic function on $H$. The measure need not have finite total mass.

Proof. We first derive the ball representation needed here from the ball Poisson formula and compactness of bounded positive measures on a compact set. Let $v\geq 0$ be harmonic on $B=B(b,R)$ and let $0<r<R$. On $\partial B$ define the measure

\[ d\mu _ r(\zeta )=v\bigl (b+(r/R)(\zeta -b)\bigr )\, dS(\zeta ). \]

The mean-value property gives $\mu _ r(\partial B)=s_{d-1}R^{d-1}v(b)$. For a sequence $r_ j\uparrow R$, compactness therefore gives a subsequence converging weakly to a finite positive measure $\mu $ on $\partial B$. To apply the Euclidean measure-selection theorem, extend $\mu _{r_ j}$ by zero to $\mathbb R^ d$; their common compact support gives uniform tightness, and their weak limit is supported on that same closed sphere. For $|x-b|<r$, the ball Poisson formula, followed by the dilation from $\partial B$ to $\partial B(b,r)$, reads

\[ v(x)=\int _{\partial B}\left(\frac rR\right)^{d-1} \frac{r^2-|x-b|^2}{s_{d-1}r\, |x-b-(r/R)(\zeta -b)|^ d}\, d\mu _ r(\zeta ). \]

For fixed $x\in B$ the integrands converge uniformly on $\partial B$ as $r\uparrow R$. The masses are uniformly bounded, so weak convergence gives

2009D781FC4A 3.2.9
\begin{equation} v(x)=\int _{\partial B}\frac{R^2-|x-b|^2}{s_{d-1}R|x-\zeta |^ d}\, d\mu (\zeta ). \end{equation}

This argument also covers $v(b)=0$, in which case all measures and $v$ vanish.

Take $a=-e_ d$, $b=-e_ d/2$, $R=1/2$, and $T(x)=a+(x-a)/|x-a|^2$. Write $q=T(x)$ and $L=|x-a|$. Direct expansion gives

B95428ABB7D8 3.2.10
\begin{equation} |q-a|=L^{-1},\qquad \frac14-|q-b|^2=\frac{t}{L^2},\qquad |T(x)-T(y)|=\frac{|x-y|}{|x-a||y-a|}. \end{equation}

For the last identity, square both sides and expand the inner products; the numerator on the left becomes $|x-a|^2+|y-a|^2-2(x-a)\cdot (y-a)=|x-y|^2$ after multiplying by $|x-a|^2|y-a|^2$. The middle identity and $T^2=1$ show that $T$ maps $H$ onto $B(b,1/2)$ and maps $\partial H$ onto $\partial B\setminus \{ a\} $.

By Theorem 3.2.2, $v(q)=|q-a|^{2-d}h(T(q))$ is nonnegative and harmonic on $B$. Apply (3.2.9) and split $\mu =m\delta _ a+\mu _0$, where $\mu _0(\{ a\} )=0$. Since $h(x)=L^{2-d}v(T(x))$, the atom contributes $2mt/s_{d-1}$. Let $\sigma =T_*\mu _0$ on $\partial H$, and put

\[ c=2m/s_{d-1},\qquad d\nu (z)=|(z,0)-a|^ d\, d\sigma (z). \]

The measure $\sigma $ is finite and positive, and its continuous positive weight is bounded on compact sets. Hence $\nu $ is a positive Radon measure. The distance identity in (3.2.10) transforms the remaining integral into

\[ L^{2-d}\frac{2}{s_{d-1}} \int _{\partial B\setminus \{ a\} } \frac{t/L^2}{|T(x)-\zeta |^ d}\, d\mu _0(\zeta ) =\frac{2t}{s_{d-1}}\int _{\mathbb R^{d-1}} \frac{d\nu (z)}{|x-(z,0)|^ d}. \]

Also $|(z,0)-a|^ d=(1+|z|^2)^{d/2}$, so the integral in (3.2.8) equals $\sigma (\mathbb R^{d-1})<\infty $. This proves the required representation.

For the converse, each kernel is smooth and harmonic as a function of $x\in H$. Indeed, with $r=|x-(z,0)|$, the radial differentiation formula used above gives $\Delta r^{-d}=2d r^{-d-2}$ and $\partial _ t r^{-d}=-dt r^{-d-2}$. Consequently $\Delta (t r^{-d})=2dt r^{-d-2}-2dt r^{-d-2}=0$. On a compact subset $K\subset H$, $t$ is bounded above and away from $0$, and $|x'|$ is bounded. Writing $v=x-(z,0)$, differentiation gives

\begin{align*} \partial _ i(t r^{-d})& =\delta _{id}r^{-d}-dtv_ i r^{-d-2},\\ \partial _ j\partial _ i(t r^{-d}) & =-d(\delta _{id}v_ j+\delta _{jd}v_ i+t\delta _{ij})r^{-d-2} +d(d+2)tv_ iv_ j r^{-d-4}. \end{align*}

For $|z|$ large, $r\geq |z|/2$ and $|v_ i|\leq r$, so these formulas bound the kernel and all its $x$ derivatives of orders at most two by $C_ K(1+|z|^2)^{-d/2}$. For bounded $z$, the lower bound on $t$ supplies the same estimate after increasing $C_ K$. These estimates also hold on a compact neighborhood of $K$ inside $H$, so dominated convergence justifies two differentiations under the integral. Condition (3.2.8) gives finiteness and harmonicity; positivity is immediate. $\square$

The weighted integrability condition and the converse above spell out the precise admissible class of measures.

3.2.11 Bounded boundary dataFA92650B78EB

Theorem 3.2.12 (Half-space Poisson integral).9ADD2DF19642 Let $f:\mathbb R^{d-1}\to \mathbb R$ be bounded and Lebesgue measurable, and let $c\in \mathbb R$. Then

\[ u(x',t)=ct+\int _{\mathbb R^{d-1}}P_ H((x',t),z)f(z)\, dz \]

is harmonic on $H$, and $|u(x',t)-ct|\leq \| f\| _\infty $. If $f$ is continuous at $z_0$, then $u(x',t)\to f(z_0)$ as $(x',t)\to (z_0,0)$ within $H$, with no restriction on the approach. Moreover $u(0,t)/t\to c$ as $t\to \infty $.

Proof. We first verify the normalization

AB2849CD8791 3.2.13
\begin{equation} \int _{\mathbb R^{d-1}}P_ H((x',t),z)\, dz=1. \end{equation}

Translation and the substitution $z=x'+tw$ reduce the integral to $\frac{2}{s_{d-1}}\int _{\mathbb R^{d-1}}(1+|w|^2)^{-d/2}\, dw$. The map

\[ F(w)=\frac{(w,1)}{\sqrt{1+|w|^2}} \]

parametrizes the upper hemisphere. With $m=d-1$, its metric matrix is $DF^{\mathsf T}DF=(1+|w|^2)^{-1}I-(1+|w|^2)^{-2}ww^{\mathsf T}$. Its eigenvalues are $(1+|w|^2)^{-1}$ in the $m-1$ perpendicular directions and $(1+|w|^2)^{-2}$ in the direction of $w$. The square root of its determinant is $(1+|w|^2)^{-d/2}$. At $w=0$ this formula holds by continuity. The surface area formula and reflection in the equatorial plane show that the last integral is $s_{d-1}/2$, proving (3.2.13). This also treats $d=2$, when $m=1$ and there are no perpendicular directions.

The weighted measure $|f(z)|\, dz$ satisfies (3.2.8), by the same integral at $(0,1)$. Applying the differentiation estimates in the proof of Theorem 3.2.6 to the positive and negative parts of $f$ proves harmonicity. The positivity and normalization of $P_ H$ give the stated bound.

Fix $\varepsilon >0$. At a continuity point $z_0$, choose $\delta >0$ so that $|f(z)-f(z_0)|<\varepsilon $ for $|z-z_0|<\delta $. The contribution of this set to $|\int P_ H(x,z)(f(z)-f(z_0))\, dz|$ is at most $\varepsilon $. If $|x'-z_0|<\delta /2$ and $|z-z_0|\geq \delta $, then $|z-x'|\geq \delta /2$. Writing $s_{d-2}$ for the area of the unit sphere in $\mathbb R^{d-1}$ (in particular $s_0=2$), polar integration gives

\[ \int _{|z-z_0|\geq \delta }P_ H(x,z)\, dz \leq \frac{2t}{s_{d-1}}s_{d-2}\int _{\delta /2}^{\infty }r^{-2}\, dr =\frac{4s_{d-2}}{s_{d-1}}\frac{t}{\delta }. \]

Thus

\[ |u(x',t)-f(z_0)|\leq |c|t+\varepsilon + \frac{8s_{d-2}}{s_{d-1}}\| f\| _\infty \frac{t}{\delta } \]

near $(z_0,0)$. Letting $(x',t)\to (z_0,0)$ and then $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ proves the boundary limit. Finally $|u(0,t)/t-c|\leq \| f\| _\infty /t\to 0$. $\square$

For continuous bounded boundary data, varying $c$ gives distinct harmonic solutions with the same finite boundary values. This states nonuniqueness without a growth condition; it does not classify all such solutions.
