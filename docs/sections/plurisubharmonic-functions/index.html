5.2 Plurisubharmonic functions

8 Plurisubharmonic functions

Plurisubharmonic functions are the basic objects of pluripotential theory. On a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$, a plurisubharmonic function is an upper semicontinuous function whose restriction to every complex line is subharmonic. By (7.5), the Laplacian of a function on $\Omega $, viewed as an open subset of $\mathbb R^{2n}$, is four times the trace of its complex Hessian, so subharmonicity controls only this trace. Subharmonicity on all complex lines is the pointwise counterpart of the positivity of the whole complex Hessian, $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$.

Since $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$ determines $u$ only almost everywhere, the pointwise definition and the distributional condition must be kept apart. We first show that the upper semicontinuity required in the definition follows from the condition on complex lines (theorem 8.1.3); the proof combines the incidence estimate of chapter 6 with the distributional theory of chapter 3. We then identify the plurisubharmonic functions with the canonical representatives, in the sense of theorem 3.2.2, of the distributions with positive complex Hessian (theorem 8.2.1). This makes the potential theory of Part I available. A smooth function is plurisubharmonic exactly when its complex Hessian is positive semidefinite, and mollification approximates every plurisubharmonic function from above by smooth ones. Plurisubharmonicity is invariant under holomorphic maps, hence makes sense on complex manifolds, and it is preserved by decreasing limits, regularized upper envelopes and $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$ limits. Moreover, plurisubharmonic functions belong to $L^ p_{\mathrm{loc}}$ for every finite $p$, and near each point $\mathrm e^{-\alpha u}$ is integrable for small $\alpha >0$.

In the last two sections we extend plurisubharmonic functions across closed sets that are locally contained in zero sets of holomorphic functions or are small in the sense of Hausdorff measure, and we prove Kiselman's minimum principle: although an infimum of plurisubharmonic functions is in general not plurisubharmonic, a partial infimum over real variables is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $ under convexity assumptions, provided that the function does not depend on the corresponding imaginary parts.

8.1 The line definition and upper semicontinuity

We identify $\mathbb {C}^ n$ with $\mathbb R^{2n}$ through $z_ j=x_ j+\mathrm{i}y_ j$, and we call a function on an open subset of $\mathbb {C}^ n$ subharmonic if it is subharmonic in the sense of definition 3.1.1 on the corresponding open subset of $\mathbb R^{2n}$. Recall from section 6.4 that an affine complex line is a set $L=\{ a+\zeta b:\zeta \in \mathbb {C}\} $ with $a\in \mathbb {C}^ n$ and $b\in \mathbb {C}^ n\setminus \{ 0\} $. For an open set $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$, the section $L\cap \Omega $ is identified with the open set $\{ \zeta \in \mathbb {C}:a+\zeta b\in \Omega \} $, and a function on a connected component of $L\cap \Omega $ is called subharmonic if its composite with $\zeta \mapsto a+\zeta b$ is subharmonic; this does not depend on the parametrization of $L$. Accordingly, the restriction of a function $u$ on $\Omega $ to $L\cap \Omega $ is the function $\zeta \mapsto u(a+\zeta b)$.

Definition 8.1.1. Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$ be a domain. If $n\ge 1$, a function $u\colon \Omega \to [-\infty ,\infty )$ is plurisubharmonic if $u\not\equiv -\infty $, $u$ is upper semicontinuous, and for every affine complex line $L$ the restriction of $u$ to each connected component of $L\cap \Omega $ is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $. The set of plurisubharmonic functions on $\Omega $ is denoted by $\mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$. In dimension zero a domain is a single point, and the plurisubharmonic functions on it are the real-valued functions.

We often abbreviate plurisubharmonic to psh. Since the constant $-\infty $ is subharmonic by definition 3.1.1, the alternative “identically $-\infty $” in the line condition is stated only for emphasis; it occurs, for instance, for $\log |z_1|^2$ on the line $\{ z_1=0\} $. In dimension one the only affine complex line is $\mathbb {C}$ itself, so $\mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$ is the set of nontrivial subharmonic functions on $\Omega $, that is, of those not identically $-\infty $. Whenever a limit of plurisubharmonic functions may degenerate to the function identically $-\infty $, we state this alternative explicitly.

We keep upper semicontinuity in definition 8.1.1, but it is redundant: theorem 8.1.3 below shows that it follows from the other two conditions. The proof of that theorem uses the following slicing lemma, which we apply again in theorem 8.2.1, theorem 8.4.3: for a locally integrable function, subharmonicity on the complex lines parallel to a fixed direction makes the second derivative in that direction a positive distribution.

Lemma 8.1.2. Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$ be open, $n\ge 1$, let $u\colon \Omega \to [-\infty ,\infty )$ be a locally integrable function, let $a\in \mathbb {C}^ n$ be a unit vector, and let $\chi \in C^\infty _ c(\Omega )$ be nonnegative. Write the points of $\mathbb {C}^ n$ as $v+ta$ with $v\in (\mathbb {C}a)^\perp $ and $t\in \mathbb {C}$, and put $K_ v=\{ t\in \mathbb {C}:v+ta\in \operatorname{Supp}\chi \} $. Assume that for almost every $v\in (\mathbb {C}a)^\perp $ there is an open set $G_ v\subset \{ t\in \mathbb {C}:v+ta\in \Omega \} $ containing $K_ v$ such that the restriction of $t\mapsto u(v+ta)$ to each connected component of $G_ v$ is subharmonic, possibly identically $-\infty $. Then

\[ \Bigl \langle \sum _{j,k}a_ j\bar a_ ku_{j\bar k},\chi \Bigr \rangle \geq 0. \]

Consequently, if this hypothesis holds for every unit vector $a$ and every nonnegative $\chi \in C^\infty _ c(\Omega )$, then $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$. This is the case, in particular, if the restriction of $u$ to each connected component of each affine complex-line section of $\Omega $ is subharmonic.

Proof. The map $(v,t)\mapsto v+ta$ is a unitary isomorphism of $(\mathbb {C}a)^\perp \times \mathbb {C}$ onto $\mathbb {C}^ n$, so it preserves Lebesgue measure, and by the chain rule $\partial _ t\partial _{\bar t}[\chi (v+ta)]=\sum _{j,k}a_ j\bar a_ k\chi _{j\bar k}(v+ta)$. We extend $u$ and $\chi $ by zero outside $\Omega $. Since $u$ is locally integrable and the derivatives of $\chi $ are bounded and supported in $\operatorname{Supp}\chi $, Fubini's theorem gives

\[ \Bigl \langle \sum _{j,k}a_ j\bar a_ ku_{j\bar k},\chi \Bigr \rangle =\int _{(\mathbb {C}a)^\perp }I(v)\, \mathrm{d}V_{2n-2}(v), \]

where

\[ I(v)=\int _{\mathbb {C}}u(v+ta)\, \partial _ t\partial _{\bar t}[\chi (v+ta)]\, \mathrm{d}V_2(t). \]

Applied to $|u|\mathbf1_{\operatorname{Supp}\chi }$, Fubini's theorem also shows that for almost every $v$ the function $t\mapsto u(v+ta)$ is integrable on $K_ v$. It therefore suffices to show that $I(v)\geq 0$ for every $v$ for which this integrability holds and an open set $G_ v$ as in the hypothesis exists.

Fix such a $v$. The compact set $K_ v$ is covered by the disjoint open connected components of $G_ v$, so it meets only finitely many of them, say $U_1,\ldots ,U_ N$, and each set $K_ v\cap U_ i$ is compact, being the complement in $K_ v$ of the open set $K_ v\cap \bigcup _{l\neq i}U_ l$. Hence the function $\chi _ i(t)=\chi (v+ta)$, $t\in U_ i$, is a nonnegative test function on $U_ i$, and

\[ I(v)=\sum _{i=1}^ N\int _{U_ i}u(v+ta)\, \partial _ t\partial _{\bar t}\chi _ i(t)\, \mathrm{d}V_2(t). \]

If $\chi _ i\equiv 0$, the $i$-th term vanishes. Otherwise $\chi _ i>0$ on a nonempty open subset of $U_ i$, contained in $K_ v$, on which $t\mapsto u(v+ta)$ is integrable and hence finite almost everywhere. Thus the subharmonic function $w_ i(t)=u(v+ta)$ on the domain $U_ i$ is not identically $-\infty $, and it is locally integrable by proposition 3.1.5. Since $\partial _ t\partial _{\bar t}=\frac14\Delta $, the $i$-th term equals $\frac14\langle \Delta w_ i,\chi _ i\rangle $, which is nonnegative by theorem 3.2.2(1). Hence $I(v)\geq 0$.

If the hypothesis holds for every unit vector $a$ and every $\chi $, then $\sum _{j,k}a_ j\bar a_ ku_{j\bar k}$ is a positive distribution for every unit vector $a$, and by homogeneity for every $a\in \mathbb {C}^ n$. Since $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ is a real $(1,1)$-current, the matrix criterion for positivity in section 7.3 shows that $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$. For the last assertion, take $G_ v=\{ t\in \mathbb {C}:v+ta\in \Omega \} $ for every $v$; its connected components are the connected components of the affine complex-line sections of $\Omega $ in the direction $a$. $\square$

The proof of the next theorem also uses polar coordinates adapted to the complex lines through a point. Let $n\geq 1$, let $\tau $ be the normalized surface measure on the unit sphere $S^{2n-1}\subset \mathbb {C}^ n$, and let $s_{2n-1}$ be the area of this sphere. Let $x\in \mathbb {C}^ n$ and $r>0$. For every Borel function $F\colon B(x,r)\to [0,\infty ]$, integration in polar coordinates (2.1), in which $\mathrm dS=s_{2n-1}\, \mathrm d\tau $, gives the first equality in

8.1
\begin{equation} \begin{split} \int _{B(x,r)}F\, \mathrm{d}V_{2n} & =s_{2n-1}\int _{S^{2n-1}}\int _0^ rF(x+\rho a)\rho ^{2n-1}\, \mathrm{d}\rho \, \mathrm{d}\tau (a)\\ & =\frac{s_{2n-1}}{2\pi }\int _{S^{2n-1}}\int _{|t|<r} F(x+ta)|t|^{2n-2}\, \mathrm{d}V_2(t)\, \mathrm{d}\tau (a). \end{split} \end{equation}

For the second, we replace $a$ by $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta }a$, which does not change $\tau $, average over $\theta \in [0,2\pi ]$, and use polar coordinates $t=\rho \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta }$ in $\mathbb {C}$; Tonelli's theorem justifies the exchanges of integrals.

Theorem 8.1.3. Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$ be a domain, $n\ge 1$, and let $u\colon \Omega \to [-\infty ,\infty )$ be a function that is not identically $-\infty $. Assume that for every affine complex line $L$ the restriction of $u$ to each connected component of $L\cap \Omega $ is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $. Then $u$ is upper semicontinuous, and hence $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$.

Proof. We first treat finite nonnegative functions, in Steps 1–3, and then reduce the general case to this one by truncation in Step 4. For the first part we prove by induction on $n\geq 1$ the following assertion: if $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$ is a domain and $u\colon \Omega \to [0,\infty )$ is a finite function whose restriction to each connected component of each affine complex-line section of $\Omega $ is subharmonic, then $u$ is upper semicontinuous. For $n=1$ the only affine complex line is $\mathbb {C}$, so $u$ is subharmonic on $\Omega $ and in particular upper semicontinuous.

Let $n\geq 2$, assume the assertion in dimension $n-1$, and let $u$ be as in the assertion. Write $z=(z',w)$ with $z'\in \mathbb {C}^{n-1}$ and $w\in \mathbb {C}$. For fixed $w$, the restriction of $u(\, \cdot \, ,w)$ to each connected component of the open set $\{ z':(z',w)\in \Omega \} $ satisfies the hypothesis in dimension $n-1$. Indeed, affine complex lines in $\mathbb {C}^{n-1}\times \{ w\} $ are affine complex lines in $\mathbb {C}^ n$, a connected component of a line section of the slice lies in a connected component of the corresponding line section of $\Omega $, and subharmonicity is inherited by open subsets. By the induction hypothesis, $u(\, \cdot \, ,w)$ is therefore upper semicontinuous on this open set. For fixed $z'$, the function $u(z',\, \cdot \, )$ is subharmonic on each connected component of $\{ w:(z',w)\in \Omega \} $.

Step 1: $u$ is Borel measurable. We need joint measurability of $u$ before we can integrate it. Fix a ball $B'\subset \mathbb {C}^{n-1}$, a point $w_0\in \mathbb {C}$ and $\rho >0$ with $B'\times B(w_0,2\rho )\subset \Omega $. For integers $N\ge 1$ put $u_ N=\min \{ u,N\} $, and for $0<r<\rho $ and $(z',w)\in B'\times B(w_0,\rho )$ define

\[ F_ r^ N(z',w)=\frac{1}{\pi r^2}\int _{B(w,r)}u_ N(z',\eta )\, \mathrm{d}V_2(\eta ). \]

The integral exists, since $u(z',\, \cdot \, )$ is subharmonic, hence upper semicontinuous, on $B(w_0,2\rho )\supset B(w,r)$, so the integrand is a Borel function with values in $[0,N]$. For fixed $w$, the function $F_ r^ N(\, \cdot \, ,w)$ is upper semicontinuous: if $z'_ l\to z'$ in $B'$, the reverse Fatou lemma, applicable because $0\le u_ N\le N$, gives

\[ \varlimsup _{l\to \infty }F_ r^ N(z'_ l,w) \le \frac{1}{\pi r^2}\int _{B(w,r)}\varlimsup _{l\to \infty }u_ N(z'_ l,\eta )\, \mathrm{d}V_2(\eta ) \le F_ r^ N(z',w), \]

where the last inequality holds because each $u_ N(\, \cdot \, ,\eta )$ is upper semicontinuous on $B'$. For fixed $z'$, the function $F_ r^ N(z',\, \cdot \, )$ is continuous: for $|w-v|\leq r$ the symmetric difference $B(w,r)\mathbin \triangle B(v,r)$ lies in the annulus $\{ r-|w-v|\leq |\eta -w|<r+|w-v|\} $, whose area is $4\pi r|w-v|$, so that $|F_ r^ N(z',w)-F_ r^ N(z',v)|\le 4N|w-v|/r$.

A function on $B'\times B(w_0,\rho )$ that is Borel in $z'$ and continuous in $w$ is Borel; we check this for $F_ r^ N$. For $k\geq 1$ partition $B(w_0,\rho )$ into countably many Borel sets $E_{k,i}$ of diameter less than $1/k$, choose a point $w_{k,i}$ in each nonempty $E_{k,i}$, and put $F_ k(z',w)=F_ r^ N(z',w_{k,i})$ for $w\in E_{k,i}$. Each $F_ k$ is Borel, because each $F_ r^ N(\, \cdot \, ,w_{k,i})$ is upper semicontinuous, hence Borel, and $F_ k(z',w)\to F_ r^ N(z',w)$ for every $(z',w)$, because $F_ r^ N(z',\, \cdot \, )$ is continuous and the chosen points $w_{k,i}$ with $w\in E_{k,i}$ tend to $w$. Thus $F_ r^ N$ is Borel, and so is $G_ N=\varlimsup _{k\to \infty }F^ N_{\rho /(k+1)}$.

Now fix $(z',w)\in B'\times B(w_0,\rho )$. The upper semicontinuous function $u(z',\, \cdot \, )$ is bounded above on the closed disc $\overline{B(w,\rho /2)}$ by some $M$. For $N\geq M$ the truncation does not change $u(z',\, \cdot \, )$ on this disc, so $F^ N_{\rho /(k+1)}(z',w)$ is the mean of $u(z',\, \cdot \, )$ over $B(w,\rho /(k+1))$. The function $u(z',\, \cdot \, )$ is subharmonic and finite on $B(w_0,2\rho )$, hence locally integrable there by proposition 3.1.5, and by (3.3) its means over the discs $B(w,\rho /(k+1))$ converge to $u(z',w)$ as $k\to \infty $. Hence $G_ N(z',w)=u(z',w)$ for all $N\geq M$, and $u=\lim _ NG_ N$ is Borel on $B'\times B(w_0,\rho )$. Countably many such product sets cover $\Omega $, so $u$ is Borel on $\Omega $.

Step 2: the canonical representative. By corollary 6.5.4, the finite Borel function $u$ is locally bounded above. Since $u\geq 0$, it follows that $u\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega )$. Since the restriction of $u$ to each connected component of each affine complex-line section of $\Omega $ is subharmonic, lemma 8.1.2 gives $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$. In particular $\Delta u=4\sum _ ju_{j\bar j}\geq 0$, so by theorem 3.2.2 $u$ has a canonical subharmonic representative $\widetilde u$, a locally integrable subharmonic function on $\Omega $ equal to $u$ almost everywhere. Its complex Hessian is the same positive current $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$. Applying proposition 7.3.3 to the parametrizations of affine complex lines, we see that the restriction of $\widetilde u$ to each connected component of each affine complex-line section of $\Omega $ is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $. Since $\widetilde u$ is upper semicontinuous and agrees almost everywhere with the finite function $u$, we conclude that $\widetilde u\in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$.

Step 3: $u=\widetilde u$ everywhere. Fix $x\in \Omega $ and $r>0$ with $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset \Omega $. The set $Z=\{ u\ne \widetilde u\} $ is Borel, by Step 1, and Lebesgue null, so the left-hand side of (8.1) vanishes for $F=\mathbf1_ Z$. Since the weight $|t|^{2n-2}$ is positive for $t\neq 0$, for $\tau $-almost every $a$ the set $\{ t:|t|<r,\ x+ta\in Z\} $ has planar measure zero. Fix such an $a$. On the disc $\{ |t|<r\} $, the function $t\mapsto u(x+ta)$ is finite and subharmonic, while $t\mapsto \widetilde u(x+ta)$ is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $; the latter is impossible, because the two functions agree almost everywhere on the disc. By proposition 3.1.5 both are locally integrable subharmonic functions on the disc that agree almost everywhere, so they agree everywhere by theorem 3.2.2. At $t=0$ this gives $u(x)=\widetilde u(x)$. As $x$ was arbitrary, $u=\widetilde u$ is upper semicontinuous. This completes the induction.

Step 4: truncation. Let $u$ be as in the theorem, and for integers $m\geq 1$ put $u_ m=\max \{ u,-m\} +m$. This is a finite nonnegative function. Its restriction to each connected component of each affine complex-line section of $\Omega $ is subharmonic: if the restriction of $u$ is subharmonic, so is its maximum with the constant $-m$, and if the restriction of $u$ is identically $-\infty $, the restriction of $u_ m$ is the constant $0$. By the case already proved, each $u_ m$, and hence each $\max \{ u,-m\} $, is upper semicontinuous. For every real $c$ we have $\{ u<c\} =\bigcup _ m\{ \max \{ u,-m\} <c\} $, which is open. Thus $u$ is upper semicontinuous, and together with the hypotheses this gives $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$. $\square$

8.2 Positive Hessians and holomorphic maps

We now compare plurisubharmonicity with the distributional condition $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$, where $(u_{j\bar k})$ is the complex Hessian of (7.5). The next theorem shows that every almost-everywhere class satisfying this condition contains exactly one plurisubharmonic function, namely its canonical representative. We then treat smooth functions, regularization and holomorphic maps, and define plurisubharmonic functions on complex manifolds.

Theorem 8.2.1. Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$ be a domain, $n\ge 1$. Every $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$ is subharmonic and locally integrable on $\Omega $, and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$. Conversely, a real distribution $T$ on $\Omega $ with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T\geq 0$ has a unique plurisubharmonic representative, that is, there is exactly one $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$ with $\langle T,\chi \rangle =\int _\Omega u\chi \, \mathrm dV_{2n}$ for every test function $\chi $. This representative is the canonical subharmonic representative of $T$ given by theorem 3.2.2, and it satisfies (3.3).

Proof. Let $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$. Being upper semicontinuous, $u$ is bounded above on compact sets. Fix $x\in \Omega $ and $r>0$ with $\overline{B(x,r)}\subset \Omega $, and let $M$ be an upper bound for $u$ on $\overline{B(x,r)}$. For every unit vector $a\in \mathbb {C}^ n$ the closed disc $\{ x+ta:|t|\leq r\} $ lies in $\Omega $, and the restriction $t\mapsto u(x+ta)$ is subharmonic, possibly identically $-\infty $, on a neighborhood of this disc. The monotonicity of circle means in theorem 3.1.3 therefore gives

\[ u(x)\leq \frac1{2\pi }\int _0^{2\pi }u(x+r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta }a)\, \mathrm d\theta . \]

With $\tau $ as in (8.1), we integrate this inequality over $a\in S^{2n-1}$ with respect to $\tau $; by Tonelli's theorem, applied to the nonnegative function $M-u$, and by the invariance of $\tau $ under $a\mapsto \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta }a$, the right-hand side becomes the sphere mean $\int _{S^{2n-1}}u(x+ra)\, \mathrm{d}\tau (a)$. Thus $u$ satisfies the submean inequality of definition 3.1.1 at every point, so $u$ is subharmonic. Since $\Omega $ is connected and $u\not\equiv -\infty $, proposition 3.1.5 shows that $u\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega )$. Since, moreover, the restriction of $u$ to each connected component of each affine complex-line section of $\Omega $ is subharmonic, possibly identically $-\infty $, lemma 8.1.2 gives $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\ge 0$.

Conversely, let $T$ be a real distribution with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T\geq 0$. Then $\Delta T=4\sum _ jT_{j\bar j}\geq 0$, so by theorem 3.2.2 $T$ has a canonical subharmonic representative $v$, which is locally integrable and satisfies (3.3). Since $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T\geq 0$, proposition 7.3.3 shows that the restriction of $v$ to each connected component of each affine complex-line section of $\Omega $ is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $. As $v$ is upper semicontinuous and locally integrable, hence not identically $-\infty $, it is plurisubharmonic. If $w$ is another plurisubharmonic representative of $T$, then $w$ is a locally integrable subharmonic function by the first part, and $w=v$ almost everywhere; by the uniqueness in theorem 3.2.2, $w=v$ everywhere. $\square$

The condition $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$ depends only on the almost-everywhere class of $u$, whereas plurisubharmonicity is a pointwise property, and theorem 8.2.1 singles out one function in each class. For example, the function equal to $1$ at one point $x_0$ and to $0$ elsewhere is upper semicontinuous and has zero distributional complex Hessian, but it violates the submean inequality on complex lines at $x_0$; the plurisubharmonic representative of its class is the zero function.

For smooth functions, plurisubharmonicity is a pointwise condition on the complex Hessian, and every plurisubharmonic function is, locally, the decreasing limit of smooth plurisubharmonic functions. In the next proposition, $\rho _\varepsilon $ is the radial mollifier of section 1.4 and, as there, $\Omega _\varepsilon =\{ x\in \Omega :\operatorname {dist}(x,\mathbb C^ n\setminus \Omega )>\varepsilon \} $ for an open set $\Omega \subset \mathbb C^ n$ and $\varepsilon >0$.

Proposition 8.2.2. Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$ be open, $n\geq 1$.

  1. A real function $u$ of class $C^2$ on $\Omega $ is plurisubharmonic on each connected component of $\Omega $ if and only if the Hermitian matrix $(u_{j\bar k}(z))$ is positive semidefinite at every point $z\in \Omega $.

  2. Let $u$ be a function on $\Omega $ which is plurisubharmonic on each connected component of $\Omega $, and let $\varepsilon >0$. Then $u*\rho _\varepsilon $ is smooth on $\Omega _\varepsilon $ and plurisubharmonic on each connected component of $\Omega _\varepsilon $, and $u*\rho _\varepsilon (x)$ decreases to $u(x)$ as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, for every $x\in \Omega $.

Proof. (1) Let $\Omega _0$ be a connected component of $\Omega $. We show that both conditions are equivalent to $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$ on $\Omega _0$. Since $u$ is of class $C^2$, integration by parts shows that its distributional derivatives $u_{j\bar k}$ are the continuous functions $\partial _{z_ j}\partial _{\bar z_ k}u$, so for $\xi \in \mathbb {C}^ n$ the distribution $\sum _{j,k}u_{j\bar k}\xi _ j\bar\xi _ k$ is the continuous real function $z\mapsto \sum _{j,k}u_{j\bar k}(z)\xi _ j\bar\xi _ k$. A continuous real function is a positive distribution exactly when it is nonnegative: if it were negative at a point, it would be negative on a ball around that point, and its integral against a nonnegative test function supported in this ball and positive at its center would be negative. Hence, by the matrix criterion of section 7.3, $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$ on $\Omega _0$ if and only if $(u_{j\bar k}(z))$ is positive semidefinite at every $z\in \Omega _0$.

If $u$ is plurisubharmonic on $\Omega _0$, then $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$ there by theorem 8.2.1. Conversely, if $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$ on $\Omega _0$, then by theorem 8.2.1 the class of $u$ has a plurisubharmonic representative $v$ on $\Omega _0$ satisfying the ball-average formula in (3.3). Since $v=u$ almost everywhere and $u$ is continuous, $v(x)$ is the limit of the averages of $u$ over the balls $B(x,r)$ as $r\to 0$, that is, $v(x)=u(x)$. Hence $u=v$ is plurisubharmonic on $\Omega _0$.

(2) By theorem 8.2.1, applied on each connected component of $\Omega $, the function $u$ is subharmonic and locally integrable on $\Omega $, and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$. By proposition 1.4.3(1), $u*\rho _\varepsilon $ is smooth on $\Omega _\varepsilon $, and its derivatives are the mollifications of the distributional derivatives of $u$. Thus, for $\xi \in \mathbb {C}^ n$, the function $\sum _{j,k}(u*\rho _\varepsilon )_{j\bar k}\xi _ j\bar\xi _ k$ is the mollification of the distribution $\sum _{j,k}u_{j\bar k}\xi _ j\bar\xi _ k$, which is positive by the matrix criterion; so it is nonnegative by proposition 1.4.3(3). By (1), $u*\rho _\varepsilon $ is plurisubharmonic on each connected component of $\Omega _\varepsilon $. Finally, since $u$ is subharmonic on $\Omega $, theorem 3.2.2(2) shows that $u*\rho _\varepsilon (x)\downarrow u(x)$ as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, for every $x\in \Omega $. $\square$

Let $u$ be a smooth real function on an open set $\Omega \subset \mathbb C^ n$. We call $u$ strictly plurisubharmonic if its complex Hessian matrix $(u_{j\bar k})$ is positive definite everywhere. For a general $u$, strict plurisubharmonicity means that every point has a neighborhood on which $u-\varepsilon |z|^2$ is plurisubharmonic for some $\varepsilon >0$; on a complex manifold we require this in local holomorphic coordinates around each point, and $\varepsilon $ may depend on the point and the coordinates.

For smooth $u$ the two definitions agree. Since $(|z|^2)_{j\bar k}=\delta _{jk}$, the complex Hessian of $u-\varepsilon |z|^2$ is $(u_{j\bar k})-\varepsilon I$. The smallest eigenvalue $\lambda (z)=\min _{|\xi |=1}\sum _{j,k}u_{j\bar k}(z)\xi _ j\bar\xi _ k$ of the matrix $(u_{j\bar k}(z))$ is a continuous function of $z$, as the minimum over the compact unit sphere of a jointly continuous function of $(z,\xi )$. If the matrix is positive definite everywhere, then $\lambda $ is positive, hence bounded below by some $\varepsilon >0$ on a closed ball around each point, and on the open ball the smooth function $u-\varepsilon |z|^2$ has a positive semidefinite complex Hessian, so it is plurisubharmonic by proposition 8.2.2(1). Conversely, the plurisubharmonicity of $u-\varepsilon |z|^2$ on a ball around a point gives $(u_{j\bar k})\geq \varepsilon I$ there, by the same criterion.

Proposition 8.2.3. Let $D\subset \mathbb {C}^ m$ and $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$ be domains, let $F\colon D\to \Omega $ be a holomorphic map, and let $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$. Then $u\circ F$ is either identically $-\infty $ or plurisubharmonic on $D$. More generally, if $D\subset \mathbb {C}^ m$ is only assumed to be open, this alternative holds on each connected component of $D$.

Proof. The statement for open $D$ follows by applying the statement for domains to each connected component of $D$. If $n=0$, then $\Omega $ is a point, $u$ is a real constant, and so is $u\circ F$. If $m=0$, then $D$ is a point and $u\circ F$ is a single value in $[-\infty ,\infty )$; it is either $-\infty $ or a real number, that is, a plurisubharmonic function on the point. Now let $m,n\geq 1$. By theorem 8.2.1, $u$ is the canonical subharmonic representative of a locally integrable function with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq 0$, so proposition 7.3.3 applies: $u\circ F$ is either identically $-\infty $, or locally integrable and subharmonic, in particular upper semicontinuous. In the second case, the restriction of $u\circ F$ to a connected component of an affine complex-line section of $D$ is the composition of $u$ with a holomorphic map from an open subset of $\mathbb {C}$, so it is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $ by the same proposition. Hence $u\circ F$ is plurisubharmonic. $\square$

Let $X$ be a complex manifold. A function $u\colon X\to [-\infty ,\infty )$ is plurisubharmonic if its expression in every holomorphic chart with connected domain is plurisubharmonic in the sense of definition 8.1.1; we write $\mathrm{PSH}(X)$ for the set of such functions.

On a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb C^ n$, plurisubharmonicity is a local property: if every point of $\Omega $ has an open neighborhood on which $u$ is plurisubharmonic, then $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$. Indeed, upper semicontinuity is a local condition. Let $\Lambda $ be a connected component of an affine complex-line section of $\Omega $. Near each point of $\Lambda $, the restriction of $u$ to $\Lambda $ is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $, hence subharmonic; since subharmonicity is a local property, the restriction of $u$ to $\Lambda $ is subharmonic. Finally, $u$ is locally integrable by theorem 8.2.1, hence not identically $-\infty $.

It follows that it suffices to check the condition on $X$ for the charts of one atlas with connected chart domains. Indeed, the transition maps are biholomorphic, so by proposition 8.2.3 the expression of $u$ in another chart with connected domain is, on a connected neighborhood of each point, plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $. The second alternative would make the expression of $u$ in a chart of the atlas identically $-\infty $ on a nonempty open set, which is impossible because plurisubharmonic functions are locally integrable. Hence the expression of $u$ in the other chart is plurisubharmonic near each point, and therefore on its connected domain.

Consequently plurisubharmonicity is also a local property on $X$: if every point of $X$ has a neighborhood on which $u$ is plurisubharmonic, then $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$, because the expression of $u$ in a chart with connected domain is then plurisubharmonic near each point. Since a plurisubharmonic function on $X$ is locally integrable in charts, it is not identically $-\infty $ on any connected component of $X$. On an open set $\Omega \subset \mathbb C^ n$, regarded as a complex manifold, a function is plurisubharmonic exactly when it is plurisubharmonic on each connected component of $\Omega $: each component is a connected chart domain, and conversely the locality just proved applies.

Limits of plurisubharmonic functions may degenerate to the constant $-\infty $. On a connected manifold, whether this happens can be decided locally.

Lemma 8.2.4. Let $X$ be a connected complex manifold, and let $v\colon X\to [-\infty ,\infty )$ be a function such that every point of $X$ has an open neighborhood on which $v$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $. Then $v$ is plurisubharmonic on $X$ or identically $-\infty $ on $X$.

Proof. Let $A$ be the set of points of $X$ having an open neighborhood on which $v\equiv -\infty $, and $B$ the set of points having an open neighborhood on which $v$ is plurisubharmonic. Both sets are open, and they cover $X$ by hypothesis. They are disjoint: if $x\in A\cap B$, with open neighborhoods $W_1$ on which $v\equiv -\infty $ and $W_2$ on which $v$ is plurisubharmonic, then the restriction of $v$ to the nonempty open set $W_1\cap W_2$ is plurisubharmonic and identically $-\infty $, which is impossible because plurisubharmonic functions are locally integrable. Since $X$ is connected, either $X=A$, and then $v\equiv -\infty $, or $X=B$, and then $v$ is plurisubharmonic near every point, hence on $X$, because plurisubharmonicity is a local property. $\square$

Every closed positive $(1,1)$-current $T$ on a complex manifold of positive dimension has local plurisubharmonic potentials: every point has a neighborhood on which $T=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ for a plurisubharmonic function $u$. Indeed, on a coordinate ball inside the neighborhood given by theorem 7.3.2, the canonical local potential is the canonical subharmonic representative of a class with positive complex Hessian, hence plurisubharmonic by theorem 8.2.1.

Proposition 8.2.5. Let $X$ be a complex manifold. Nonnegative multiples, finite sums and maxima of nonempty finite families of plurisubharmonic functions on $X$ are plurisubharmonic; here the zero multiple of a function is the zero function. Moreover, let $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$, let $D\subset X$ be open, and let $v\in \mathrm{PSH}(D)$. If

\[ \varlimsup _{D\ni y\to x}v(y)\le u(x)\qquad \text{for every }x\in \partial D, \]

where $\partial D$ is the boundary of $D$ in $X$, then the function equal to $\max \{ u,v\} $ on $D$ and to $u$ on $X\setminus D$ is plurisubharmonic on $X$. This boundary inequality holds in particular if

\[ \varliminf _{\substack {D\ni y\to x\\ v(y)>-\infty }}(u(y)-v(y))\ge 0 \qquad \text{for every }x\in \partial D. \]

Proof. In local coordinates, upper semicontinuity and the submean inequality on complex lines are preserved by nonnegative multiples, sums and maxima; for sums we use that circle means are additive on functions with values in $[-\infty ,\infty )$. It remains to check that the resulting functions are not identically $-\infty $ on any component. For $cu$ with $c>0$ this holds because $cu=-\infty $ exactly where $u=-\infty $; a sum of plurisubharmonic functions is locally integrable by theorem 8.2.1, and a maximum dominates each of its members. This proves the first assertion.

Let $w$ denote the glued function. On $D$ it equals $\max \{ u,v\} $, which is plurisubharmonic by the first part, and on the open set $X\setminus \overline D$ it equals $u$. At $x\in \partial D$ we have $w(x)=u(x)$, and the upper semicontinuity of $u$ together with the boundary inequality gives

\[ \varlimsup _{y\to x}w(y)\leq \max \Bigl \{ \varlimsup _{y\to x}u(y),\ \varlimsup _{D\ni y\to x}v(y)\Bigr \} \leq u(x)=w(x). \]

Hence $w$ is upper semicontinuous. To verify the line condition, work in a chart and restrict $w$ to a connected component of an affine complex-line section. At points of $D$ and of $X\setminus \overline D$, the restriction satisfies the submean inequality on small circles, because $w$ is plurisubharmonic near such points. At a point $x\in \partial D$ we have $w\geq u$ everywhere and $w(x)=u(x)$, so each small circle mean of $w$ is at least the corresponding circle mean of $u$, which is at least $u(x)=w(x)$; if the restriction of $u$ is identically $-\infty $, then $w(x)=u(x)=-\infty $ and there is nothing to prove. Thus the restriction of $w$ is upper semicontinuous and satisfies the submean inequality on small circles at every point; that is, it is subharmonic. Finally $w\geq u$, so $w$ is not identically $-\infty $ in any chart, and $w$ is plurisubharmonic.

For the sufficient condition, fix $x\in \partial D$ and $\varepsilon >0$. By hypothesis, $x$ has a neighborhood $W$ such that $v(y)<u(y)+\varepsilon $ for all $y\in W\cap D$ with $v(y)>-\infty $. Hence $v\leq u+\varepsilon $ on $W\cap D$, the inequality being trivial where $v=-\infty $, and the upper semicontinuity of $u$ gives

\[ \varlimsup _{D\ni y\to x}v(y)\leq \varlimsup _{y\to x}u(y)+\varepsilon \leq u(x)+\varepsilon . \]

Letting $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ gives the boundary inequality. $\square$

8.3 Limits, envelopes and local integrability

This section treats limits and envelopes of plurisubharmonic functions and their local integrability. Throughout, $X$ is a complex manifold of positive dimension, except in theorem 8.3.7, which also allows dimension zero. By theorem 8.2.1, plurisubharmonic functions are locally integrable, and we regard each of them as the distinguished representative of its class. Local $L^ p$ norms on $X$ are computed with Euclidean volume in local coordinates, and “almost everywhere” refers to Lebesgue measure in charts. Any smooth positive volume form gives the same local $L^ p$ spaces and the same $L^ p_{\mathrm{loc}}$ convergence, since in coordinates its density is bounded above and below by positive constants on compact sets. By the discussion following definition 7.1.1, each connected component of $X$ has a countable basis; $X$ itself need not have one, and we assume this explicitly when a global countable selection is needed.

We call a subset of an open set in $\mathbb {C}^ n$ real polar if it is polar in the sense of definition 3.5.1 when $\mathbb {C}^ n$ is identified with $\mathbb R^{2n}$. The adjective distinguishes these sets from the pluripolar sets of chapter 10. Real polar sets are Lebesgue null, by the remark following definition 3.5.1.

Theorem 8.3.1. Let $(u_ i)_{i\in I}$ be a decreasing net in $\mathrm{PSH}(X)$, that is, $u_ j\leq u_ i$ whenever $i\leq j$, and let $u=\lim _ iu_ i=\inf _ iu_ i$ be its pointwise limit. Then on each connected component $X_0$ of $X$, exactly one of the following holds: either $u$ is plurisubharmonic on $X_0$ and $u_ i\to u$ in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(X_0)$, or $u\equiv -\infty $ on $X_0$ and $u_ i\to -\infty $ locally uniformly on $X_0$.

Proof. The limit $u$ is upper semicontinuous, since $\{ u<c\} =\bigcup _ i\{ u_ i<c\} $ is open for every real $c$. Fix a connected component $X_0$ of $X$; it has a countable basis.

Step 1: a decreasing sequence with the same limit. Let $i_0\in I$. We show that there are indices $i_0\leq i_1\leq i_2\leq \cdots $ such that $u_{i_ l}\downarrow u$ pointwise on $X_0$. Replacing $I$ by the cofinal directed set $\{ i\in I:i\geq i_0\} $, which does not change the limit, we only have to find indices $i_1\leq i_2\leq \cdots $. For each rational $c$, the open sets $\{ u_ i<c\} \cap X_0$ cover $\{ u<c\} \cap X_0$, and countably many of them suffice: for each basis element of $X_0$ contained in some set $\{ u_ i<c\} $, choose one such index $i$; every point of $\{ u<c\} \cap X_0$ lies in such a basis element. Let $k_1,k_2,\ldots $ be an enumeration, with repetitions allowed, of the indices chosen for all rational $c$. Since $I$ is directed, we can choose inductively $i_1\geq k_1$ and $i_{l+1}\geq i_ l,k_{l+1}$. The sequence $(u_{i_ l})$ is decreasing and bounded below by $u$. If $u(x)<c$ with $c$ rational and $x\in X_0$, then $u_{k_ l}(x)<c$ for some $l$, hence $u_{i_ l}(x)\leq u_{k_ l}(x)<c$. Therefore $u_{i_ l}\downarrow u$ on $X_0$.

Step 2: the limit. Let $V\subset X_0$ be a connected coordinate neighborhood, identified with a domain in $\mathbb {C}^ n$. On each connected component of each affine complex-line section of $V$, the restrictions of the $u_{i_ l}$ form a decreasing sequence of subharmonic functions, whose limit is subharmonic by theorem 3.1.6. Since $u$ is upper semicontinuous, it is either plurisubharmonic on $V$ or identically $-\infty $ on $V$, by definition 8.1.1. As $X_0$ is connected, lemma 8.2.4 shows that either $u$ is plurisubharmonic on $X_0$ or $u\equiv -\infty $ on $X_0$.

Step 3: convergence. Suppose first that $u$ is plurisubharmonic on $X_0$. Fix $i_0\in I$, take the sequence of Step 1, and let $K$ be a compact subset of a coordinate neighborhood in $X_0$. Then $0\leq u_{i_ l}-u\leq u_{i_0}-u$ almost everywhere, namely wherever $u$ is finite, and $u_{i_0}-u$ is integrable on $K$. By dominated convergence, $\| u_{i_ l}-u\| _{L^1(K)}\to 0$. Given $\varepsilon >0$, choose $l$ with $\| u_{i_ l}-u\| _{L^1(K)}<\varepsilon $; for every $i\geq i_ l$ we have $0\leq u_ i-u\leq u_{i_ l}-u$ almost everywhere, hence $\| u_ i-u\| _{L^1(K)}<\varepsilon $. Thus the net converges to $u$ in $L^1(K)$, and covering an arbitrary compact subset of $X_0$ by finitely many such sets $K$ gives convergence in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(X_0)$.

Suppose now that $u\equiv -\infty $ on $X_0$, and let $K\subset X_0$ be compact and $A>0$. The open sets $\{ u_ i<-A\} $ cover $K$, so finitely many of them, say those with indices $j_1,\ldots ,j_ p$, already cover $K$. If $i\geq j_1,\ldots ,j_ p$, then $u_ i\leq u_{j_ q}$ for each $q$, and hence $u_ i<-A$ on $K$. This is locally uniform convergence to $-\infty $. $\square$

Recall that a function $F$ with values in $[-\infty ,\infty )$ that is locally bounded above has an upper semicontinuous regularization $F^*$, the smallest upper semicontinuous function that dominates $F$. On $X$ it is given by $F^*(x)=\inf _ W\sup _ WF$, where $W$ ranges over the neighborhoods of $x$, and in a chart it is the regularization of section 3.4.

Theorem 8.3.2. Let $(u_ i)_{i\in I}$ be a nonempty family in $\mathrm{PSH}(X)$ that is locally uniformly bounded above, and let $F=\sup _ iu_ i$ be its upper envelope. Then the upper semicontinuous regularization $F^*$ is plurisubharmonic, and $F=F^*$ almost everywhere; more precisely, in every coordinate chart the set $\{ F<F^*\} $ is real polar. If $X$ has a countable basis, there is a countable subset $J\subset I$ such that $(\sup _{i\in J}u_ i)^*=F^*$.

Proof. Since $F^*$ is defined by a local formula and plurisubharmonicity is a local property, it suffices to prove that $F^*$ is plurisubharmonic on every connected coordinate neighborhood $V$, which we identify with a domain in $\mathbb {C}^ n$. By theorem 3.4.2, $F^*$ is subharmonic on $V$, and $F$ is Lebesgue measurable and equal to $F^*$ almost everywhere on $V$. By Choquet's lemma, lemma 3.4.1, there is a countable set $J_ V\subset I$ with $(\sup _{i\in J_ V}u_ i)^*=F^*$ on $V$.

We next show that $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F^*\geq 0$ on $V$. Fix $i_0\in I$, enumerate $J_ V\cup \{ i_0\} $ as $i_0,i_1,i_2,\ldots $, with repetitions if this set is finite, and put $v_ j=\max \{ u_{i_0},\ldots ,u_{i_ j}\} $. The functions $v_ j$ are plurisubharmonic by proposition 8.2.5, increase with $j$, are locally uniformly bounded above, and dominate the locally integrable function $u_{i_0}$. Their limit $G=\sup _ jv_ j$ lies between $\sup _{i\in J_ V}u_ i$ and $F$, so $G^*=F^*$, and hence $G=F^*$ almost everywhere by theorem 3.4.2 applied to the countable family. On a compact set $K\subset V$ on which every $u_ i$ is bounded above by $M$, we have $0\leq v_ j-u_{i_0}\leq M-u_{i_0}$, so dominated convergence gives $v_ j\to F^*$ in $L^1(K)$. Therefore $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F^*\geq 0$, as a limit of positive currents. Since $F^*$ is subharmonic and dominates the locally integrable function $u_{i_0}$, it is locally integrable and hence the canonical representative of its class, and theorem 8.2.1 shows that $F^*$ is plurisubharmonic on $V$.

If $X$ has a countable basis, cover $X$ by countably many connected coordinate neighborhoods $V_1,V_2,\ldots $ and put $J=\bigcup _ mJ_{V_ m}$. On each $V_ m$ we have $F^*=(\sup _{i\in J_{V_ m}}u_ i)^*\leq (\sup _{i\in J}u_ i)^*\leq F^*$, and since the regularization is local, $(\sup _{i\in J}u_ i)^*=F^*$ on $X$.

Finally, in each chart the functions $u_ i$ are subharmonic, so the second assertion of theorem 4.4.4 shows that $\{ F<F^*\} $ is polar in the corresponding open subset of $\mathbb R^{2n}$, that is, real polar. $\square$

Proposition 8.3.3. Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$ be a domain, $n\geq 1$, and let $g=(g_1,\ldots ,g_ r)$, $r\geq 1$, be a tuple of holomorphic functions on $\Omega $, not all identically zero. Then the function $\log \sum _ j|g_ j|^2$, with the value $-\infty $ at the common zeros of the $g_ j$, is plurisubharmonic on $\Omega $. In particular, for every $a\in \mathbb {C}^ n$ the function $\log |z-a|^2$ is plurisubharmonic on $\mathbb {C}^ n$.

Proof. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, with equality for $\lambda $ proportional to $(\overline{g_1(z)},\ldots ,\overline{g_ r(z)})$, we have

\[ \log \sum _ j|g_ j(z)|^2 =\sup _{\sum _ j|\lambda _ j|^2=1}\log \left|\sum _ j\lambda _ jg_ j(z)\right|^2 \]

for every $z\in \Omega $, both sides being $-\infty $ at a common zero. The supremum does not change if we omit the $\lambda $ for which $\sum _ j\lambda _ jg_ j$ vanishes identically: at a point $z$ that is not a common zero, the maximizing $\lambda $ gives $\sum _ j\lambda _ jg_ j(z)\neq 0$, and at a common zero all terms equal $-\infty $. Some $g_ j$ is not identically zero, so the remaining family is nonempty. Its members are plurisubharmonic by proposition 7.4.3, theorem 8.2.1, and they are locally uniformly bounded above by the left-hand side. Their supremum $\log \sum _ j|g_ j|^2$ is already upper semicontinuous: it is continuous as a map to $[-\infty ,\infty )$, because $\sum _ j|g_ j|^2$ is continuous and its logarithm tends to $-\infty $ at a common zero. Hence it equals its regularization, which is plurisubharmonic by theorem 8.3.2. The particular case is the tuple $(z_1-a_1,\ldots ,z_ n-a_ n)$. $\square$

Theorem 8.3.4. Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb C^ n$ be a domain, $n\geq 1$. Every sequence in $\mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$ that is locally uniformly bounded above has a subsequence that either converges locally uniformly to $-\infty $ or converges in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega )$ to a plurisubharmonic function. If a sequence $(u_ j)$ in $\mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$ converges in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega )$, then its limit has a plurisubharmonic representative $u$, and the following holds: for every compact set $K\subset \Omega $ and every lower semicontinuous function $f\colon K\to \mathbb R$ with $u<f$ on $K$, for instance every real continuous function on $K$, one has $u_ j<f$ on $K$ for all sufficiently large $j$. In particular $\varlimsup _ j u_ j(x)\le u(x)$ for every $x\in \Omega $.

Proof. Plurisubharmonic functions are subharmonic by theorem 8.2.1. Hence, by the compactness theorem (theorem 3.3.2), a sequence as in the first assertion either tends to $-\infty $ locally uniformly, or has a subsequence converging in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega )$ to a locally integrable subharmonic function $v$. In the second case $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v\geq 0$, as a distributional limit of positive currents, and $v$ is the canonical representative of its class; so $v$ is plurisubharmonic by theorem 8.2.1.

Now let $(u_ j)$ converge in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega )$ to $T$. As above, $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T\geq 0$, so $T$ has a plurisubharmonic representative $u$, namely its canonical subharmonic representative. The $u_ j$ are locally integrable subharmonic functions converging in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega )$, and $u$ is the canonical representative of their limit, so the remaining assertions are Hartogs' lemma for subharmonic functions, theorem 3.3.3, which requires no upper bound on the sequence; the last one is its first assertion with the constant sequence $x_ j=x$. $\square$

We next show that plurisubharmonic functions belong to $L^ p_{\mathrm{loc}}$ for every finite $p$, and that $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$ convergence of plurisubharmonic functions implies $L^ p_{\mathrm{loc}}$ convergence. We first treat a subharmonic function $v\leq 0$ on the unit disc. By the Riesz decomposition (theorem 3.2.3), $v$ is locally the sum of a harmonic function and the fundamental potential $E_2*\mu $ of a measure, and the kernel $E_2(z)=(2\pi )^{-1}\log |z|$ belongs locally to every $L^ p$ with $p<\infty $; this gives an $L^ p$ bound in terms of the value of $v$ at the center. Applying this bound in one complex variable at a time then gives the estimate for plurisubharmonic functions on polydiscs.

We write $D(a,r)=\{ z\in \mathbb C:|z-a|<r\} $ and $D=D(0,1)$, and for $t>0$ we write $tD=D(0,t)$; thus $D^ n$ is the unit polydisc in $\mathbb C^ n$ and $(tD)^ n$ the polydisc of radius $t$ centered at $0$. Integrals over discs and polydiscs are taken with respect to Lebesgue measure $\mathrm dV$.

Lemma 8.3.5. There is a constant $C_0\geq 1$ with the following property. Let $v\leq 0$ be a subharmonic function on $D$ with $v(0)>-\infty $. Then there are a finite positive Borel measure $\nu $ with support in $D(0,9/10)$ and a harmonic function $h$ on $D(0,4/5)$ such that

8.4
\begin{equation} \begin{gathered} v(z)=P(z)+h(z)\quad (z\in D(0,4/5)),\qquad P(z)=\frac1{2\pi }\int \log |z-\zeta |\, \mathrm d\nu (\zeta ),\\ \nu (D)\leq C_0|v(0)|,\qquad \sup _{\overline{D(0,1/2)}}|h|\leq C_0|v(0)|. \end{gathered} \end{equation}

Moreover, for every $1\leq p<\infty $ there is a constant $C_ p>0$ such that every such $v$ satisfies

\[ \| v\| _{L^ p(D(0,1/2))}\leq C_ p|v(0)|. \]

Proof. In this proof $C$ denotes constants that depend only on two cutoff functions $\chi $ and $\eta $ fixed below, and $C'$ constants that depend in addition on $p$. Since $v(0)>-\infty $, the function $v$ is not identically $-\infty $, and hence it is locally integrable by proposition 3.1.5. For $0<r<1$, the ball submean inequality (theorem 3.1.3) and $v\leq 0$ give

8.5
\begin{equation} \| v\| _{L^1(D(0,r))}=-\int _{D(0,r)}v\, \mathrm dV\leq \pi r^2|v(0)|. \end{equation}

Let $\mu =\Delta v$ be the Riesz measure of $v$, and choose a smooth cutoff $\chi \geq 0$ supported in $D(0,19/20)$ and equal to one on $D(0,9/10)$. By the definition of the distributional Laplacian and (8.5),

\[ \mu (D(0,9/10))\leq \int \chi \, \mathrm d\mu =\int v\Delta \chi \, \mathrm dV\leq \sup |\Delta \chi |\, \| v\| _{L^1(D(0,19/20))}\leq C|v(0)|. \]

Next choose a smooth cutoff $0\leq \eta \leq 1$ supported in $D(0,9/10)$ and equal to one on a neighborhood of $\overline{D(0,4/5)}$. The fundamental solution in the plane is $(2\pi )^{-1}\log |z|$, so theorem 3.2.3, applied with the measure $\nu =\eta \mu $, which is finite and supported in $\operatorname{Supp}\eta \subset D(0,9/10)$, gives

\[ v(z)=P(z)+h(z),\qquad P(z)=\frac1{2\pi }\int \log |z-\zeta |\, \mathrm d\nu (\zeta ), \]

for every $z\in D(0,4/5)$, with $h$ harmonic on $D(0,4/5)$.

We estimate $P$. For $|\zeta |\leq 9/10$, the translated disc $D(0,4/5)-\zeta $ lies in $D(0,2)$, so

\[ \int _{D(0,4/5)}|\log |z-\zeta ||^ p\, \mathrm dV(z) \leq L_ p=\int _{D(0,2)}|\log |z||^ p\, \mathrm dV(z)<\infty ; \]

the integral $L_ p$ is finite because its part over $D$ equals $2\pi \int _0^1r|\log r|^ p\, \mathrm dr$, whose integrand is bounded on $(0,1]$, while $|\log |z||\leq \log 2$ on the rest of $D(0,2)$. Let $m=\nu (D)=\int \eta \, \mathrm d\mu $, so that $m\leq \mu (D(0,9/10))\leq C|v(0)|$. If $m=0$, then $P=0$. Otherwise, $|P(z)|\leq \frac m{2\pi }\int |\log |z-\zeta ||\, \mathrm d\nu (\zeta )/m$, and Hölder's inequality for the probability measure $\nu /m$ gives

\[ |P(z)|^ p\leq \Bigl (\frac m{2\pi }\Bigr )^ p\int |\log |z-\zeta ||^ p\, \frac{\mathrm d\nu (\zeta )}m, \]

and integrating over $z\in D(0,4/5)$ with Tonelli's theorem yields $\| P\| _{L^ p(D(0,4/5))}\leq L_ p^{1/p}m/(2\pi )\leq C'|v(0)|$.

We now estimate $h$. The case $p=1$ of the bound for $P$, in which $L_1$ is an absolute constant, and (8.5) give $\| h\| _{L^1(D(0,4/5))}\leq C|v(0)|$. For $|z|\leq 1/2$, the closed disc $\overline{D(z,1/4)}$ lies in $D(0,4/5)$, and the ball mean-value property of the harmonic function $h$ (proposition 2.1.1) gives $|h(z)|\leq (16/\pi )\| h\| _{L^1(D(0,4/5))}$. We have thus found a constant $C_0\geq 1$, depending only on the cutoff functions $\chi $ and $\eta $, such that $\nu (D)=m\leq C_0|v(0)|$ and $\sup _{\overline{D(0,1/2)}}|h|\leq C_0|v(0)|$; this proves (8.4). Adding the estimates for $P$ and $h$ on $D(0,1/2)$, we obtain

\[ \| v\| _{L^ p(D(0,1/2))}\leq \| P\| _{L^ p(D(0,4/5))}+(\pi /4)^{1/p}C_0|v(0)|\leq C'|v(0)|, \]

which proves the last assertion. $\square$

Proposition 8.3.6. Let $n\geq 1$ and $1\leq p<\infty $. There are constants $C_{n,p},A_{n,p}>0$ such that every plurisubharmonic function $v\leq 0$ on the unit polydisc $D^ n$ satisfies

\[ \| v\| _{L^ p((\tfrac 12D)^ n)}\leq C_{n,p}|v(0)| \quad \text{if }v(0)>-\infty , \]

and

8.6
\begin{equation} \| v\| _{L^ p((\tfrac 14D)^ n)}\leq A_{n,p}\| v\| _{L^1(D^ n)}. \end{equation}

Here $\| v\| _{L^1(D^ n)}$ may be infinite, in which case there is nothing to prove.

Proof. We prove the first estimate by induction on $n$. For $n=1$ it is lemma 8.3.5. Let $n\geq 2$, write $z=(z_1,z')$ with $z'\in \mathbb C^{n-1}$, and let $v$ be as in the statement with $v(0)>-\infty $. The function $w(z')=v(0,z')$ is the composition of $v$ with the holomorphic map $z'\mapsto (0,z')$, so by proposition 8.2.3 it is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on $D^{n-1}$. Since $w(0)=v(0)>-\infty $, it is plurisubharmonic, hence locally integrable and finite outside a Lebesgue null set $E\subset D^{n-1}$. For $z'\in D^{n-1}\setminus E$, the function $z_1\mapsto v(z_1,z')$ is subharmonic and nonpositive on $D$, with the finite value $w(z')$ at the center, so lemma 8.3.5 gives

\[ \int _{\frac12D}|v(z_1,z')|^ p\, \mathrm dV(z_1) \leq C_ p^ p|w(z')|^ p. \]

We integrate over $z'\in (\tfrac 12D)^{n-1}$ using Tonelli's theorem; the null set $E$, on which the inner integral may be infinite, does not contribute. The induction hypothesis applied to $w$ then gives

\[ \| v\| _{L^ p((\frac12D)^ n)}^ p\leq C_ p^ p\| w\| _{L^ p((\frac12D)^{n-1})}^ p\leq C_ p^ pC_{n-1,p}^ p|v(0)|^ p, \]

which is the first estimate with $C_{n,p}=C_ pC_{n-1,p}$.

For the second estimate, let $Q=(\tfrac 1{10}D)^ n$ and $|Q|$ its volume. If $\| v\| _{L^1(D^ n)}=0$, then $v=0$ almost everywhere, hence $v\equiv 0$ by theorem 8.2.1, and there is nothing to prove; we may also assume that $\| v\| _{L^1(D^ n)}<\infty $. Put $\lambda =2|Q|^{-1}\| v\| _{L^1(D^ n)}>0$. By Chebyshev's inequality, the set of points of $Q$ with $|v|>\lambda $ has measure at most $\lambda ^{-1}\int _ Q|v|\, \mathrm dV\leq |Q|/2$, so its complement in $Q$ has positive measure. Since $v$ is finite almost everywhere, there is a point $a\in Q$ with $v(a)>-\infty $ and

\[ |v(a)|\leq 2|Q|^{-1}\| v\| _{L^1(D^ n)}. \]

The polydisc of radius $4/5$ centered at $a$ lies in $D^ n$, and the polydisc of radius $2/5$ centered at $a$ contains $(\tfrac 14D)^ n$, since $\tfrac 14+\tfrac 1{10}<\tfrac 25$. We apply the first estimate to the plurisubharmonic function $\zeta \mapsto v(a+\tfrac 45\zeta )\leq 0$ on $D^ n$ and change variables:

\[ \| v\| _{L^ p((\frac14D)^ n)}\leq \bigl (\tfrac 45\bigr )^{2n/p}C_{n,p}|v(a)|\leq 2\bigl (\tfrac 45\bigr )^{2n/p}C_{n,p}|Q|^{-1}\| v\| _{L^1(D^ n)}. \]

This is (8.6). $\square$

Theorem 8.3.7. Let $X$ be a complex manifold and $1\leq p<\infty $. Every plurisubharmonic function on $X$ belongs to $L^ p_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)$. If a sequence of plurisubharmonic functions on $X$ converges in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)$, then it converges in $L^ p_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)$ to the same limit.

Proof. If $X$ has dimension zero, every compact subset of $X$ is finite, plurisubharmonic functions are real-valued, and convergence in $L^ p_{\mathrm{loc}}$ is pointwise convergence; so both assertions hold. Let $X$ have dimension $n\geq 1$. Every compact subset of $X$ is covered by finitely many coordinate polydiscs, so it suffices to work near a point $x\in X$ in a chart in which $x=0$ and the closed polydisc $\overline{(RD)^ n}$ lies in the chart, for some $R>0$.

Let $v$ be plurisubharmonic. Being upper semicontinuous, $v$ is bounded above on $\overline{(RD)^ n}$ by some constant $A$. The function $\zeta \mapsto v(R\zeta )-A$ is plurisubharmonic and nonpositive on $D^ n$, and its $L^1$ norm on $D^ n$ is finite because $v$ is locally integrable. By (8.6), $v$ belongs to $L^ p$ on the polydisc $(\tfrac R4D)^ n$. This proves the first assertion.

Now let $(u_ j)$ be a sequence of plurisubharmonic functions converging in $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)$. For $p=1$ there is nothing to prove, so let $p>1$. By theorem 8.3.4 applied in charts, together with the uniqueness in theorem 8.2.1, the limit is represented by a plurisubharmonic function $u$. The norms $\| u_ j\| _{L^1((RD)^ n)}$ are bounded because the sequence converges. If $y\in (\tfrac R2D)^ n$, the ball $B(y,R/2)$ lies in $(RD)^ n$, so the ball submean inequality gives $u_ j(y)\leq |B(y,R/2)|^{-1}\| u_ j\| _{L^1((RD)^ n)}$. Hence there is a constant $M$, independent of $j$, with $u_ j\leq M$ and $u\leq M$ on $(\tfrac R2D)^ n$. Applying (8.6) to $\zeta \mapsto u_ j(\tfrac R2\zeta )-M$ and to $\zeta \mapsto u(\tfrac R2\zeta )-M$, whose $L^1$ norms on $D^ n$ are bounded independently of $j$, we obtain a bound for the $L^{2p}$ norms of $u_ j$ and $u$ on $(\tfrac R8D)^ n$ that is uniform in $j$. By a finite covering, for every compact set $K\subset X$ the norms $\| u_ j\| _{L^{2p}(K)}$ and $\| u\| _{L^{2p}(K)}$ are bounded independently of $j$.

We interpolate between $L^1$ and $L^{2p}$. For a function $f$ on $K$, write $|f|^ p=|f|^{p/(2p-1)}\cdot |f|^{p(2p-2)/(2p-1)}$ and apply Hölder's inequality with the conjugate exponents $(2p-1)/p$ and $(2p-1)/(p-1)$; since $\frac{p(2p-2)}{2p-1}\cdot \frac{2p-1}{p-1}=2p$, this gives

\[ \int _ K|f|^ p\, \mathrm dV\leq \Bigl (\int _ K|f|\, \mathrm dV\Bigr )^{p/(2p-1)}\Bigl (\int _ K|f|^{2p}\, \mathrm dV\Bigr )^{(p-1)/(2p-1)}, \]

that is, $\| f\| _{L^ p(K)}\leq \| f\| _{L^1(K)}^{1/(2p-1)}\| f\| _{L^{2p}(K)}^{(2p-2)/(2p-1)}$. With $f=u_ j-u$, whose $L^{2p}(K)$ norms are bounded independently of $j$, we obtain

\[ \| u_ j-u\| _{L^ p(K)} \leq \| u_ j-u\| _{L^1(K)}^{1/(2p-1)} \| u_ j-u\| _{L^{2p}(K)}^{(2p-2)/(2p-1)}\longrightarrow 0. \]
$\square$

The same Riesz decomposition gives exponential integrability: if $v\leq 0$ is plurisubharmonic on a ball, then $\mathrm e^{-\alpha v}$ is integrable on a smaller concentric ball for every sufficiently small $\alpha >0$, and the admissible $\alpha $ and the bound for the integral depend only on a bound for the $L^1$ norm of $v$. In one variable this follows from Jensen's inequality applied to the fundamental potential in the Riesz decomposition. In several variables we integrate the one-variable estimates over all complex lines through a suitable point. For $t>0$ we write $tB$ for the ball of radius $t$ centered at $0$ in $\mathbb C^ n$; thus $B$ is the unit ball.

Lemma 8.3.8. There are constants $\beta ,C>0$ such that every subharmonic function $v\leq 0$ on $D$ with $v(0)\geq -1$ satisfies

\[ \int _{D(0,1/2)}\mathrm e^{-\beta v}\, \mathrm dV\leq C. \]

Proof. Since $v(0)\geq -1>-\infty $, lemma 8.3.5 gives a finite positive measure $\nu $ with support in $D(0,9/10)$ and a harmonic function $h$ on $D(0,4/5)$ such that

\[ v=P+h,\qquad P(z)=\frac1{2\pi }\int \log |z-\zeta |\, \mathrm d\nu (\zeta ) \]

at every point of $D(0,4/5)$. Since $|v(0)|\leq 1$, the bounds (8.4) give $M=\nu (D)\leq C_0$ and $\sup _{D(0,1/2)}|h|\leq C_0$, where $C_0\geq 1$ is the universal constant of that lemma. Fix $\beta \in (0,1]$ with $\beta C_0\leq 2\pi $.

We show that $\int _{D(0,1/2)}\mathrm e^{-\beta P}\, \mathrm dV\leq 8\pi $. If $M=0$, then $P=0$, and this integral is $\pi /4$. Assume $M>0$ and put $b=\beta M/(2\pi )\in (0,1]$. Fix $z\in D(0,1/2)$. For $N\geq 1$ the Borel function $f_ N(\zeta )=-b\max \{ \log |z-\zeta |,-N\} $ is bounded on $D(0,9/10)$, so Jensen's inequality (lemma 1.1.4), applied to the convex function $\exp $ on $\mathbb R$ and the probability measure $\nu /M$, gives

\[ \exp \Bigl (\int f_ N\, \frac{\mathrm d\nu }M\Bigr )\leq \int \mathrm e^{f_ N}\, \frac{\mathrm d\nu }M\leq \frac1M\int |z-\zeta |^{-b}\, \mathrm d\nu (\zeta ). \]

As $N\to \infty $, the functions $f_ N$ increase to $-b\log |z-\zeta |$, which may take the value $+\infty $, and they are bounded below by the bounded function $f_1$. By monotone convergence, $\int f_ N\, \mathrm d\nu /M$ increases to $\int -b\log |z-\zeta |\, \mathrm d\nu (\zeta )/M=-\beta P(z)$, and therefore

\[ \mathrm e^{-\beta P(z)}\leq \frac1M\int |z-\zeta |^{-b}\, \mathrm d\nu (\zeta ) \]

for every $z\in D(0,1/2)$, with the convention $\mathrm e^{\infty }=\infty $. For every $\zeta \in D(0,9/10)$ the translated disc $D(0,1/2)-\zeta $ lies in $D(0,2)$, and $r^{-b}\leq 1+r^{-1}$ for $r>0$ because $0<b\leq 1$; hence

\[ \int _{D(0,1/2)}|z-\zeta |^{-b}\, \mathrm dV(z) \leq \int _{D(0,2)}(1+|z|^{-1})\, \mathrm dV(z)=8\pi . \]

Integrating the bound for $\mathrm e^{-\beta P(z)}$ over $z\in D(0,1/2)$ with Tonelli's theorem, we obtain $\int _{D(0,1/2)}\mathrm e^{-\beta P}\, \mathrm dV\leq 8\pi $. Finally, since $\mathrm e^{-\beta v}=\mathrm e^{-\beta P}\mathrm e^{-\beta h}\leq \mathrm e^{\beta C_0}\mathrm e^{-\beta P}$ on $D(0,1/2)$, the lemma follows with $C=8\pi \mathrm e^{C_0}$. $\square$

Proposition 8.3.9. Let $n\geq 1$, and let $B$ be the unit ball of $\mathbb C^ n$. There are constants $\alpha _ n,C_ n>0$ such that every plurisubharmonic function $v\leq 0$ on $B$ with $\| v\| _{L^1(B)}\leq 1$ satisfies

8.7
\begin{equation} \int _{\frac14B}\mathrm e^{-\alpha _ n v}\, \mathrm dV\leq C_ n. \end{equation}

More generally, if $\| v\| _{L^1(B)}\leq M$ for some $M>0$, the same bound holds with the exponent $\alpha _ n/M$ in place of $\alpha _ n$.

Proof. Put $A_ n=\max \{ 1,2/|\frac18B|\} $. The mean of $|v|$ over $\frac18B$ is at most $1/|\frac18B|<A_ n$. Hence the set of points of $\frac18B$ with $|v|\leq A_ n$ has positive measure, since otherwise $|v|>A_ n$ almost everywhere on $\frac18B$ and the mean would be at least $A_ n$; so there is a point $a\in \frac18B$ with $|v(a)|\leq A_ n$. For each unit vector $e\in \mathbb C^ n$, the disc $\{ a+\tfrac 34\zeta e:|\zeta |<1\} $ lies in $B$, so the function

\[ \zeta \longmapsto A_ n^{-1}v(a+\tfrac 34\zeta e) \]

is subharmonic and nonpositive on $D$, with value at least $-1$ at $0$. Set $\alpha _ n=\beta /A_ n$, with $\beta $ as in lemma 8.3.8. That lemma and the change of variables $\zeta \mapsto \frac34\zeta $ give a bound, independent of $e$ and $v$, for

\[ \int _{|\zeta |<3/8}\mathrm e^{-\alpha _ n v(a+\zeta e)}\, \mathrm dV(\zeta ). \]

It remains to pass from these integrals over discs in complex lines to an integral over a ball. Let $\tau $ and $s_{2n-1}$ be as in (8.1). By that identity, applied with center $a$, and since $|\zeta |^{2n-2}\leq r^{2n-2}$ for $|\zeta |<r$, every Borel function $F\geq 0$, possibly taking the value $+\infty $, satisfies

\[ \int _{|z-a|<r}F(z)\, \mathrm dV(z) \leq \frac{s_{2n-1}r^{2n-2}}{2\pi } \int _{S^{2n-1}}\int _{|\zeta |<r}F(a+\zeta e)\, \mathrm dV(\zeta )\, \mathrm d\tau (e) \]

for every $r>0$. We apply this with $F=\mathrm e^{-\alpha _ n v}$ and $r=3/8$. Since $\frac14B\subset B(a,3/8)$, the uniform bound for the line integrals proves (8.7). Finally, if $\| v\| _{L^1(B)}\leq M$ with $M>0$, then $v/M$ is a nonpositive plurisubharmonic function with $\| v/M\| _{L^1(B)}\leq 1$, and (8.7) for $v/M$ is the last assertion. $\square$

Consequently, every plurisubharmonic function $u$ on a complex manifold $X$ is locally exponentially integrable: every point $x\in X$ has a neighborhood $W$ for which there is $\alpha _0>0$ such that $\mathrm e^{-\alpha u}$ is integrable on $W$ for $0<\alpha \leq \alpha _0$, the integrals being computed in local coordinates. In dimension zero we may take $W=\{ x\} $. In dimension $n\geq 1$, choose a chart in which $x=0$ and the closed ball $\overline{B(0,R)}$ lies in the chart domain, for some $R>0$, and let $c$ be an upper bound for $u$ on this ball. The function $v(\zeta )=u(R\zeta )-c$ is plurisubharmonic and nonpositive on the unit ball $B$, and since $u$ is locally integrable there is $A>0$ with $\| v\| _{L^1(B)}\leq A$. Put $\alpha _0=\alpha _ n/A$ and $W=B(0,R/4)$. For $0<\alpha \leq \alpha _0$ we have $\mathrm e^{-\alpha v}\leq \mathrm e^{-\alpha _0v}$, because $v\leq 0$, so the change of variables $z=R\zeta $ and the last assertion of proposition 8.3.9 give

\[ \int _ W\mathrm e^{-\alpha u}\, \mathrm dV=R^{2n}\mathrm e^{-\alpha c}\int _{\frac14B}\mathrm e^{-\alpha v}\, \mathrm dV\leq R^{2n}\mathrm e^{-\alpha c}C_ n<\infty . \]

8.4 Extension across closed exceptional sets

Let $S$ be a relatively closed subset of a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$. We give two conditions under which plurisubharmonic functions on $\Omega \setminus S$ extend across $S$. In the first, $S$ is locally contained in the zero set of a holomorphic function, and the function is assumed to be locally bounded above near $S$; a logarithmic barrier built from the holomorphic function then gives the extension. In the second, $S$ is small in the sense of Hausdorff measure, and the local upper bound is not assumed but follows from the smallness of $S$ by an argument with complex lines.

Theorem 8.4.1. Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$ be a domain, $n\ge 1$, and let $S\subset \Omega $ be relatively closed. Assume that every point of $S$ has a connected neighborhood $W\subset \Omega $ with a holomorphic function $f\not\equiv 0$ on $W$ such that $S\cap W\subset \{ f=0\} $. Let $u$ be a plurisubharmonic function on $\Omega \setminus S$ that is locally bounded above near $S$, that is, every point of $S$ has a neighborhood $W$ such that $u$ is bounded above on $W\setminus S$. Then $u$ has a unique plurisubharmonic extension $\widetilde u$ to $\Omega $, and its value at $x\in S$ is

\[ \widetilde u(x)=\varlimsup _{\Omega \setminus S\ni y\to x}u(y). \]

Proof. Define $\widetilde u$ by $u$ on $\Omega \setminus S$ and by the displayed formula on $S$. Since plurisubharmonicity is a local property, it suffices to show that every point $x_0\in S$ has a neighborhood on which $\widetilde u$ is plurisubharmonic; uniqueness is proved at the end. By hypothesis there are a connected neighborhood $W_0$ of $x_0$ with a holomorphic function $f\not\equiv 0$ on $W_0$ such that $S\cap W_0\subset \{ f=0\} $, and a neighborhood $W_1$ of $x_0$ such that $u$ is bounded above on $W_1\setminus S$. Choose a ball $W$ about $x_0$ with $\overline W\subset W_0\cap W_1$. By the identity theorem, $f$ does not vanish identically on $W$, and $u$ is bounded above on $W\setminus S$. Multiplying $f$ by a nonzero constant, we may assume that $|f|\leq 1$, that is, $\log |f|^2\le 0$, on $W$. For $\varepsilon >0$ define $u_\varepsilon $ on $W$ by

\[ u_\varepsilon =u+\varepsilon \log |f|^2\quad \text{on }W\setminus S,\qquad u_\varepsilon =-\infty \quad \text{on }S\cap W. \]

Step 1: $u_\varepsilon \in \mathrm{PSH}(W)$. At a point $x\in S\cap W$ we have $f(x)=0$, so $\log |f(y)|^2\to -\infty $ as $y\to x$, while $u$ is bounded above near $x$; hence $u_\varepsilon (y)\to -\infty $ as $y\to x$, and $u_\varepsilon $ is upper semicontinuous at $x$. On $W\setminus S$, $u_\varepsilon $ is upper semicontinuous as a sum of upper semicontinuous functions.

Now let $\Lambda $ be a connected component of $L\cap W$ for an affine complex line $L$. If $f$ vanishes identically on $\Lambda $, then $u_\varepsilon \equiv -\infty $ on $\Lambda $. Otherwise the zeros of $f$ on the connected set $\Lambda $ are isolated, by the identity theorem in one variable. Since $S$ is relatively closed, $A=S\cap \Lambda $ is therefore a closed discrete subset of $\Lambda $, hence countable, because each of the countably many compact sets exhausting $\Lambda $ contains only finitely many of its points. It is polar, because points are polar in the plane and countable unions of polar sets are polar by proposition 4.2.3.

Each connected component of $\Lambda \setminus A$ is a connected subset of $L\cap (\Omega \setminus S)$ and therefore lies in a connected component of this line section, on which the restriction of $u$ is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $. Hence the restriction of $u$ to $\Lambda \setminus A$ is subharmonic, the constant $-\infty $ being subharmonic. The function $\log |f|^2$ is subharmonic on $\Lambda $ by example 3.2.4. Thus $u_\varepsilon $ is subharmonic on $\Lambda \setminus A$, and it is bounded above there by $\sup _{W\setminus S}u$, because $\log |f|^2\leq 0$. By theorem 3.5.2, it has a subharmonic extension to $\Lambda $ whose value at $x\in A$ is $\varlimsup _{\Lambda \setminus A\ni y\to x}u_\varepsilon (y)=-\infty $, which is the value of $u_\varepsilon $ at $x$. Hence the restriction of $u_\varepsilon $ to $\Lambda $ is subharmonic. Finally, $u_\varepsilon $ is not identically $-\infty $, because $u$ is finite almost everywhere on $W\setminus S$ and $\{ f=0\} $ is Lebesgue null by lemma 7.4.1. Thus $u_\varepsilon \in \mathrm{PSH}(W)$.

Step 2: the extension. As $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ the functions $u_\varepsilon $ increase, because $\log |f|^2\leq 0$, and they are bounded above on $W$ by $\sup _{W\setminus S}u$. Let $F=\sup _{\varepsilon >0}u_\varepsilon $. By theorem 8.3.2, $F^*\in \mathrm{PSH}(W)$ and $F^*=F$ almost everywhere. Since $F=u$ outside the null set $\{ f=0\} $, we get $F^*=u$ almost everywhere on $W\setminus S$; both functions are locally integrable and subharmonic there, so $F^*=u$ everywhere on $W\setminus S$ by theorem 3.2.2.

It remains to show that $F^*=\widetilde u$ on $S\cap W$, which is Lebesgue null as a subset of $\{ f=0\} $. Let $x\in S\cap W$. Since $F^*=u$ on $W\setminus S$, the upper semicontinuity of $F^*$ gives $\widetilde u(x)=\varlimsup _{W\setminus S\ni y\to x}u(y)\leq F^*(x)$. Conversely, for small $r>0$ the ball submean inequality for $F^*$ gives $F^*(x)\leq |B(x,r)|^{-1}\int _{B(x,r)\setminus S}F^*\, \mathrm dV\leq \sup _{B(x,r)\setminus S}u$, because removing the null set $S$ does not change the integral, and letting $r\to 0$ gives $F^*(x)\leq \widetilde u(x)$. Hence $\widetilde u=F^*$ on $W$, and $\widetilde u$ is plurisubharmonic on $W$.

Uniqueness. The set $S$ is Lebesgue null, since it is covered by countably many neighborhoods $W$ as in the hypothesis and $S\cap W\subset \{ f=0\} $ is null. Two plurisubharmonic extensions of $u$ therefore agree almost everywhere, and hence everywhere by theorem 8.2.1. $\square$

For the second criterion we measure the size of $S$ by Hausdorff measure. Let $q>0$. A subset $E$ of a metric space is $\mathcal H^ q$-null if for all $\delta ,\eta >0$ it can be covered by countably many balls of radii $r_ l<\delta $ with $\sum _ l r_ l^ q<\eta $. This means that the $q$-dimensional Hausdorff measure of $E$, which is defined by coverings with sets of small diameter, vanishes, whatever its normalization: a set of diameter $\ell $ lies in the ball of radius $\ell '$ about any of its points for every $\ell '>\ell $, and a ball of radius $r$ has diameter at most $2r$. Every subset of an $\mathcal H^ q$-null set is $\mathcal H^ q$-null, and so is every countable union of $\mathcal H^ q$-null sets $E_1,E_2,\ldots $: cover $E_ l$ by balls of radii less than $\delta $ with sum of the $q$-th powers of the radii less than $2^{-l}\eta $.

We use two further elementary properties. First, if $E$ is $\mathcal H^ q$-null and $\Phi $ is a Lipschitz map with constant $L>0$ from $E$ to a metric space, then $\Phi (E)$ is $\mathcal H^ q$-null. To see this, discard the covering balls that do not meet $E$, and replace each remaining ball $B(c,r)$ by $B(c',2r)$ with $c'\in E\cap B(c,r)$; then $\Phi (E\cap B(c',2r))\subset B(\Phi (c'),2Lr)$, so $\Phi (E)$ is covered by balls of radii $2Lr_ l<2L\delta $ with $\sum _ l(2Lr_ l)^ q<(2L)^ q\eta $. Second, if $q\leq b$, every $\mathcal H^ q$-null subset of $\mathbb R^ b$ is Lebesgue null, because the total volume of the covering balls is at most a constant times $\sum _ lr_ l^ b\leq \delta ^{b-q}\sum _ l r_ l^ q$.

For $n\geq 2$, let $\sigma $ be the unitarily invariant probability measure on $\mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ and $d$ the metric on $\mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ introduced in section 6.3. For $v\in \mathbb {C}^ n\setminus \{ 0\} $ we write $[v]\in \mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ for the complex line spanned by $v$; thus the restriction of $v\mapsto [v]$ to the unit sphere is the Hopf map of section 6.3. For $y\neq x$ in $\mathbb {C}^ n$, the point $[y-x]$ is the direction of $y$ from $x$.

Lemma 8.4.2. Let $n\ge 2$ and let $K\subset \mathbb {C}^ n$ be a compact $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null set. Then:

  1. $K$ is real polar;

  2. for every $x\in \mathbb {C}^ n$, the set of directions from $x$ of the points of $K\setminus \{ x\} $ has $\sigma $-measure zero;

  3. the orthogonal projection of $K$ onto any complex hyperplane is Lebesgue null.

Proof. (1) Suppose that $K$ is not polar. By theorem 4.3.3, $K$ carries a nonzero positive measure of finite energy; dividing it by its mass, we obtain a probability measure $\mu $ on $K$ of finite energy $I(\mu )$ for the Newtonian kernel $k(x,y)=|x-y|^{2-2n}/((2n-2)s_{2n-1})$ of section 4.1 in $\mathbb R^{2n}$. Its potential $U^\mu $ is lower semicontinuous, and it is finite $\mu $-almost everywhere because $\int U^\mu \, \mathrm d\mu =I(\mu )<\infty $. The sets $E_ M=K\cap \{ U^\mu \le M\} $, $M\in \mathbb N$, are compact, because $U^\mu $ is lower semicontinuous, and they increase to a set of full $\mu $-measure; hence $\mu (E_ M)>0$ for $M$ large enough. Let $\nu $ be the restriction of $\mu $ to $E_ M$. For $a\in E_ M$ and $r>0$,

\[ \frac{r^{2-2n}\, \nu (B(a,r))}{(2n-2)s_{2n-1}}\leq \int _{B(a,r)}k(a,y)\, \mathrm d\nu (y)\leq U^\mu (a)\leq M, \]

so $\nu (B(a,r))\le C_ nMr^{2n-2}$ with $C_ n=(2n-2)s_{2n-1}$. Now cover $E_ M$ by balls $B(c_ l,r_ l)$ with $r_ l<1$ and $\sum _ lr_ l^{2n-3}<\eta $, and discard those not meeting $E_ M$. Each remaining ball lies in $B(a_ l,2r_ l)$ for some $a_ l\in E_ M$, so

\[ \nu (E_ M)\leq \sum _ l\nu (B(a_ l,2r_ l))\leq 2^{2n-2}C_ nM\sum _ lr_ l^{2n-2}\leq 2^{2n-2}C_ nM\eta , \]

where we used $r_ l<1$. Since $\eta >0$ is arbitrary, $\nu (E_ M)=0$, a contradiction. Hence $K$ is polar.

(2) Fix $x$. For $m\geq 1$ the set $K_ m=K\cap \{ |y-x|\geq 1/m\} $ is compact and $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null, and $K\setminus \{ x\} =\bigcup _ mK_ m$. The direction map $\Phi (y)=[y-x]$ is Lipschitz on $K_ m$. Indeed, $\Phi (y)=[(y-x)/|y-x|]$. The normalization $y\mapsto (y-x)/|y-x|$ has Lipschitz constant at most $2m$ on $\{ |y-x|\geq 1/m\} $: for nonzero vectors $a,b$,

\[ \Bigl |\frac a{|a|}-\frac b{|b|}\Bigr |\leq \frac{|a-b|}{|a|}+|b|\, \Bigl |\frac1{|a|}-\frac1{|b|}\Bigr |=\frac{|a-b|+\bigl ||b|-|a|\bigr |}{|a|}\leq \frac{2|a-b|}{|a|}, \]

and we apply this with $a=y-x$, $b=y'-x$ and $|a|\geq 1/m$. The Hopf map is Lipschitz with constant $\pi /2$ for the metric $d$. Indeed, let $e,e'$ be unit vectors and $\delta =\arccos \operatorname{Re}\langle e,e'\rangle \in [0,\pi ]$ their spherical distance. Since $d$ is the minimum of the spherical distance over the unit representatives (section 6.3), we have $d([e],[e'])\leq \delta $. Moreover $|e-e'|^2=2-2\cos \delta =4\sin ^2(\delta /2)$, and $\sin s\geq 2s/\pi $ for $0\leq s\leq \pi /2$, so $|e-e'|\geq 2\delta /\pi $. Hence

\[ d([e],[e'])\leq \delta \leq \tfrac \pi 2|e-e'|. \]

Since Lipschitz images of $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null sets are $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null, the compact set $\Phi (K_ m)$ is $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null in $(\mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C),d)$. Cover it by balls of radii $r_ l<1$ with $\sum _ lr_ l^{2n-3}<\eta $. By lemma 6.3.2, and since $r_ l<1$, we have $\sigma (\Phi (K_ m))\leq \sum _ lr_ l^{2n-2}<\eta $, so $\sigma (\Phi (K_ m))=0$. Hence the set of directions $\Phi (K\setminus \{ x\} )=\bigcup _ m\Phi (K_ m)$ is a Borel set of $\sigma $-measure zero.

(3) An orthogonal projection onto a complex hyperplane $H\cong \mathbb R^{2n-2}$ is $1$-Lipschitz. Hence the projection of $K$ is $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null in $H$, and therefore Lebesgue null because $2n-3<2n-2$. $\square$

Theorem 8.4.3. Let $n\ge 2$, let $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ n$ be a domain, and let $S\subset \Omega $ be relatively closed and locally $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null, that is, every point of $S$ has a neighborhood $W$ such that $S\cap W$ is $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null. Then every plurisubharmonic function on $\Omega \setminus S$ extends uniquely to a plurisubharmonic function on $\Omega $, given on $S$ by the same upper-limit formula as in theorem 8.4.1.

Proof. Let $u$ be a plurisubharmonic function on $\Omega \setminus S$. For every closed ball $\overline B\subset \Omega $, the compact set $S\cap \overline B$ is covered by finitely many neighborhoods $W$ as in the hypothesis, and is therefore $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null.

Step 1: $u$ is locally bounded above near $S$. Fix $p\in S$ and $r>0$ with $\overline B=\overline{B(p,r)}\subset \Omega $, and write $B=B(p,r)$. By lemma 8.4.2(2) applied with $x=p$, almost every complex line through $p$ meets $S\cap \overline B$ only at $p$; fix such a line $L=\{ p+te:t\in \mathbb {C}\} $, with $|e|=1$. The circle $\Gamma =L\cap \partial B$ is a compact subset of $\Omega \setminus S$, so the upper semicontinuous function $u$ is bounded above on $\Gamma $ by a finite constant $C$, and the open set $O=\{ y\in \Omega \setminus S:u(y)<C+1\} $ contains $\Gamma $.

Suppose that $u$ is not bounded above near $p$. Then there are points $x_ j\in B\setminus S$ with $x_ j\to p$ and $u(x_ j)\to +\infty $. For each $j$, lemma 8.4.2(2) applied with $x=x_ j$ shows that the directions of the lines through $x_ j$ meeting $S\cap \overline B$ form a $\sigma $-null set. Every nonempty open ball in $\mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ has positive $\sigma $-measure by lemma 6.3.2, so the complement of this null set is dense. We can therefore choose directions $\xi _ j$ outside this null set with $d(\xi _ j,[e])<1/j$. Since $d$ is the minimum of the spherical distance over the unit representatives, $\xi _ j$ has a unit representative $e_ j$ whose spherical distance to $e$ is less than $1/j$, and then $|e_ j-e|<1/j$, because a chord is shorter than the arc. Thus $e_ j\to e$, and the lines $L_ j=\{ x_ j+te_ j:t\in \mathbb {C}\} $ do not meet $S\cap \overline B$.

We claim that $L_ j\cap \partial B\subset O$ for all large $j$. Let $c_ j$ be the point of $L_ j$ closest to $p$, that is, the orthogonal projection of $p$ to $L_ j$; then $|c_ j-p|\leq |x_ j-p|\to 0$. Since $L_ j$ is a complex line, $c_ j=x_ j+\langle p-x_ j,e_ j\rangle e_ j$ and $\langle c_ j-p,e_ j\rangle =0$ for the Hermitian inner product, so $\operatorname{Re}\langle c_ j-p,se_ j\rangle =0$ and $|c_ j+se_ j-p|^2=|c_ j-p|^2+|s|^2$ for $s\in \mathbb {C}$. Hence $L_ j\cap \partial B$ is the circle $\{ c_ j+\rho _ j\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta }e_ j\} $ with $\rho _ j=(r^2-|c_ j-p|^2)^{1/2}\to r$. Since $|c_ j+\rho _ j\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta }e_ j-p-r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta }e|\leq |c_ j-p|+|\rho _ j-r|+r|e_ j-e|$, these circles converge uniformly in $\theta $ to $\Gamma =\{ p+r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta }e\} $. The distance from the compact set $\Gamma $ to the closed complement of the open set $O$ is positive, so the circles lie in $O$ for large $j$.

For such $j$, the closed disc $\Delta _ j=L_ j\cap \overline B$ lies in $\Omega \setminus S$, and the restriction of $u$ to the connected component of $L_ j\cap (\Omega \setminus S)$ containing $\Delta _ j$ is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $. Its values on the boundary circle of $\Delta _ j$ are less than $C+1$, so the maximum principle, that is, theorem 3.1.3 with the constant harmonic function $C+1$, gives $u\leq C+1$ on $\Delta _ j$. Since $x_ j\in B\cap L_ j\subset \Delta _ j$, this gives $u(x_ j)\leq C+1$, contradicting $u(x_ j)\to +\infty $. Hence $u$ is bounded above near every point of $S$.

Step 2: the subharmonic extension. By lemma 8.4.2(1), every compact set $S\cap \overline B$ as above is real polar. Countably many such sets cover $S$, so $S$ is real polar by proposition 4.2.3. By Step 1 and the upper semicontinuity of $u$ on $\Omega \setminus S$, the function $u$ is bounded above on $K\setminus S$ for every compact $K\subset \Omega $. Moreover $u$ is subharmonic on $\Omega \setminus S$ by theorem 8.2.1. By theorem 3.5.2, $u$ has a subharmonic extension $\widetilde u$ to $\Omega $, given on $S$ by the upper-limit formula. Since $S$ is Lebesgue null and $u$ is finite almost everywhere on $\Omega \setminus S$, the function $\widetilde u$ is not identically $-\infty $ on the domain $\Omega $, so it is locally integrable.

Step 3: $\widetilde u$ is plurisubharmonic. We verify the hypothesis of lemma 8.1.2 for the locally integrable function $\widetilde u$. Fix a unit vector $a\in \mathbb {C}^ n$ and a nonnegative $\chi \in C^\infty _ c(\Omega )$, and write $z=v+ta$ with $v\in (\mathbb {C}a)^\perp $ and $t\in \mathbb {C}$, as in that lemma. The compact set $S\cap \operatorname{Supp}\chi $ is $\mathcal H^{2n-3}$-null, since it is covered by finitely many of the sets $S\cap \overline B$ of the first paragraph, so by lemma 8.4.2(3) its orthogonal projection onto $(\mathbb {C}a)^\perp $ is Lebesgue null. For $v$ outside this projection, the line $\{ v+ta:t\in \mathbb {C}\} $ meets $\operatorname{Supp}\chi $ only in $\Omega \setminus S$. Thus the open set $G_ v=\{ t:v+ta\in \Omega \setminus S\} $ contains $K_ v=\{ t:v+ta\in \operatorname{Supp}\chi \} $, and its connected components are the connected components of an affine complex-line section of $\Omega \setminus S$, on each of which the restriction of $\widetilde u=u$ is subharmonic or identically $-\infty $. As $a$ and $\chi $ are arbitrary, lemma 8.1.2 gives $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\widetilde u\geq 0$. Since $\widetilde u$ is a locally integrable subharmonic function, it is the canonical representative of its class, and it is plurisubharmonic by theorem 8.2.1. Uniqueness follows from the same theorem, since two plurisubharmonic extensions agree outside the Lebesgue null set $S$. $\square$

8.5 Kiselman's minimum principle

An infimum of plurisubharmonic functions need not be plurisubharmonic. For instance, the function $v(z,w)=|w|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(zw)=|w+\bar z|^2-|z|^2$ is plurisubharmonic on $\mathbb {C}^2$, since $2\operatorname{Re}(zw)$ is pluriharmonic, but $\inf _{w\in \mathbb {C}}v(z,w)=-|z|^2$ is not. Kiselman's minimum principle shows that, under convexity assumptions on the domain, the infimum is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $ if it is taken only over the real parts of some variables and the function does not depend on their imaginary parts. The reason is visible for a smooth function $u(z,w)$ that depends only on $z$ and $\operatorname{Re}w$. Its complex Hessian in $w$ is a quarter of its real Hessian in $\operatorname{Re}w$, and at a nondegenerate minimum over $\operatorname{Re}w$, the complex Hessian of the infimum is the Schur complement of the $w$-block in the complex Hessian of $u$, which is positive semidefinite.

Throughout this section, points of $\mathbb {C}^ m\times \mathbb {C}^ k$ are written $(z,w)$, and $w=t+\mathrm{i}s$ with $t=\operatorname{Re}w$ and $s=\operatorname{Im}w$ in $\mathbb R^ k$. A set $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ m\times \mathbb {C}^ k$ is invariant under imaginary translations if $(z,w+\mathrm{i}s')\in \Omega $ for all $(z,w)\in \Omega $ and $s'\in \mathbb R^ k$. A function $u$ on such a set is invariant under imaginary translations if $u(z,w+\mathrm{i}s')=u(z,w)$ for all such $(z,w)$ and $s'$, that is, if $u(z,w)$ depends only on $z$ and $\operatorname{Re}w$. For such $\Omega $ and $u$, we identify $t\in \mathbb R^ k$ with $t+\mathrm{i}0\in \mathbb {C}^ k$, so that $u(z,t)$ is defined. We write

\[ V_ z=\{ t\in \mathbb R^ k:(z,t)\in \Omega \} ,\qquad \Omega _{\mathbb R}=\{ (z,t)\in \mathbb {C}^ m\times \mathbb R^ k:t\in V_ z\} \]

for the real fiber of $\Omega $ over $z\in \mathbb {C}^ m$ and the real slice of $\Omega $, and $p(\Omega )=\{ z:V_ z\neq \varnothing \} $ for the projection of $\Omega $ to $\mathbb {C}^ m$; it is the image of $\Omega $ under $(z,w)\mapsto z$, because $(z,\operatorname{Re}w)\in \Omega $ for every $(z,w)\in \Omega $ by invariance. Since this projection is continuous and open, $p(\Omega )$ is open if $\Omega $ is open, and connected if $\Omega $ is connected.

Theorem 8.5.1 (Kiselman's minimum principle). Let $m,k\ge 1$, and let $\Omega \subset \mathbb {C}^ m\times \mathbb {C}^ k$ be a domain invariant under imaginary translations whose nonempty real fibers $V_ z$ are convex. Assume that there is a plurisubharmonic function $\psi $ on $\Omega $, invariant under imaginary translations, such that for every $c\in \mathbb R$ the set

\[ \{ (z,t)\in \Omega _{\mathbb R}:\psi (z,t)\le c\} \]

is compact. If $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$ is invariant under imaginary translations, then the function

\[ v(z)=\inf _{t\in V_ z}u(z,t) \]

is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on $p(\Omega )$.

Proof. We first treat smooth functions with positive definite real Hessian in $t$ that tend to $+\infty $ at the boundary of the real fibers (Step 1). We then approximate $u$ by such functions on subdomains exhausting $\Omega $ (Step 2) and pass to the limit (Step 3).

Step 1: a smooth case. In this step, $\Omega $ may be any open set invariant under imaginary translations whose real fibers are convex. Suppose that $u$ is smooth and plurisubharmonic, invariant under imaginary translations, and that for every $z\in p(\Omega )$ the function $t\mapsto u(z,t)$ has positive definite real Hessian on $V_ z$ and tends to $+\infty $ at the boundary of $V_ z$ and, if $V_ z$ is unbounded, as $|t|\to \infty $. We show that $v$ is plurisubharmonic on each connected component of $p(\Omega )$.

Fix $z\in p(\Omega )$ and put $f(t)=u(z,t)$ for $t\in V_ z$. Since its Hessian is positive definite, $f$ is strictly convex on every segment in the open convex set $V_ z$, hence on $V_ z$. Its sublevel sets $\{ t\in V_ z:f(t)\leq c\} $ are compact: they are bounded, because $f\to +\infty $ as $|t|\to \infty $ when $V_ z$ is unbounded, and the limit in $\mathbb R^ k$ of a convergent sequence in such a set cannot lie on the boundary of $V_ z$, because $f\to +\infty $ there, so it lies in $V_ z$ and, since $f$ is continuous on $V_ z$, in the sublevel set. Hence $f$ attains its minimum on the nonempty set $V_ z$, and by strict convexity at a unique point $g(z)$.

This point is the only critical point of $f$: if $\nabla f(t_1)=0$, then the convex function $s\mapsto f(t_1+s(t_2-t_1))$ has derivative zero at $s=0$, hence $f(t_2)\geq f(t_1)$ for every $t_2\in V_ z$, and $t_1=g(z)$. Fix $z_0\in p(\Omega )$. Since the $t$-Hessian is invertible, the implicit function theorem gives a smooth solution $t=G(z)$ of $\partial _ tu(z,t)=0$ near $z_0$ with $G(z_0)=g(z_0)$, and $G=g$ near $z_0$ because $g(z)$ is the only critical point. Hence $g$ and $v(z)=u(z,g(z))$ are smooth on $p(\Omega )$.

At the point $(z,g(z))$, let $H=(\partial ^2u/\partial t_ a\partial t_ b)$, $B=(\partial ^2u/\partial z_ j\partial t_ a)$ and $C=(\partial ^2u/\partial z_ j\partial \bar z_ l)$. Thus $H$ is a real symmetric positive definite $k\times k$ matrix and $B$ is a complex $m\times k$ matrix; since $u$ is real, $(\partial ^2u/\partial t_ a\partial \bar z_ j)=B^*$, the conjugate transpose of $B$. Differentiating the identity $\partial _ tu(z,g(z))=0$ with respect to $\bar z$ gives $B^*+Hg_{\bar z}=0$, where $g_{\bar z}=(\partial g_ a/\partial \bar z_ j)$; hence $g_{\bar z}=-H^{-1}B^*$. By the chain rule and the same identity, $v_{z_ j}(z)=u_{z_ j}(z,g(z))$, and differentiating once more with respect to $\bar z$ gives

\[ v_{z\bar z}=C+Bg_{\bar z}=C-BH^{-1}B^*. \]

On the other hand, for functions of $z$ and $\operatorname{Re}w$ we have $\partial _{w_ a}=\partial _{\bar w_ a}=\frac12\partial _{t_ a}$. Hence the complex Hessian of $u$ with respect to $(z,w)$, which is positive semidefinite by proposition 8.2.2(1), is

\[ \begin{pmatrix} C & B/2 \\ B^*/2 & H/4 \end{pmatrix}\ge 0. \]

Evaluating the associated Hermitian quadratic form at $(\xi ,-2H^{-1}B^*\xi )$, $\xi \in \mathbb {C}^ m$, gives $\xi ^*(C-BH^{-1}B^*)\xi \geq 0$. Thus $v_{z\bar z}\geq 0$, and the smooth function $v$ is plurisubharmonic on each component of $p(\Omega )$ by proposition 8.2.2(1).

Step 2: regularization. Now let $u$ be as in the theorem. For $\varepsilon >0$ let $u_\varepsilon =u*\rho _\varepsilon $ and $\psi _\varepsilon =\psi *\rho _\varepsilon $ on $\Omega _\varepsilon =\{ x\in \Omega :\operatorname {dist}(x,\mathbb {C}^{m+k}\setminus \Omega )>\varepsilon \} $, where $\rho _\varepsilon $ is the radial mollifier of section 1.4. Imaginary translations are isometries preserving $\Omega $, and convolution commutes with translations, so $\Omega _\varepsilon $, $u_\varepsilon $ and $\psi _\varepsilon $ are invariant under imaginary translations. By proposition 8.2.2, the functions $u_\varepsilon $ and $\psi _\varepsilon $ are smooth and plurisubharmonic on each connected component of $\Omega _\varepsilon $, so their complex Hessians are positive semidefinite at every point, and they decrease to $u$ and $\psi $ as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$; in particular $u_\varepsilon \geq u$ and $\psi _\varepsilon \geq \psi $.

The real fibers of $\Omega _\varepsilon $ are convex. Indeed, $(z,t)\in \Omega _\varepsilon $ if and only if the closed ball of radius $\varepsilon $ about $(z,t)$ lies in $\Omega $ (if it does, its distance to the closed complement of $\Omega $ is positive, because it is compact); by invariance this means that $(z+y,t+y')\in \Omega _{\mathbb R}$ for all $(y,y')\in \mathbb {C}^ m\times \mathbb R^ k$ with $|y|^2+|y'|^2\leq \varepsilon ^2$. Hence the real fiber of $\Omega _\varepsilon $ over $z$ is the intersection of the convex sets $V_{z+y}-y'$ over these $(y,y')$. Moreover, on each real fiber of $\Omega _\varepsilon $ the function $t\mapsto \psi _\varepsilon (z,t)$ is convex, since by invariance its real Hessian is four times the complex Hessian of $\psi _\varepsilon $ in $w$, which is positive semidefinite, and a smooth function with positive semidefinite Hessian on an open convex set is convex along every segment.

For $c\in \mathbb R$ let $K_ c=\{ (z,t)\in \Omega _{\mathbb R}:\psi (z,t)\le c\} $; it is a compact subset of $\Omega $. Choose $\varepsilon _ j\downarrow 0$, $j\geq 1$, such that $\operatorname {dist}(K_{j+1},\mathbb {C}^{m+k}\setminus \Omega )>4\varepsilon _ j$ whenever $K_{j+1}\neq \varnothing $. Since $\psi $ and the distance to the complement of $\Omega $ are invariant under imaginary translations, the same bound holds for the set $\{ x\in \Omega :\psi (x)\leq j+1\} =K_{j+1}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb R^ k$. Define

\[ G_ j=\{ (z,w)\in \Omega _{\varepsilon _ j}:\psi _{\varepsilon _ j}(z,w)<j\} ,\qquad U_ j=u_{\varepsilon _ j}+\frac{|\operatorname{Re}w|^2}{j} +\frac{1}{j-\psi _{\varepsilon _ j}}\quad \text{on }G_ j. \]

The open set $G_ j$ is invariant under imaginary translations, and its real fiber over $z$ is a sublevel set of the convex function $\psi _{\varepsilon _ j}(z,\, \cdot \, )$ on the convex real fiber of $\Omega _{\varepsilon _ j}$, hence convex. Since $\psi \leq \psi _{\varepsilon _ j}$, the real slice of $G_ j$ is contained in the compact set $K_ j\subset K_{j+1}$, which lies in $\Omega _{\varepsilon _ j}$ at positive distance from its complement. Consequently, for each $z$, the closure of the real fiber of $G_ j$ over $z$ is a compact subset of the real fiber of $\Omega _{\varepsilon _ j}$, and $\psi _{\varepsilon _ j}(z,\, \cdot \, )=j$ on its boundary.

The function $U_ j$ is smooth, plurisubharmonic and invariant under imaginary translations. Indeed, by the chain rule the complex Hessian of $1/(j-\psi _{\varepsilon _ j})$ has the entries

\[ \frac{(\psi _{\varepsilon _ j})_{a\bar b}}{(j-\psi _{\varepsilon _ j})^2} +\frac{2(\psi _{\varepsilon _ j})_ a(\psi _{\varepsilon _ j})_{\bar b}}{(j-\psi _{\varepsilon _ j})^3}, \]

where the subscripts denote derivatives with respect to the coordinates of $(z,w)$. Since $j-\psi _{\varepsilon _ j}>0$ on $G_ j$ and $(\psi _{\varepsilon _ j})_{\bar b}=\overline{(\psi _{\varepsilon _ j})_ b}$, this matrix is the sum of positive multiples of the complex Hessian of $\psi _{\varepsilon _ j}$ and of a positive semidefinite matrix of rank at most one, hence positive semidefinite. Moreover, $|\operatorname{Re}w|^2$ has the positive semidefinite complex Hessian in $(z,w)$ given by the diagonal matrix with entries $0$ in the $z$-directions and $\frac12$ in the $w$-directions. Hence $U_ j$ is plurisubharmonic on each connected component of $G_ j$, by proposition 8.2.2(1).

The real $t$-Hessian of $U_ j$ is positive definite: the $t$-Hessians of $u_{\varepsilon _ j}$ and $1/(j-\psi _{\varepsilon _ j})$ are positive semidefinite, as for $\psi _\varepsilon $ above, and that of $|t|^2/j$ is $(2/j)I$. On each real fiber of $G_ j$, which is bounded, $U_ j(z,t)\to +\infty $ at the boundary, because $1/(j-\psi _{\varepsilon _ j})\to +\infty $ there while the other two terms are bounded below on the compact closure of the fiber. Hence Step 1 applies to $U_ j$ on $G_ j$, and

\[ v_ j(z)=\inf \{ U_ j(z,t):(z,t)\in G_ j\} \]

is plurisubharmonic on each connected component of $p(G_ j)$.

Step 3: passage to the limit. The sets $G_ j$ increase to $\Omega $. Indeed, $\varepsilon _{j+1}\leq \varepsilon _ j$ gives $\Omega _{\varepsilon _ j}\subset \Omega _{\varepsilon _{j+1}}$ and $\psi _{\varepsilon _{j+1}}\leq \psi _{\varepsilon _ j}<j<j+1$ on $G_ j$. Every point $x\in \Omega $ lies in $\Omega _{\varepsilon _ j}$ for large $j$, and $\psi _{\varepsilon _ j}(x)\to \psi (x)<\infty $, so $x\in G_ j$ for large $j$. On $G_ j$ we have $U_{j+1}\leq U_ j$, since $u_{\varepsilon _{j+1}}\leq u_{\varepsilon _ j}$, $|\operatorname{Re}w|^2/(j+1)\leq |\operatorname{Re}w|^2/j$ and $0<j-\psi _{\varepsilon _ j}\leq j+1-\psi _{\varepsilon _{j+1}}$. Hence the functions $v_ j$ decrease on the increasing open sets $p(G_ j)$. They satisfy $v_ j\geq v$, since $U_ j\geq u_{\varepsilon _ j}\geq u$. Conversely, let $z\in p(\Omega )$ and $t\in V_ z$. Then $(z,t)\in G_ j$ for large $j$, and $U_ j(z,t)\to u(z,t)$, also when $u(z,t)=-\infty $, because $u_{\varepsilon _ j}(z,t)\to u(z,t)$ and the other two terms tend to zero. Hence $\lim _ jv_ j(z)\leq u(z,t)$ for every $t\in V_ z$, and therefore $v_ j\downarrow v$ on $p(\Omega )$.

Let $B'\Subset p(\Omega )$ be a ball. Since $\overline{B'}$ is compact and covered by the increasing open sets $p(G_ j)$, we have $B'\subset p(G_ j)$ for large $j$, and the $v_ j$ with such $j$ form a decreasing sequence of plurisubharmonic functions on $B'$. By theorem 8.3.1, $v$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on $B'$. Since every point of the connected open set $p(\Omega )$ lies in such a ball, lemma 8.2.4 shows that $v$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on $p(\Omega )$; this proves the theorem. $\square$

Corollary 8.5.2. Let $m,k\geq 1$, let $D\subset \mathbb {C}^ m$ be a domain and let $V\subset \mathbb {R}^ k$ be a nonempty open convex set. If $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(D\times (V+\mathrm{i}\mathbb {R}^ k))$ is invariant under imaginary translations, then the function $z\mapsto \inf _{t\in V}u(z,t)$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on $D$. The same holds for $z\mapsto \inf _{t\in V}(u(z,t)-\ell (t))$, for every real affine function $\ell $ on $\mathbb R^ k$.

Proof. It suffices to prove the assertion on every ball $B(z_0,r)\Subset D$: since $D$ is connected, lemma 8.2.4 then gives it on $D$. We construct a function $\psi $ as in theorem 8.5.1 for the domain $B(z_0,r)\times (V+\mathrm{i}\mathbb R^ k)$, which is invariant under imaginary translations and has real fibers $V$.

We first need a continuous convex function $b\geq 0$ on $V$ with compact sublevel sets. If $V=\mathbb R^ k$, take $b(t)=|t|^2$. Otherwise let $\delta (t)=\operatorname {dist}(t,\mathbb {R}^ k\setminus V)$, which is continuous and positive on $V$. It is concave on $V$: if the balls $B(t_1,r_1)$ and $B(t_2,r_2)$ lie in $V$ and $0\leq \lambda \leq 1$, then by convexity $V$ contains $\lambda B(t_1,r_1)+(1-\lambda )B(t_2,r_2)=B(\lambda t_1+(1-\lambda )t_2,\lambda r_1+(1-\lambda )r_2)$, so $\delta (\lambda t_1+(1-\lambda )t_2)\geq \lambda r_1+(1-\lambda )r_2$, and letting $r_ i\uparrow \delta (t_ i)$ gives concavity. Consequently $1/\delta $ is convex: since $x\mapsto 1/x$ is decreasing and convex on $(0,\infty )$,

\[ \frac1{\delta (\lambda t_1+(1-\lambda )t_2)}\leq \frac1{\lambda \delta (t_1)+(1-\lambda )\delta (t_2)}\leq \frac\lambda {\delta (t_1)}+\frac{1-\lambda }{\delta (t_2)}. \]

Put $b(t)=|t|^2+1/\delta (t)$. For $c>0$ the set $\{ b\leq c\} $ is closed in $V$ and contained in the compact set $\{ t:|t|^2\leq c,\ \delta (t)\geq 1/c\} \subset V$, hence compact.

A continuous convex function $f$ on $V$ defines a plurisubharmonic function $f(\operatorname{Re}w)$ on every domain of the form $D'\times (V+\mathrm{i}\mathbb R^ k)$. Indeed, $f(\operatorname{Re}w)$ is continuous, and its restriction to an affine complex line is $f\circ A$ for a real affine map $A$ from an open subset of $\mathbb {C}$ to $V$. If the closed disc of radius $r$ about $\zeta _0$ lies in the domain of $A$, then, since $A$ is affine, $A(\zeta _0)$ is the midpoint of $A(\zeta _0+r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta })$ and $A(\zeta _0-r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta })$ for every $\theta $, and the convexity of $f$ gives

\[ f(A(\zeta _0))\leq \tfrac 12\bigl (f(A(\zeta _0+r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta }))+f(A(\zeta _0-r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta }))\bigr ). \]

Averaging over $\theta \in [0,2\pi ]$, we obtain the submean inequality for the continuous function $f\circ A$ on this circle.

Now put $\psi (z,w)=(r^2-|z-z_0|^2)^{-1}+b(\operatorname{Re}w)$. This function is continuous, positive and invariant under imaginary translations. It is plurisubharmonic as a sum of two plurisubharmonic functions (proposition 8.2.5). The second term is plurisubharmonic by the preceding paragraph. For the first, $x\mapsto (r^2-x)^{-1}$ is convex and increasing on $(-\infty ,r^2)$ and $|z-z_0|^2$ has the complex Hessian $I$, so by the chain rule the complex Hessian of $(r^2-|z-z_0|^2)^{-1}$ is positive semidefinite, and proposition 8.2.2(1) applies. For $c>0$, the set $\{ (z,t)\in B(z_0,r)\times V:\psi (z,t)\leq c\} $ is closed in $B(z_0,r)\times V$ and, since both terms of $\psi $ are nonnegative, contained in the compact set $\{ |z-z_0|^2\leq r^2-1/c\} \times \{ b\leq c\} $; it is therefore compact, and for $c\leq 0$ it is empty. Hence theorem 8.5.1 applies on $B(z_0,r)\times (V+\mathrm{i}\mathbb R^ k)$, and shows that $\inf _{t\in V}u(z,t)$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty $ on $B(z_0,r)$.

Finally, if $\ell $ is real affine, then $\ell (\operatorname{Re}w)$ is the real part of a complex affine function of $w$. It is invariant under imaginary translations, and the first part applies to it. $\square$

1.1 The definition of plurisubharmonic functions

In this section, we recall the notion of plurisubharmonic functions. We will also take care of the $0$-dimensional case, which makes a number of induction arguments easier to carry out. None of our references treats the $0$-dimensional case, but the reader can easily verify that the results in this section hold in this exceptional case.

1.1.1 The -dimensional case

Let $\Omega $ be a domain (a connected open subset) in $\mathbb {C}$.

Definition 1.1.1. A subharmonic function on $\Omega $ is a function $\varphi \colon \Omega \rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ satisfying the following three conditions:

  1. $\varphi \not\equiv -\infty $;

  2. $\varphi $ is upper semicontinuous;

  3. $\varphi $ satisfies the sub-mean value inequality: For any $a\in \Omega $ and $r>0$ such that $\overline{B_1(a,r)}\subseteq \Omega $, we have

    \[ \varphi (a)\leq \frac{1}{2\pi }\int _0^{2\pi } \varphi (a+r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta })\, \mathrm{d}\theta .\footnotemark \]
Note. Condition 2 guarantees that $\varphi $ is measurable and locally bounded from above, and hence the integral in Condition 3 makes sense.

We will denote the set of subharmonic functions on $\Omega $ by $\mathrm{SH}(\Omega )$.

Here, $B_1(a,r)\coloneqq \{ z\in \mathbb {C}:|z-a|<r\} $ denotes the open ball with center $a$ and radius $r$.

In fact, for each $a\in \Omega $, in 3, it suffices to require the sub-mean value inequality for all small enough $r>0$.

Intuitively, at a specific point $a\in \Omega $, Condition 2 says $\varphi (a)$ is no smaller than the upper limit of nearby values, while Condition 3 says $\varphi (a)$ is no larger than the mean. This intuition leads to the following rigidity theorem:

Theorem 1.1.2. Let $\varphi \colon \Omega \rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ be a measurable function. Then the following are equivalent:

  1. $\varphi $ is locally integrable and $\Delta \varphi \geq 0$.

  2. $\varphi $ coincides almost everywhere with a subharmonic function $\psi $ on $\Omega $.

Moreover, the subharmonic function $\psi $ in 2 is unique.

Proof. Suppose first that $\varphi\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$ and $\Delta\varphi\geq0$. Apply the distributional characterization of subharmonic functions to the distribution defined by $\varphi$. It gives a subharmonic representative $\psi$ of this distribution. Equality of the distributions defined by two locally integrable functions is equality almost everywhere, so $\varphi=\psi$ almost everywhere.

Conversely, a subharmonic function on the connected domain $\Omega$ is locally integrable and has positive distributional Laplacian by the same theorem. Replacing it on a null set changes neither its distribution nor its local integrability. Finally, two subharmonic representatives that agree almost everywhere agree at every point: their values are the limits of their averages over shrinking balls. This also proves uniqueness. Multiplying the usual planar Laplacian by a fixed positive normalization constant does not change positivity.

Here in Condition 1, $\Delta \varphi =2\mathrm{i}\partial \bar\partial \varphi $ is the Laplacian in the sense of currents. This is a special case of theorem 1.1.6 below.

This theorem gives a very useful way of constructing subharmonic functions.

1.1.3 The manifold case

Let $X$ be a complex manifold. In the whole book, complex manifolds are assumed to be paracompact, namely, all connected components have countable bases.

Definition 1.1.10. A plurisubharmonic function on $X$ is a function $\varphi \colon X\rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ such that for any $x\in X$, there exists an open neighborhood $U$ of $x$ in $X$, an integer $n\in \mathbb {N}$, a domain $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb {C}^ n$ and a biholomorphic map $F\colon \Omega \rightarrow U$ such that $F^*(\varphi |_ U)\in \mathrm{PSH}(\Omega )$.

The set of plurisubharmonic functions on $X$ is denoted by $\mathrm{PSH}(X)$.

Example 1.1.11. When $X$ is a domain in $\mathbb {C}^ n$, the notions of plurisubharmonic functions in definition 1.1.10 and in definition 1.1.3 coincide.

Example 1.1.12. Write $\{ X_ i\} _{i\in I}$ for the set of connected components of $X$. Then we have a natural bijection

\[ \mathrm{PSH}(X) \cong \prod _{i\in I} \mathrm{PSH}(X_ i). \]

Here the product is in the category of sets. In particular, if $X=\varnothing $, then $\mathrm{PSH}(X)$ is a singleton.

This example allows us to reduce to the case of connected manifolds when studying general plurisubharmonic functions.

Proposition 1.1.13. Let $Y$ be a complex manifold and $f\colon Y\rightarrow X$ be a holomorphic map. Then for any $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$, exactly one of the following cases occurs:

  1. $f^*\varphi $ is identically $-\infty $ on some connected component of $Y$;

  2. $f^*\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(Y)$.

Proof. Work on one connected component $Y_0$ of $Y$. In coordinate neighborhoods of $Y$ and $X$, holomorphic pullback says that $\varphi\circ f$ is either plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty$. A plurisubharmonic function cannot be identically $-\infty$ on a nonempty open subset of a connected domain, by its local integrability. Thus a neighborhood on which the pullback is identically $-\infty$ cannot overlap a neighborhood on which it is plurisubharmonic. The two types form disjoint open subsets of $Y_0$; connectedness leaves only one type. If the first type never occurs on any component, the pullback is plurisubharmonic on $Y$. Zero-dimensional charts are covered by the finite-value convention.

This proposition follows from proposition 1.1.7 applied separately on each connected component of $Y$. theorem 1.1.6 implies immediately the general form of the rigidity theorem:

Theorem 1.1.14. Let $\varphi \colon X\rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ be a measurable function. Then the following are equivalent:

  1. $\varphi $ is locally integrable and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\varphi \geq 0$;

  2. $\varphi $ coincides almost everywhere with a plurisubharmonic function $\psi $ on $X$.

Moreover, the plurisubharmonic function $\psi $ in 2 is unique.

Proof. Restrict a locally integrable $\varphi$ with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm c}\varphi\geq0$ to each coordinate chart and apply the local representative theorem. The resulting plurisubharmonic representatives agree almost everywhere on overlaps, hence everywhere there by the uniqueness part of the same theorem. They therefore glue to a plurisubharmonic function on $X$ representing $\varphi$.

Conversely, local integrability and positivity hold in every chart for a plurisubharmonic function and are preserved by almost-everywhere equality. The same overlap argument proves uniqueness globally. On a zero-dimensional component all assertions reduce to finiteness at each point.

1.3 Plurifine topology

In this section, we introduce the notion of plurifine topology. Unlike other sections in this chapter, this section contains full details. This is mainly due to the unfortunate omissions and numerous minor problems in the foundational paper [BT87]. In particular, the key theorem theorem 1.3.9 was claimed by Bedford–Taylor without proof. Our presentation largely follows [EMW06]. We also include enough details so that this section is readable for those who are not familiar with the classical potential theory.

Very recently, El Kadiri–Fuglede published a book [EKF] about classical fine potential theory, containing essentially all results in this section. The interested reader may consult their book for further details.

1.3.1 Plurifine topology on domains

Let $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb {C}^ n$ ($n\in \mathbb {N}$) be a domain.

See Definition 10.3.1.

We want to distinguish the Euclidean topology from the plurifine topology. In the whole book, topological notions without adjectives refer to those with respect to the Euclidean topology. We include the symbol $\mathcal{F}$ in order to denote those with respect to the plurifine topology. For example, we will say $\mathcal{F}$-open subset, $\mathcal{F}$-neighborhood, $\mathcal{F}$-closure, etc. The $\mathcal{F}$-closure of a set $E\subseteq \Omega $ will be denoted by $\overline{E}^{\mathcal{F}}$.

Recall that in the whole book, we follow Bourbaki's convention: a neighborhood is not necessarily open. Similarly, an $\mathcal{F}$-neighborhood is not necessarily $\mathcal{F}$-open.

A priori, we should include $\Omega $ into the notations as well, but as we will see shortly in corollary 1.3.11, this is usually unnecessary.

When $n=0$, the domain $\Omega $ is a singleton and all assertions in this subsection are trivial. In the proofs below we therefore assume $n>0$.

Proof. It suffices to show that $\varphi |_{\{ \varphi \neq -\infty \} }$ is $\mathcal{F}$-continuous, since $\{ \varphi =-\infty \} $ is $\mathcal{F}$-closed.

The problem is local, so we may assume that $X\subseteq \mathbb {C}^ n$ is a domain and $\varphi =\psi +g$, where $\psi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$ and $g\in C^{\infty }(X)$ and $|g|\leq C$ for some $C>0$. For an open interval $(a,b)\subseteq \mathbb {R}$, it suffices to show that

\[ U\coloneqq \left\{ x\in X:a<\varphi (x)<b\right\} =\left\{ x\in X:a-g(x)<\psi (x)<b-g(x)\right\} \]

is $\mathcal{F}$-open. Take $x\in U$. We can find an open neighborhood $V$ of $x$ in $U$ such that

\[ \sup _{y\in V} \left( a-g(y)\right)<\psi (x)< \inf _{y\in V} \left( b-g(y) \right). \]

Therefore,

\[ \left\{ z\in V:\sup _{y\in V} \left(a-g(y)\right)<\psi (z)<\inf _{y\in V} \left(b-g(y)\right)\right\} \]

is an $\mathcal{F}$-open neighborhood of $x$ in $U$. We conclude that $U$ is $\mathcal{F}$-open. $\square$

Corollary 1.3.16. Let $\varphi ,\psi \in \mathrm{QPSH}(X)$. Then the set

\[ \{ x\in X: \varphi (x)>\psi (x)\} \]

is $\mathcal{F}$-open.

Proof. It suffices to show that for any $x\in X$ such that $\varphi (x)>\psi (x)$, the same holds on an $\mathcal{F}$-neighborhood $U$ of $x$. Observe that $\varphi (x)\neq -\infty $. If $\psi (x)\neq -\infty $, then it suffices to apply proposition 1.3.15. Otherwise, take

\[ U\coloneqq \{ y\in X: \varphi (y)>\varphi (x)-1\} \cap \{ y\in X: \psi (y)<\varphi (x)-1\} . \]

This is an $\mathcal{F}$-open neighborhood of $x$ contained in $\{ \varphi >\psi \} $. $\square$

Lemma 1.3.17. Let $Z\subseteq X$ be a pluripolar subset. Then

\[ \overline{X\setminus Z}^{\mathcal{F}}=X. \]

Proof. The problem is local, so we may assume that $X$ is a domain in $\mathbb {C}^ n$ and that there is $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$ with $Z\subseteq \{ \varphi =-\infty \} $. It suffices to show that $\{ \varphi >-\infty \} $ is $\mathcal{F}$-dense.

Let $x\in X$ be a point with $\varphi (x)=-\infty $ and $U\subseteq X$ be an $\mathcal{F}$-open neighborhood of $x$ in $X$. We need to show that $U\cap \{ \varphi >-\infty \} \neq \varnothing $.

Thanks to theorem 1.3.9, after shrinking $U$, we may assume that there is $\psi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$ such that $U=\{ \psi >0\} $. Observe that $U$ is not a pluripolar set: Otherwise, $\psi \leq 0$ almost everywhere by proposition 1.2.4, and hence everywhere by proposition 1.2.6. So $\varphi |_ U\not\equiv -\infty $. We conclude that $U\cap \{ \varphi >-\infty \} \neq \varnothing $, as desired. $\square$

Corollary 1.3.18. Let $\varphi ,\psi \in \mathrm{QPSH}(X)$. Set

\[ W=\left\{ x\in X:\varphi (x)=-\infty \right\} \textup{ or }W=\left\{ x\in X:\psi (x)=-\infty \right\} . \]

Then for any pluripolar set $Z\subseteq X$, we have

\[ \sup _{X\setminus W}(\varphi -\psi ) =\sup _{X\setminus (W\cup Z)}(\varphi -\psi ),\quad \inf _{X\setminus W}(\varphi -\psi ) =\inf _{X\setminus (W\cup Z)}(\varphi -\psi ). \]

In particular, taking $\psi =0$, we find that

\[ \sup _{X\setminus Z}\varphi =\sup _ X \varphi . \]

Proof. This is an immediate consequence of 1.3.17 and proposition 1.3.15. $\square$

In the literature about pluripotential theory, one often finds the careless expressions like $\sup _ X (\varphi -\psi )$. The issue is that $\varphi -\psi $ is not defined everywhere, and hence this expression does not make sense if you read it literally. corollary 1.3.18 tells you that you do not have to worry too much about the details on a pluripolar set. In other words, $\sup $ and $\inf $ could always be understood as a kind of essential supremum and essential infimum modulo pluripolar sets.

There is a convenient way to fix this issue in the literature — just replace the suprema by quasi-suprema:

Definition 1.3.19. Let $Z\subseteq X$ be a pluripolar set, and $f\colon X\setminus Z\rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty ]$ be a function. We define the quasi-supremum and the quasi-infimum of $f$ as follows:

\[ \begin{split} \operatorname *{q-sup}_ X f\coloneqq & \inf \left\{ \sup _{X\setminus W} f:W\supseteq Z, W \textup{ is pluripolar}\right\} ,\\ \operatorname *{q-inf}_ X f\coloneqq & \sup \left\{ \inf _{X\setminus W} f:W\supseteq Z, W \textup{ is pluripolar}\right\} . \end{split} \]
For two functions $f$ and $g$ equal quasi-everywhere, the quasi-suprema and quasi-infima of them are equal, as is clear from the definition.

For a quasi-plurisubharmonic function $\varphi $, we have

\[ \operatorname *{q-sup}_ X \varphi =\sup _ X \varphi , \]

as a consequence of corollary 1.3.18.

1.4 Lelong numbers and multiplier ideal sheaves

In this section, we briefly recall the notions of Lelong numbers and multiplier ideal sheaves. Our presentation is by no means intended to be complete. Readers are encouraged to read the textbooks [Section 2.3, GZ17] and [Dem12].

Let $X$ be a complex manifold.

Definition 1.4.1. Let $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$ and $x\in X$. The Lelong number $\nu (\varphi ,x)$ of $\varphi $ at $x$ is defined as follows: If $x$ is a point on a $0$-dimensional connected component of $X$, we set

\[ \nu (\varphi ,x)=0. \]

Otherwise, take an open neighborhood $U$ of $x$ in $X$ and a biholomorphic map $F\colon U\rightarrow \Omega $, where $\Omega $ is a domain in $\mathbb {C}^ n$. Then we define

1.24
\begin{equation} \nu (\varphi ,x)\coloneqq \sup \left\{ \gamma \in \mathbb {R}_{\geq 0}: \varphi (F^{-1}(y))\leq \gamma \log |y-F(x)|^2+\mathcal{O}(1)\textup{ as }y\to F(x) \right\} . \end{equation}
Observe that $\nu (\varphi ,x)$ does not depend on the choices of $U$ and $F$, see [Page 167, Corollary 7.2, DemBook]. Furthermore, it follows from proposition 1.4.3 below that the supremum in (1.24) is a maximum.

Remark 1.4.2. Our definition of the Lelong number is not standard. It differs from the standard definition by a factor of $2$. As a mnemonic, just remember

\[ \nu \left(\log |z|^2,0\right)=1 \quad (\text{instead of } 2). \]

Our convention of the Lelong numbers together with the convention of the multiplier ideal sheaves below make sure that (1.30) has no extra factors.

Proposition 1.4.3. Let $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(B_ n(0,1))$. Then

1.25
\begin{equation} \nu (\varphi ,0)=\lim _{r \to 0+} \frac{\sup _{B_ n(0,r)}\varphi }{\log r^2}\in [0,\infty ). \end{equation}

Proof. Note that (1.25) is trivial when $n=0$, so we may assume that $n>0$ in the proof.

It follows from proposition 1.1.8 that the limit in (1.25) exists and is finite. We shall denote the limit by $\nu '(\varphi ,0)$ for the time being.

We first observe that by proposition 1.1.8, for any fixed $r_1\in (0,1)$ there is a constant $C_{r_1}$ such that

1.26
\begin{equation} \varphi (x)\leq \nu '(\varphi ,0)\log |x|^2+C_{r_1} \end{equation}

when $x\in B_ n(0,r_1)\setminus \{ 0\} $. In particular, $\nu (\varphi ,0)\geq \nu '(\varphi ,0)$.

In order to argue the reverse inequality, we may assume that $\nu (\varphi ,0)>0$.

Next observe that by (1.24), for each small enough $\epsilon >0$, we can find $r_0\in (0,1)$ and $C>0$ so that for all $x\in B_ n(0,r_0)\setminus \{ 0\} $, we have

\[ \varphi (x)\leq (\nu (\varphi ,0)-\epsilon )\log |x|^2+C. \]

It follows that $\nu '(\varphi ,0)\geq \nu (\varphi ,0)-\epsilon $. Letting $\epsilon \to 0+$, we conclude that $\nu '(\varphi ,0)\geq \nu (\varphi ,0)$, which combined with the previous inequality gives (1.25). $\square$

We recall Siu's semicontinuity theorem.

Theorem 1.4.4 (Siu). Let $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$. Then the map $X\ni x\mapsto \nu (\varphi ,x)$ is upper semicontinuous with respect to the Zariski topology.

Proof. For every $c>0$, the analytic Lelong-level-set theorem proves that $\{x:\nu(\varphi,x)\geq c\}$ is an analytic subset. Since Lelong numbers are nonnegative, for $c\leq0$ this upper level set is all of $X$. These are exactly the upper level sets needed for upper semicontinuity in the analytic Zariski topology. The normalization is the same in both statements: $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm c}=\mathrm i\partial\bar\partial/(2\pi)$ and $\nu(\log|z|^2,0)=1$.

For an elegant proof we refer to [Theorem 2.10, Dem12].

Proposition 1.4.5. Let $\varphi ,\psi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$, $\lambda \in \mathbb {R}_{\geq 0}$ and $x\in X$. Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \nu (\varphi \lor \psi ,x)=& \min \{ \nu (\varphi ,x),\nu (\psi ,x)\} ,\\ \nu (\varphi +\psi ,x)=& \nu (\varphi ,x)+\nu (\psi ,x),\\ \nu (\lambda \varphi ,x)=& \lambda \nu (\varphi ,x). \end{aligned} \]

Proof. All properties are local, so we may assume that $X=B_ n(0,1)$ for some $n\in \mathbb {N}$. All properties follow directly from proposition 1.4.3. $\square$

Corollary 1.4.6. Let $(\varphi _ i)_{i\in I}$ be a non-empty family in $\mathrm{PSH}(X)$ locally uniformly bounded from above and $x\in X$. Then

\[ \nu \left(\operatorname *{sup}_{i\in I}{}\! {}^{*}\varphi _ i,x\right)=\inf _{i\in I}\nu (\varphi _ i,x). \]

Proof. We may assume that $X$ is connected. Write $\varphi =\operatorname *{sup}_{i\in I}{}\! {}^{*} \varphi _ i$. Then $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$ by proposition 1.2.2.

We observe that the $\leq $ inequality is trivial. It remains to argue the reverse inequality.

It follows from proposition 1.2.3 that we may assume that $I$ is countable. When $I$ is finite, this is already proved in proposition 1.4.5. Otherwise, we may further assume that $I=\mathbb {Z}_{>0}$. Thanks to proposition 1.4.5, we may further assume that $(\varphi _ i)_{i\in \mathbb {Z}_{>0}}$ is an increasing sequence. Furthermore, since the problem is local, we may assume that $x=0$, that $X=B_ n(0,1)$ for some $n\in \mathbb {N}$, and that $(\varphi _ i)_ i$ is uniformly bounded from above on some ball $B_ n(0,r_1)$ with $0<r_1<1$. Since the sequence $(\varphi _ i)_ i$ is increasing, the sequence $\nu (\varphi _ i,0)$ is decreasing. Thus the constants in (1.26) can be chosen independently of $i$, and we have

\[ \varphi _ i(x)\leq \nu (\varphi _ i,0)\log |x|^2+C \]

for all $x\in B_ n(0,r_1)\setminus \{ 0\} $ and all $i\geq 1$, where $C$ is a constant independent of $i$. In particular, thanks to proposition 1.2.5, for almost all $x\in B_ n(0,r_1)$, we have

\[ \varphi (x)\leq \lim _{i\to \infty }\nu (\varphi _ i,0)\log |x|^2+C. \]

Thanks to proposition 1.2.6, the same holds for all $x\neq 0$ near $0$, and hence

\[ \nu (\varphi ,0)\geq \lim _{i\to \infty } \nu (\varphi _ i,0). \]

This completes the proof. $\square$

Definition 1.4.7. Let $F\subseteq X$ be a non-empty irreducible analytic subset. Then we define the generic Lelong number of $\varphi $ along $F$ as

\[ \nu (\varphi ,F)\coloneqq \min _{x\in F}\nu (\varphi ,x). \]

Note that the minimum is attained by Siu's semicontinuity theorem theorem 1.4.4.

Definition 1.4.8. Let $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$. Let $E$ be a prime divisor over $X$ (see definition B.1.1). Take a proper bimeromorphic morphism $\pi \colon Y\rightarrow X$ from a complex manifold $Y$ such that $E$ is a prime divisor on $Y$, then we define the generic Lelong number of $\varphi $ along $E$ as

It follows from theorem 1.4.4 that $\nu (\varphi ,E)$ does not depend on the choice of $\pi $.

Theorem 1.4.12. Let $\varphi ,\psi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$. Then

\[ \mathcal{I}(\varphi +\psi )\subseteq \mathcal{I}(\varphi )\cdot \mathcal{I}(\psi ). \]

Proposition 1.4.13. Let $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$ and $E$ be a prime divisor over $X$. Then

\begin{equation} \nu (\varphi ,E)=\lim _{k\to \infty }\frac{1}{k}\operatorname{ord}_ E\mathcal{I}(k\varphi ). \end{equation}

Proof. By the divisorial multiplier-ideal estimate, for every positive integer $k$, \[k\nu(\varphi,E)-A_X(E)<\operatorname{ord}_E\mathcal I(k\varphi)\leq k\nu(\varphi,E).\] The log discrepancy $A_X(E)$ is a fixed finite number on a fixed smooth model carrying $E$. Lemma 1.4.14. Let $x \in X$ and $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X)$. Let $\pi \colon Y\rightarrow X$ be the blow-up of $X$ at $x$ with exceptional divisor $E$. Then

\[ \nu (\varphi ,x)=\nu (\varphi ,E), \]

Proof. For a coherent ideal $\mathfrak a$ near $x$, the order of $\mathfrak a\mathcal O_Y$ along the exceptional divisor of the blowup at $x$ is its ordinary vanishing order at $x$. Indeed, in a blowup chart substitute $z=(t,tw_2,\ldots,tw_n)$ into each Taylor series. Its lowest nonzero homogeneous term factors as $t^d$ times a polynomial in $w$ which is not identically zero on the exceptional divisor, so its generic order there is $d$; taking the minimum over generators proves the ideal assertion.

Apply this to $\mathfrak a=\mathcal I(k\varphi)$. The pointwise order estimate gives $k^{-1}\operatorname{ord}_x\mathcal I(k\varphi)\to\nu(\varphi,x)$, while its divisorial counterpart gives $k^{-1}\operatorname{ord}_E\mathcal I(k\varphi)\to\nu(\varphi,E)$. The two sequences are identical, proving the equality. In dimension one the blowup of the point ideal is an isomorphism and the assertion reduces to the point itself.

Qualification and proof. For the general germ statement, the precise formulation is the local one: divisors range over modifications of a sufficiently small neighborhood on which $f$ is represented. The proved uniform valuative criterion gives exactly \[f\in\mathcal I(\varphi)_x\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\exists\varepsilon>0:\ \operatorname{ord}_Ef+A_X(E)\geq(1+\varepsilon)\nu(\varphi,E)\] for all such divisors whose centers contain $x$. The same $\varepsilon$ must work for every divisor.

In the analytic-singularity clause, one must test all components of the total pole divisor on the log resolution whose centers contain $x$, including nonexceptional components. Restricting the test to exceptional divisors is false: on $\mathbb C$ take $\varphi=\log|z|^2$ and the identity resolution. There are no exceptional divisors, but $1\notin\mathcal I(\varphi)_0$ since $|z|^{-2}$ is not locally integrable.

Here is the finite test with this correction. On a log resolution write $\varphi\circ\pi=\sum_i a_i\log|s_i|^2+O(1)$ and let $b_i$ be the order of the holomorphic Jacobian along $D_i=\{s_i=0\}$. If $c_i=\operatorname{ord}_{D_i}(f\circ\pi)$, change of variables makes the integrand, up to a bounded factor and a holomorphic residual factor, a product of $|s_i|^{2(c_i+b_i-a_i)}$. At a generic point of each divisor this residual factor is nonzero. The one-variable integral $\int_0^\delta r^{2(c_i+b_i-a_i)+1}\,\mathrm dr$ is finite exactly when $c_i+b_i+1>a_i$. Since the divisor is simple normal crossings, Fubini proves sufficiency for their simultaneous inequalities and the generic-point calculation proves necessity. Thus the test is $\operatorname{ord}_{D_i}f+A_X(D_i)>\nu(\varphi,D_i)$ for all the indicated total pole components. This is also the log-resolution integrability criterion. Resolution itself remains the permitted Hironaka input.

1.5 Quasi-plurisubharmonic functions

In practice, it is important to consider a variant of plurisubharmonic functions. We will fix a complex manifold $X$.

Definition 1.5.1. Let $\theta $ be a closed real smooth $(1,1)$-form on $X$.

A $\theta $-plurisubharmonic function or $\theta $-psh function for short on $X$ is a function $\varphi \colon X\rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ such that for each $x\in X$ and each open neighborhood $U$ of $x$ in $X$ satisfying the condition that $\theta =\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}g$ for some smooth function $g$ on $U$, we have $g+\varphi |_ U\in \mathrm{PSH}(U)$. The set of $\theta $-psh functions on $X$ is denoted by $\mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$.

A quasi-plurisubharmonic function on $X$ is a function $\varphi \colon X\rightarrow [-\infty ,\infty )$ such that there exists a smooth closed real $(1,1)$-form $\theta '$ on $X$ such that $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta ')$. The set of quasi-plurisubharmonic functions on $X$ is denoted by $\mathrm{QPSH}(X)$.

Requiring $a_l\leq\sup_{X_l}u\leq b_l$ on every component gives a finite product of these compact sets, hence a compact set in $L^1(X)$. The global supremum is then the maximum of finitely many continuous component suprema and is continuous.

\[ \varphi \leq \varphi -\sup _ K \varphi . \]

Corollary 1.5.6. Assume that $X$ is compact. Let $\theta $ be a closed real smooth $(1,1)$-form on $X$, and $(\varphi _ i)_{i\in I}$ be a decreasing net in $\mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ such that $\sup _ X \varphi _ i$ is uniformly bounded from below. \begin{equation} \adjustlimits \lim _{i\in I}\sup _ X \varphi _ i=\sup _ X \varphi ,\quad \varphi _ i\xrightarrow {L^1}\varphi . \end{equation}

Proof. We may assume that $X$ is connected.

It suffices to show that $\varphi \not\equiv -\infty $. From this, it follows that $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and $\varphi _ i\to \varphi $ in $L^1$ by proposition 1.2.2. The $L^1$-continuity of $\eta \mapsto \sup _ X\eta $ on $\mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ from proposition 1.5.4 then gives the first equality in (1.35).

Fix $i_0\in I$ and replace $I$ by the cofinal tail $I_{\geq i_0}$. This does not change the infimum. On this tail we have

\[ \sup _ X\varphi _ i\leq \sup _ X\varphi _{i_0}, \]

while the suprema are bounded from below by assumption. Hence proposition 1.5.4 shows that the net $(\varphi _ i)_{i\in I}$ is relatively compact with respect to the $L^1$-topology. We may therefore take a subnet $(\varphi _ j)_{j\in J}$ converging in $L^1$ to some $\psi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$. Since $(\varphi _ j)_{j\in J}$ is a Willard subnet of $(\varphi _ i)_{i\in I}$, we know that it is also decreasing. Therefore, $\psi =\inf _{j\in J} \varphi _ j$ due to proposition 1.2.2 and proposition 1.2.6.

Fix $i_0\in I$. For all sufficiently large $j\in J$, the corresponding index of the subnet dominates $i_0$, hence

\[ \varphi _ j\leq \varphi _{i_0}. \]

Therefore, $\psi \leq \varphi _{i_0}$. As $i_0$ was arbitrary, $\psi \leq \varphi $ everywhere. In particular, $\varphi $ is not identically $-\infty $. $\square$

Next we recall the notion of modified nef classes.

Definition 1.7.13. A class $\alpha \in \mathrm{H}^{1,1}(X,\mathbb {R})$ is modified nef if the following condition holds: after fixing a reference Kähler metric $\omega $ on $X$, for any $\epsilon >0$, we can find a closed $(1,1)$-current $T\in \alpha $ such that

  1. $T+\epsilon \omega \geq 0$;

  2. $\nu (T+\epsilon \omega ,D)=0$ for any prime divisor $D$ on $X$.

This definition is independent of the choice of $\omega $.

These classes are called nef en codimension $1$ in Boucksom's thesis [Bou02], where they were introduced for the first time. They are also known as movable classes in other references. Modified nef classes form a closed convex cone in $\mathrm{H}^{1,1}(X,\mathbb {R})$. Note that a modified nef class is necessarily pseudo-effective. A nef class is obviously modified nef. Definition 1.7.14. Let $\alpha \in \mathrm{H}^{1,1}(X,\mathbb {R})$ be a pseudo-effective class and $D$ be a prime divisor on $X$. We define the Lelong number $\nu (\alpha ,D)$ as follows:

  1. When $\alpha $ is big, define $\nu (\alpha ,D)=\nu (T,D)$ for any closed positive $(1,1)$-current $T\in \alpha $ with minimal singularities.

  2. In general, define

    \[ \nu (\alpha ,D)\coloneqq \lim _{\epsilon \to 0+}\nu (\alpha +\epsilon \{ \omega \} ,D). \]
When $\alpha $ is big, 2 is compatible with 1 and the definition is independent of the choice of $\omega $.

By definition, a pseudo-effective class $\alpha $ is modified nef if and only if $\nu (\alpha ,D)=0$ for all prime divisors $D$ on $X$.

Let us recall the behavior of several cones under modifications.

Proposition 1.7.15. Let $\pi \colon Y\rightarrow X$ be a proper bimeromorphic morphism from a Kähler manifold $Y$.

  1. For any nef class $\alpha \in \mathrm{H}^{1,1}(X,\mathbb {R})$, $\pi ^*\alpha $ is nef.

  2. For any modified nef class $\beta \in \mathrm{H}^{1,1}(Y,\mathbb {R})$, $\pi _*\beta $ is modified nef.

Proof. Only 2 requires a proof. Fix a Kähler class $\gamma $ on $Y$. Since the modified nef cone is closed, replacing $\beta $ by $\beta +\epsilon \gamma $ and then letting $\epsilon \to 0+$ reduces the proof to the case where $\beta $ is big as well. Let $T$ (resp. $S$) be a current with minimal singularities in $\pi _*\beta $ (resp. in $\beta $), and let $D$ be a prime divisor on $X$. It suffices to show that

\[ \nu (T,D)=0. \]

Since $\beta $ is modified nef, $S$ is non-divisorial. Hence by 1.7.16 below, $\nu (\pi _*S,D)=0$. Since $T$ has minimal singularities in $\pi _*\beta $, we have $T\succeq \pi _*S$, and the desired equality follows. $\square$

Lemma 1.7.16. Let $\pi \colon Y\rightarrow X$ be a proper bimeromorphic morphism from a Kähler manifold $Y$. Let $T$ be a non-divisorial current on $Y$. Then $\pi _*T$ is non-divisorial.

Conversely, if $S$ is a non-divisorial current on $X$, $\pi ^*S$ could have divisorial part. As a simple example, consider $S$ on $\mathbb {P}^2$, whose local potential near $0\in \mathbb {C}^2_{z,w}$ looks like $\log (|z|^2+|w|^2)$.

Proof. Let $D$ be a prime divisor on $X$. It follows from Zariski's main theorem theorem B.1.4 that $D$ is not contained in the exceptional locus of $\pi $. Let $D'$ be the strict transform of $D$. Thanks to Siu's semicontinuity theorem theorem 1.4.4, we have

\[ \nu (\pi _*T,D)=\nu (T,D')=0. \]

Hence $\pi _*T$ is non-divisorial. $\square$

1.8 Plurisubharmonic metrics on line bundles

A natural source of quasi-plurisubharmonic functions is the metrics on line bundles.

Let $X$ be a connected Kähler manifold and $L$ be a holomorphic line bundle on $X$. Usually, we do not distinguish $L$ from the associated invertible sheaf $\mathcal{O}_ X(L)$.

Definition 1.8.1. Let $V$ be a $1$-dimensional complex linear space. A Hermitian form $h$ on $V$ is a map $h\colon V\times V\rightarrow \mathbb {C}$ such that

  1. $h$ is $\mathbb {C}$-linear in the second variable and conjugate linear in the first, and

  2. \[ |v|_ h^2\coloneqq h(v,v)\in \mathbb {R}_{>0} \]

    for each $v\in V\setminus \{ 0\} $.

We usually identify $h$ with the quadratic form $V\rightarrow \mathbb {R}$ sending $v$ to $|v|_ h^2$. We write $|v|_ h=\sqrt{|v|_ h^2}$ for any $v\in V$.

The singular Hermitian form on $V$ is the map $V\rightarrow \{ 0,\infty \} $ sending $0$ to $0$ and other elements to $\infty $.

Definition 1.8.2. Let $V_1$ and $V_2$ be $1$-dimensional complex linear spaces. Given two maps $h_ i\colon V_ i\rightarrow [0,\infty ]$ ($i=1,2$), each of which is either a Hermitian form or a singular Hermitian form, we define the tensor product $h_1\otimes h_2\colon V_1\otimes V_2\rightarrow [0,\infty ]$ as follows:

  1. If either $h_1$ or $h_2$ is singular, we define $h_1\otimes h_2$ as the singular Hermitian form;

  2. otherwise, define $h_1\otimes h_2$ as the usual tensor product: For any $v_1\in V_1$, $v_2\in V_2$, set

    \[ h_1\otimes h_2(v_1\otimes v_2)=h_1(v_1)h_2(v_2). \]

Definition 1.8.3. A Hermitian metric $h$ on $L$ is a family of Hermitian forms $(h_ x)_{x\in X}$, such that

  1. for each $x\in X$, $h_ x$ is a Hermitian form on $L_ x$, and

  2. for each local holomorphic section $s$ of $\mathcal{O}_ X(L)$, the map $x\mapsto |s(x)|^2_{h_ x}$ is smooth.

The pair $(L,h)$ is called a Hermitian line bundle. We shall write $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h=c_1(L,h)$
Note. The unusual notation $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h$ is sometimes referred to as the Göteborg notation because it is widely used by complex geometers in Göteborg (usually spelled as Gothenburg in English, the second largest (yet very poorly known) city in Sweden). As I identify myself as Göteborgare, I do not feel guilty about this notation.
for the first Chern form of $h$
Note. In the literature, people sometimes define the curvature form of $(L,h)$ as $\Theta _{h}=-2\pi \mathrm{i}\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h$.
, normalized so that
\[ [c_1(L,h)]=c_1(L). \]

The map $x\mapsto |s(x)|_{h_ x}$ will be denoted by $|s|_ h$.

To be more precise, if $U\subseteq X$ is an open subset on which $L$ admits a nowhere vanishing holomorphic section $s$, then we define

\[ (\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h)|_ U=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\left(-\log |s|_ h^2\right). \]

Proposition 1.8.4 (Lelong–Poincaré). Let $s\in \mathrm{H}^0(X,L)$ be non-zero and $h$ be a Hermitian metric on $L$. Then

1.45
\begin{equation} c_1(L,h)+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log |s|_ h^2=[Z(s)], \end{equation}

where $Z(s)$ is the zero divisor defined by $s$ and $[\bullet ]$ denote the associated current of integration.

Proof. In a holomorphic frame $e$ of $L$, write $s=fe$ and $|e|_h^2=\exp(-\rho)$. Then $\log|s|_h^2=\log|f|^2-\rho$ and $c_1(L,h)=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm c}\rho$. The normalized Poincaré--Lelong formula gives $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm c}\log|f|^2=[Z(f)]$. Substitution proves \[c_1(L,h)+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm c}\log|s|_h^2=[Z(s)].\] Changing the frame multiplies $f$ by a nowhere-vanishing holomorphic function; its logarithmic squared modulus is pluriharmonic, so the local identities agree on overlaps. This also checks the factor $1/(2\pi)$ in the normalization.

See [(3.11), Dem12]. Again, we want to check that our conventions are compatible by investigating the following simple example.

Example 1.8.5. Let $X=\mathbb {P}^1$ and $L=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb {P}^1}(1)$. The homogeneous coordinates on $\mathbb {P}^1$ will be denoted by $[X_0:X_1]$. At a point $x=[X_0:X_1]\in \mathbb {P}^1$, the fiber $L_ x$ is identified with the dual of $[x]$, where $[x]\subseteq \mathbb {C}^2$ is the line represented by $x$.

In order to introduce the Hermitian metric $h$ on $L$, we fix the standard Hermitian norm $\| \bullet \| $ on $\mathbb {C}^2$. Then given $\lambda \in L_ x=[x]^{\vee }$, we introduce

\[ |\lambda |_{h_ x}=\frac{|\lambda (\tilde{x})|}{\| \tilde{x}\| }, \]

where $\tilde{x}$ is an arbitrary non-zero element in $[x]$. The reader can easily verify that $h$ is indeed a Hermitian metric on $L$. The Hermitian metric $h$ is known as the Fubini–Study metric.

A holomorphic section $s\in \mathrm{H}^0(X,L)$ can be formally identified with a linear form $a_0X_0+a_1X_1$: At $x\in X$, the corresponding linear form on $[x]$ is given by sending $(X_0,X_1)$ to $a_0X_0+a_1X_1$.

Next we compute $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h=c_1(L,h)$. For this purpose, we cover $\mathbb {P}^1$ by $\mathbb {C}=\mathbb {P}^1\setminus \{ \infty \} $ and $\mathbb {P}^1\setminus \{ 0\} $. Both are holomorphic coordinate charts with coordinate function $z=X_0/X_1$ and $z^{-1}=X_1/X_0$ respectively.

We claim that on $\mathbb {C}$,

1.46
\begin{equation} \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log (1+|z|^2). \end{equation}

In fact, let $t$ be the nowhere vanishing section of $L$ on $\mathbb {C}$ corresponding to $X_1$. Then for $z\in \mathbb {C}$, we have an obvious lift $(z,1)\in [z]$, so

\[ |t|_ h^2(z)=\frac{1}{|z|^2+1}. \]

So (1.46) follows.

In order to obtain a non-trivial case of the Lelong–Poincaré formula, we need to consider a section which vanishes at some points in $\mathbb {C}$. Let $s$ be the holomorphic section of $L$ corresponding to $X_0$. Then

\[ \log |s|_ h^2(z)=\log \frac{|z|^2}{|z|^2+1} \]

for any $z\in \mathbb {C}$ using the same argument as above. Therefore, we find that restricted to $\mathbb {C}$, we have

\[ c_1(L,h)+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log |s|_ h^2=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f=[0], \]

where $f(z)=\log |z|^2$. So the Lelong–Poincaré formula (1.45) is verified in this case.

The Kähler form $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h$ on $\mathbb {P}^1$ is also known as the Fubini–Study metric.

Definition 1.8.6. A (singular) plurisubharmonic metric (or psh metric for short)
Note. In the literature, people usually refer to such metrics as positively curved singular Hermitian metrics. I dislike this terminology, as having positive curvature only determines a plurisubharmonic metric almost everywhere, not everywhere.
$h$ on $L$ is a family $(h_ x)_{x\in X}$ such that

  1. for each $x\in X$, $h_ x$ is either a Hermitian form on $L_ x$ or the singular Hermitian form on $L_ x$, and

  2. there is a Hermitian metric $h_0$ on $L$ and $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,c_1(L,h_0))$ such that for each $x\in X$ and each $v\in L_ x$, we have

    1.47
    \begin{equation} |v|^2_{h_ x}= \left\{ \begin{aligned} 0,& \quad \textup{if }v=0;\\ |v|^2_{h_{0,x}}\mathrm{e}^{-\varphi (x)},& \quad \textup{if }v\neq 0. \end{aligned} \right. \end{equation}

The (first) Chern current of $h$ is by definition

\[ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h=c_1(L,h)\coloneqq c_1(L,h_0)+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\varphi . \]

The set of psh metrics on $L$ will be denoted by $\mathrm{PSH}(L)$.

We shall write the plurisubharmonic metric defined by (1.47) as $h_0\exp (-\varphi )$
Note. Be careful, this is not $h_0^2\exp (-\varphi )$, as I prefer to think of $h_0$ as a quadratic form.
. With these conventions, $c_1(L,h)$ does not depend on the choice of $h_0$.

Given a local holomorphic section $s$ of $L$, we shall use the following notations interchangeably to denote the map $x\mapsto h_ x(s)$:

\[ |s|^2_ h=h(s)=h(s,s). \]

Remark 1.8.7. In the literature, some people prefer the convention that in (1.47), neither side has the square. Our choice seems to be the most natural one given our normalization of $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$.

Observe that once a Hermitian metric $h_0$ on $L$ is given, the construction in 2 gives a bijection between $\mathrm{PSH}(X,c_1(L,h_0))$ and the set of plurisubharmonic metrics on $L$.

Based on this correspondence, plurisubharmonic metrics have the same functoriality property as quasi-plurisubharmonic functions in proposition 1.1.13. In particular, given a connected closed submanifold $Y$ of $X$, $h|_ Y$ is a plurisubharmonic metric as long as it is not singular everywhere.

Definition 1.8.8. Given two holomorphic line bundles $L_1$, $L_2$ on $X$ and plurisubharmonic metrics $h_1$ on $L_1$ and $h_2$ on $L_2$, we define the tensor product plurisubharmonic metric $h_1\otimes h_2$ on $L_1\otimes L_2$ as follows: for each $x\in X$, define

\[ (h_1\otimes h_2)_ x=h_{1,x}\otimes h_{2,x} \]

in the sense of definition 1.8.2.

We can easily verify that $h_1\otimes h_2$ is indeed a plurisubharmonic metric on $L_1\otimes L_2$.

Example 1.8.9. We continue with our example example 1.8.5. Let $X=\mathbb {P}^1$ and $L=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb {P}^1}(1)$. Let $h^0$ denote the Fubini–Study metric on $L$ as defined in example 1.8.5. Note that we have changed the notation from $h$ to $h^0$. Let $\omega =\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h^0$.

We construct $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\omega )$ as follows: On $\mathbb {C}$, define

1.50
\begin{equation} \varphi (z)=\log \frac{|z|^2}{1+|z|^2}. \end{equation}
Then $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(\mathbb {C},\omega |_{\mathbb {C}})$ by (1.46). Setting $\varphi (\infty )=0$, we can easily verify that $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(\mathbb {P}^1,\omega )$.
Note. This can also be verified using the Grauert–Remmert extension theorem theorem 1.2.9.

We then get a plurisubharmonic metric $h=h^0\exp (-\varphi )$. To be more explicit, the fiber metric $h$ at $0$ is singular, $h_{\infty }=h^0_{\infty }$, while for $z\in \mathbb {C}\setminus \{ 0\} $ and $\lambda \in [z]^{\vee }$, we have

\[ |\lambda |_{h_ z}=\frac{|\lambda (z,1)|}{|z|}. \]

In the remainder of this section, we assume that $X$ is compact.

Definition 1.8.10.Assume that $L$ is a pseudo-effective line bundle on $X$. A Fubini–Study metric on $L$ is a psh metric $h$ on $L$ of the following form: There exists $m\in \mathbb {Z}_{>0}$, finitely many sections $s_1,\ldots ,s_ N\in \mathrm{H}^0(X,L^ m)$ and $\lambda _1,\ldots ,\lambda _ N\in \mathbb {Q}$ such that for any local nowhere vanishing holomorphic section $s$ of $L$, we have

\[ |s|_ h^2=\min _{i=1,\ldots ,N}\left| \frac{s^{\otimes m}}{\mathrm{e}^{\lambda _ i/2}s_ i}\right|^{2m^{-1}}. \]

We write $\mathrm{FS}(L)$ for the set of Fubini–Study metrics on $L$.

If we fix a reference smooth Hermitian metric $h_0$ on $L$ with $\theta =\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h_0$, we can write $h=h_0\exp (-\varphi )$ with
\[ \varphi =\frac{1}{m}\max _{i=1,\ldots ,N}\left(\log |s_ i|^2_{h_0^{m}}+\lambda _ i\right). \]

Similarly, we write $\mathrm{FS}(X,\theta )$ for the set of such functions.

Definition 1.8.11. Assume that $L$ is a pseudo-effective line bundle on $X$. The set $\widetilde{\mathrm{FS}}(L)$ of generalized Fubini–Study metrics is the smallest subset of $\mathrm{PSH}(L)$ containing $\mathrm{FS}(L)$ which is closed under the following two operations:

  1. $\mathbb {Q}$-convex combinations: if $h_1,h_2\in \widetilde{\mathrm{FS}}(L)$ and $t\in (0,1)$, then

    \[ h_1^ t \otimes h_2^{1-t}\in \widetilde{\mathrm{FS}}(L); \]

  2. minima: if $h_1,h_2\in \widetilde{\mathrm{FS}}(L)$, then

    \[ \min \{ h_1,h_2\} \in \widetilde{\mathrm{FS}}(L). \]
We shall need the following Ohsawa–Takegoshi type extension theorem.
Theorem 1.8.12. Assume that $L$ is big and $T$ is a holomorphic line bundle on $X$. Fix a Hermitian metric $h_ T$ on $T$. Fix a Hermitian metric $h$ on $L$ and let $\theta =c_1(L,h)$. Take a Kähler form $\omega $ on $X$. Let $Y\subseteq X$ be a connected closed submanifold of dimension $m$. Suppose that $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta - \delta \omega )$ for some $\delta >0$ and $\varphi |_ Y\not\equiv -\infty $. Then there exists $k_0(\delta ,h_ T)>0$ such that for all $k \geq k_0$ and $s \in \mathrm{H}^0(Y, T\otimes L|_ Y^ k \otimes \mathcal{I}(k\varphi |_ Y))$

, there exists an extension $\tilde s \in \mathrm{H}^0(X, T\otimes L^ k \otimes \mathcal{I}(k\varphi ))$ such that
\[ \int _ X |\tilde{s}|^2_{h^ k\otimes h_ T}\mathrm{e}^{-k\varphi }\, \omega ^ n \leq C \int _ Y |s|^2_{(h^ k\otimes h_ T)|_ Y} \mathrm{e}^{-k\varphi |_ Y}\, \omega |_ Y^ m, \]

where $C>0$ is an absolute constant, independent of the data $(\varphi ,s,k)$.

This is a special case of [Theorem 1.4, His12]. Let us also recall a simple result about the Bergman kernel approximations:

Proposition 1.8.13. Let $(L,h)$ be a Hermitian line bundle on $X$ and set $\theta =c_1(L,h)$. Let $(T,h_ T)$ be a Hermitian line bundle on $X$. Assume that $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ is a potential with analytic singularities such that $\theta _{\varphi }$ is a Kähler current. Fix a Kähler form $\omega $ on $X$. For each $k\geq 1$, we let

1.51
\begin{equation} \varphi _ k \coloneqq \frac{1}{k} \log \sup _{\substack {s \in \mathrm{H}^0(X,L^ k \otimes T)\\ \int _ X |s|^2_{h^ k \otimes h_ T}\mathrm{e}^{-k\varphi } \omega ^ n \leq 1}} |s|^2_{h^ k \otimes h_ T}. \end{equation}

Then for all sufficiently large $k$,

\[ \varphi \preceq \varphi _ k\preceq \alpha _ k \varphi , \]

where $\alpha _ k\in (0,1)$ is an increasing sequence with limit $1$.

Proof. First we prove $\varphi \preceq \varphi _ k$. Since $\theta _{\varphi }$ is a Kähler current, there is $\delta >0$ such that $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta -\delta \omega )$. Let $x\in X$ with $\varphi (x)>-\infty $. Applying theorem 1.8.12 to the point $Y=\{ x\} $, for all sufficiently large $k$ and every unit vector $e_ x\in (L^ k\otimes T)_ x$, there is a section $\tilde{s}\in \mathrm{H}^0(X,L^ k\otimes T\otimes \mathcal{I}(k\varphi ))$ with $\tilde{s}(x)=e_ x$ and

\[ \int _ X |\tilde{s}|^2_{h^ k\otimes h_ T}\mathrm{e}^{-k\varphi }\omega ^ n\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-k\varphi (x)}. \]

After rescaling $\tilde{s}$, the definition of $\varphi _ k$ gives

\[ \varphi _ k(x)\geq \varphi (x)-\frac{\log C}{k}. \]

This proves $\varphi \preceq \varphi _ k$.

The other part of the inequality follows from 1.6.14 and (1.42).

More precisely, if $s_1,\ldots ,s_ N$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathrm{H}^0(X,L^ k\otimes T\otimes \mathcal{I}(k\varphi ))$, with respect to the $L^2$-norm:

\[ s\mapsto \left( \int _ X |s|^2_{h^ k\otimes h_ T}\mathrm{e}^{-k\varphi }\, \omega ^ n \right)^{1/2}, \]

then by (1.42),

\[ s_ i\in \mathrm{H}^0\left(X,L^ k\otimes T\otimes \mathcal{I}_{\infty }\left((k-c^{-1}n)_+\varphi \right)\right), \]

where $c>0$ depends only on $\varphi $. So there is $C_ k>0$ so that

\[ \sum _{i=1}^ N |s_ i|^2_{h^ k\otimes h_ T}\mathrm{e}^{-(k-c^{-1}n)_+\varphi }\leq C_ k\quad \textup{quasi-everywhere}. \]

In other words,

\[ \varphi _ k\preceq \frac{1}{k}\left( k-c^{-1}n \right)_+ \varphi . \]

It suffices to take $\alpha _ k=(1-k^{-1}c^{-1}n)_+$. $\square$
