11.4 Volumes under bounded mass

Definitions, results, and proofs on this subject.

30 Volumes under bounded mass

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

In a Hermitian background, Monge–Ampère masses need not be bounded above or away from zero. On the standard Hopf threefold the masses of smooth admissible potentials of a Gauduchon form are unbounded (theorem 8.0.1), while on the threefold of theorem 26.18.1 every Hermitian form $h$ admits smooth admissible potentials whose masses tend to zero. In this part such bounds are imposed explicitly. The upper bound is the bounded mass property of definition 6.5.1: $v_+(\omega )<+\infty $ for one, and hence for every, Hermitian form $\omega $ (proposition 6.5.2), where $v_+(\omega )$ and $v_-(\omega )$ denote the supremum and the infimum of the masses of bounded $\omega $-plurisubharmonic functions. A lower bound takes one of two forms. In chapter 31, which does not assume bounded mass, it is positive lower mass $v_-(\theta )>0$ of a semipositive background $\theta $. In this chapter and in chapter 32, which assume bounded mass, it is usually a positive lower singularity volume of the particular potentials involved, rather than a bound for all bounded potentials.

In a closed background, the non-pluripolar mass of a potential depends only on its ordinary singularity type. In a nonclosed background, potentials of the same type can have different masses, so a singularity type carries two numbers, the infimum and the supremum of the masses of its members. These lower and upper singularity volumes keep the order information needed for comparison. Bounded mass makes the upper volume finite, and a positive lower volume prevents the auxiliary envelopes used below from collapsing. Together these bounds turn a small upper-volume deficit into a large admissible stretch, and this relates equality of volumes to the relative full-mass class and to ordinary envelopes.

The upper volume of the maximum of two potentials measures how far apart their ordinary envelopes are. On models with a uniform positive lower-volume bound, the stretching estimate controls three-point comparisons, monotone limits and convergence in capacity. The comparison itself need not satisfy the triangle inequality, even on the projective plane, and at zero volume it can identify distinct models (example 30.6.2). Finite chains nevertheless give a complete metric once the reciprocal of the lower volume is included to prevent escape to zero volume. Analytic regularization identifies the multiplier-ideal models among the resulting limits, and strict barriers allow these comparisons to persist under small changes of background.

On a curve, the polar part of the curvature current determines the ordinary model, and the volume comparison is the total variation of polar measures. This description extends to the zero-volume boundary. Taking the atomic part gives the nearest ideal model, while diffuse polar curvature measures the failure of analytic approximation. Area rescaling and smooth gauges then describe changes of background, including the collapse of all normalized potentials when the backgrounds converge uniformly to a form of zero area.

Throughout the chapter, $X$ is a connected compact complex manifold of dimension $n\geq 1$ with the bounded mass property, and $\theta $ is a big smooth real $(1,1)$-form on $X$, not necessarily closed; bigness means that $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho \geq \varepsilon \omega $ for some $\rho \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, some $\varepsilon >0$ and a Hermitian form $\omega $ (section 1.19). From section 30.12 on, we work instead on a compact Riemann surface with the backgrounds specified there.

We recall the notation used from earlier chapters. For quasi-plurisubharmonic functions, $u\preceq v$ means $u\leq v+C$ for a constant $C$, and $u\sim v$ means $u\preceq v\preceq u$; the classes of $\sim $ are the ordinary singularity types (definition 1.12.1). Similarly, $u\preceq _{\mathcal I}v$ means $\mathcal I(tu)\subseteq \mathcal I(tv)$ for every $t>0$, and $u\sim _{\mathcal I}v$ means that these multiplier ideals are equal (section 1.12). For $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, $\mathrm{MA}_\theta (u)$ is the non-pluripolar Monge–Ampère measure of theorem 6.1.2, which is finite under bounded mass by proposition 6.5.3. The lower and upper singularity volumes $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)$ and $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)$ of definition 13.3.1 are the infimum and the supremum of $\int _ X\mathrm{MA}_\theta (v)$ over all $v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $v\sim u$. A statement about $\operatorname {vol}_\theta $, without a bar, is asserted for both volumes.

Given an obstacle $f$, we denote by $P_\theta (f)$ the regularized upper envelope of the $\theta $-plurisubharmonic functions lying below $f$, and we write $P_\theta (u,v)=P_\theta (\min (u,v))$ for the rooftop envelope and $V_\theta =P_\theta (0)$ (section 1.19). The ordinary envelope $P_\theta [u]$ is the regularized supremum of all $h\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $h\preceq u$ and $h\leq 0$ (definition 1.19.4), and $P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}$ is the multiplier-ideal envelope of definition 16.2.1, defined in the same way with $h\sim _{\mathcal I}u$ in place of $h\preceq u$. We call $u$ an ordinary model if $P_\theta [u]=u$, and an ideal model if $P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}=u$. Both kinds have supremum zero, and every ideal model is an ordinary model by corollary 16.2.4. Finally, $\nu (u,x)$ denotes the Lelong number at $x$, and a potential is called analytic if it has analytic singularities in the rational-coefficient convention of section 1.13.

30.1 Comparison of singularity volumes

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Choose a Hermitian form $\Omega \geq \theta $. By proposition 6.5.3, theorem 6.1.2, the mass of every $\theta $-plurisubharmonic potential is at most $v_+(\Omega )$. Hence, for $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$,

\[ 0\leq \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u) \leq \int _ X\mathrm{MA}_\theta (u) \leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)<+\infty . \]

If $\theta $ is closed, both volumes equal $\int _ X\mathrm{MA}_\theta (u)$, because in the closed theory the non-pluripolar mass depends only on the singularity type [SGPT]. For a nonclosed background the two volumes can differ even at $u=0$, as example 2.3.1 shows.

Both volumes increase when the background increases or the singularities become milder. In the proof, a rooftop envelope for the lower volume and a maximum for the upper volume produce competitors of the required singularity types, and locality of non-pluripolar products confines the error to sets whose mass tends to zero.

Theorem 30.1.1. Let $\theta _1,\theta _2$ be smooth real $(1,1)$-forms on $X$. Suppose $\theta _1$ is big, $\theta _1\leq \theta _2$, and $u_ i\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta _ i)$ satisfy $u_1\preceq u_2$. Then

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _1}(u_1) \leq \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _2}(u_2),\qquad \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _1}(u_1) \leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _2}(u_2). \]

Proof. We first treat the lower volume. After adding a constant to $u_1$ we may assume $u_1\leq u_2$. Choose $v_2\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta _2)$ with $v_2\sim u_2$ and, for real $t>0$, consider the rooftop envelope

\[ p_ t=P_{\theta _1}(u_1+t,v_2). \]

Since $u_1\leq u_2\leq v_2+C$ for a constant $C$, the function $u_1-C$ is a competitor; moreover $p_ t\leq u_1+t$. Hence $p_ t\sim u_1$. By the contact theorem (theorem 14.7.4), the measure $\mathrm{MA}_{\theta _1}(p_ t)$ is carried by the union of $\{ p_ t=u_1+t\} \subset \{ u_1\leq v_2-t\} $ and $\{ p_ t=v_2\} $. On the first set, ordered contact in background $\theta _1$ (proposition 6.2.1) bounds it by $\mathrm{MA}_{\theta _1}(u_1)$. On the second set, $p_ t$ is also $\theta _2$-plurisubharmonic and lies below $v_2$; positivity of the mixed products (lemma 6.1.1) and ordered contact in background $\theta _2$ bound it by $\mathrm{MA}_{\theta _2}(v_2)$. Therefore

\[ \mathrm{MA}_{\theta _1}(p_ t) \leq \mathbf1_{\{ u_1\leq v_2-t\} }\mathrm{MA}_{\theta _1}(u_1) +\mathrm{MA}_{\theta _2}(v_2). \]

As $t\to +\infty $, the first measure on the right has total mass tending to zero: the sets $\{ u_1\leq v_2-t\} $ decrease to a subset of the pole set of $u_1$, and $\mathrm{MA}_{\theta _1}(u_1)$ is a finite measure that puts no mass on pluripolar sets. Integrating and letting $t\to +\infty $ gives $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _1}(u_1)\leq \int _ X\mathrm{MA}_{\theta _2}(v_2)$, and taking the infimum over $v_2$ proves the first inequality.

For the upper volume, choose any $v_1\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta _1)$ with $v_1\sim u_1$, and put $w_ t=\max (v_1,u_2-t)$ for $t>0$. This function is $\theta _2$-plurisubharmonic and satisfies $w_ t\sim u_2$. On the set $D_ t=\{ v_1>u_2-t\} $, locality and positivity of the mixed products imply

\[ \mathbf1_{D_ t}\mathrm{MA}_{\theta _1}(v_1) \leq \mathbf1_{D_ t}\mathrm{MA}_{\theta _2}(w_ t). \]

The sets $D_ t$ exhaust the complement of the pole set of $v_1$, which carries no mass. Integrating and letting $t\to +\infty $ therefore gives $\int _ X\mathrm{MA}_{\theta _1}(v_1)\leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _2}(u_2)$, and taking the supremum over $v_1$ proves the second inequality. $\square$

Adding a positive current also increases both volumes. For the lower volume, the potential of the added current is subtracted inside an envelope, which produces a competitor in the original background.

Proposition 30.1.2. Let $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and $v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\eta )$ for a smooth real $(1,1)$-form $\eta $ on $X$. Then, for either the lower or the upper volume,

\[ \operatorname {vol}_\theta (u) \leq \operatorname {vol}_{\theta +\eta }(u+v). \]

Proof. The form $\theta +\eta $ is big: adding the positive current $\eta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v$ to a strictly positive current $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho $ gives a strictly positive current $\theta +\eta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(\rho +v)$. For the upper volume, add $v$ to each competitor $u'\sim u$ in $\mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$. Then $u'+v\sim u+v$, and positivity of the mixed products shows that $\int _ X\mathrm{MA}_{\theta +\eta }(u'+v)\geq \int _ X\mathrm{MA}_\theta (u')$.

For the lower volume, choose $w\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta +\eta )$ with $w\sim u+v$, and let $p=P_\theta (w-v)$. Since $w-v=u+O(1)$ outside the pole sets, a translate of $u$ is a competitor and $p\leq u+C$ for a constant $C$. Thus $p\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and $p\sim u$. By the contact theorem, $\mathrm{MA}_\theta (p)$ is carried by $D=\{ p+v=w\} $.

Furthermore $p+v\leq w$ everywhere by lemma 1.3.3. Positivity of the mixed products and ordered contact therefore give

\[ \mathrm{MA}_\theta (p) \leq \mathbf1_ D\mathrm{MA}_{\theta +\eta }(p+v) \leq \mathbf1_ D\mathrm{MA}_{\theta +\eta }(w). \]

Integrating and taking the infimum over $w$ proves the assertion. $\square$

30.2 Volume deficits and relative full mass

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

The upper-volume deficit measures how far one potential can be stretched away from another, and the positive lower volume of the less singular potential prevents the stretching envelopes from collapsing. We use the stretching envelopes of chapter 13. For $u,v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and $b\geq 1$, the competitor family $\mathcal A_ b(u,v)$ consists of all $h\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $b^{-1}h+(1-b^{-1})v\leq u$. When it is nonempty, its upper envelope is the $\theta $-plurisubharmonic function $Q_ b(u,v)=P_\theta (bu-(b-1)v)$, which again belongs to $\mathcal A_ b(u,v)$. For $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, the relative envelope class $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,\varphi )$ of definition 13.4.1 consists of all $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $u\preceq \varphi $ and $\mathcal A_ b(u,\varphi )\neq \varnothing $ for every $b\geq 1$.

Lemma 30.2.1. Let $u,v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ satisfy $u\preceq v$, and put

\[ \delta =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (v)>0, \qquad D=\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (v) -\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u). \]

If $b>1$ and $b^ nD<\delta $, then $\mathcal A_ b(u,v)\neq \varnothing $; that is, the stretching envelope $Q_ b(u,v)$ is defined.

Proof. Adding constants to $u$ and $v$ only translates the competitors, so we may assume $v\leq 0$. Write $V=\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (v)$. Since $D<b^{-n}\delta $, the definition of the upper volume gives $w\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $w\sim u$ and

30.1
\begin{equation} V-\int _ X\mathrm{MA}_\theta (w)<b^{-n}\delta . \end{equation}

After a translation of $w$ we may also assume $w\leq v$. For positive integers $j$, put

\[ w_ j=\max (w,v-j),\qquad h_ j=Q_ b(w_ j,v). \]

The function $v-bj$ is a competitor for $h_ j$, and the defining convex inequality together with $w_ j\leq v$ gives $h_ j\leq v$. Thus $v-bj\leq h_ j\leq v$, so $w_ j\sim v$ and $h_ j\sim v$, and $h_ j$ decreases as $j$ increases. We claim that the decreasing limit $h$ of the $h_ j$ is not identically $-\infty $. If it were, theorem 1.4.2 would give $\sup _ Xh_ j\to -\infty $.

By theorem 14.7.4, the measure of $h_ j$ is carried by the contact set $C_ j=\{ b^{-1}h_ j+(1-b^{-1})v=w_ j\} $. Positivity of the mixed products and ordered contact for $b^{-1}h_ j+(1-b^{-1})v\leq w_ j$ give

\[ \mathrm{MA}_\theta (h_ j) \leq b^ n\mathbf1_{C_ j}\mathrm{MA}_\theta (w_ j). \]

Fix a positive integer $k$. If $h\equiv -\infty $, then $h_ j\leq -bk$ everywhere for all large $j>k$. Since $v\leq 0$, every point of $C_ j$ then satisfies $w_ j\leq -k$, hence $w\leq -k$. Moreover $w_ j=w$ on $\{ w>-k\} \subset \{ w>v-j\} $, so locality gives

\[ \begin{split} \delta \leq \int _ X\mathrm{MA}_\theta (h_ j) & \leq b^ n\int _{\{ w\leq -k\} }\mathrm{MA}_\theta (w_ j)\\ & \leq b^ n\left(V-\int _{\{ w>-k\} }\mathrm{MA}_\theta (w)\right). \end{split} \]

Here the first inequality uses $h_ j\sim v$, and the last uses $w_ j\sim v$. Letting $k\to \infty $ contradicts (30.1), since the finite non-pluripolar measure $\mathrm{MA}_\theta (w)$ gives no mass to the pole set of $w$. This proves the claim. Consequently $h\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, and $b^{-1}h+(1-b^{-1})v\leq w$. If $w\leq u+C$, then $h-bC\in \mathcal A_ b(u,v)$, which proves the lemma. $\square$

Proposition 30.2.2. Let $\pi :Z\to X$ be a smooth compact modification. There is a finite constant $C_\pi $, depending only on $\pi $ and $\theta $, such that, for $u,v,\delta ,D$ as in lemma 30.2.1,

\[ 0\leq \nu (u\circ \pi ,z)-\nu (v\circ \pi ,z) \leq C_\pi \min \left\{ 1,\left(\frac D\delta \right)^{1/n}\right\} \qquad (z\in Z). \]

The same estimate holds for generic Lelong numbers along every prime divisor on $Z$.

Proof. Let $C_\pi $ be the uniform bound for Lelong numbers of $\pi ^*\theta $-plurisubharmonic functions on $Z$ given by lemma 1.17.7. Since $u\preceq v$, the difference of Lelong numbers is nonnegative, and it is at most $\nu (u\circ \pi ,z)\leq C_\pi $. This proves the assertion when $D\geq \delta $.

If $D<\delta $, then by lemma 30.2.1 every $b>1$ with $b^ nD<\delta $ admits some $h\in \mathcal A_ b(u,v)$. Pulling back the defining convex inequality and taking Lelong numbers gives

\[ \nu (u\circ \pi ,z)-\nu (v\circ \pi ,z) \leq b^{-1}\bigl (\nu (h\circ \pi ,z)-\nu (v\circ \pi ,z)\bigr ) \leq b^{-1}C_\pi . \]

For $D>0$, let $b\uparrow (\delta /D)^{1/n}$; for $D=0$, let $b\to \infty $. Taking generic values along a prime divisor proves the divisorial assertion. $\square$

At zero deficit every finite stretch is possible. This leads to a second description of the relative envelope class. Fix a reference potential $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$.

Definition 30.2.3. The relative full-mass class of $\varphi $ is

\[ \mathcal E_{\mathrm{vol}}(X,\theta ,\varphi ) =\{ u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta ):u\preceq \varphi , \ \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u) =\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\varphi )\} . \]

Conversely, convex combinations turn the envelope condition into equality of volumes.

Theorem 30.2.4. Suppose $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\varphi )>0$. Then

\[ \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,\varphi ) =\mathcal E_{\mathrm{vol}}(X,\theta ,\varphi ). \]

Every member $u$ of this class also satisfies $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u) =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\varphi )$.

Proof. First let $u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,\varphi )$. For each $a\in (0,1)$, a member of $\mathcal A_ b(u,\varphi )$ with $b=(1-a)^{-1}$ gives $h\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ such that $a\varphi +(1-a)h\leq u$. By theorem 30.1.1, proposition 30.1.2 and homogeneity, for either volume,

\[ a^ n\operatorname {vol}_\theta (\varphi ) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta (a\varphi +(1-a)h) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta (u) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta (\varphi ). \]

Letting $a\to 1$ proves that both volumes of $u$ equal those of $\varphi $; this direction does not use positive lower volume.

Conversely, let $u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{vol}}(X,\theta ,\varphi )$. Then $D=0$ in lemma 30.2.1 with $v=\varphi $, so $\mathcal A_ b(u,\varphi )\neq \varnothing $ for every $b>1$. For $b=1$ the family contains $u$. This is the defining condition of $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,\varphi )$. $\square$

30.3 Domination and ordinary envelopes

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Since $u\preceq P_\theta [u]$, monotonicity gives one inequality between the volumes of a potential and those of its ordinary envelope. For the reverse inequality we identify a limit of obstacle envelopes by means of contact support and a domination principle. In the domination argument, positive lower volume guarantees that the stretching envelopes are defined. From now on we write $\mu _ w=\mathrm{MA}_\theta (w)$ for $w\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$.

Theorem 30.3.1. Let $\varphi \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ have positive lower volume, let $u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,\varphi )$ and $v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $v\preceq \varphi $, and let $0\leq c<1$. If

\[ \mathbf1_{\{ u<v\} }\mu _ u\leq c\, \mathbf1_{\{ u<v\} }\mu _ v, \]

then $u\geq v$.

Proof. Put $\ell =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\varphi )>0$ and $\mathcal E_\varphi =\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,\varphi )$. We may replace $v$ by $\max (u,v)$: by locality, the hypothesis is unchanged on $\{ u<v\} $, and by upward stability (proposition 13.4.2) the new $v$ lies in $\mathcal E_\varphi $. Thus we may assume $u\leq v$. Translate $u,v$ by the same constant so that $v\leq \varphi $, and then translate all three potentials so that $\varphi \leq 0$. The idea is to stretch $u$ away from $v$; the stretched potentials keep lower volume $\ell $, and this is incompatible with a strict inequality $u<v$ on a set of positive volume. Set

\[ q_ b=Q_ b(u,v),\qquad D_ b=\{ b^{-1}q_ b+(1-b^{-1})v=u\} . \]

Lemma 13.4.3 gives $q_ b\in \mathcal E_\varphi $ and $q_ b\leq u$. Since $b^{-1}q_ b+(1-b^{-1})v\leq u$ with contact set $D_ b$, contact support and positivity of the mixed products give

\[ b^{-n}\mu _{q_ b}+(1-b^{-1})^ n\mathbf1_{D_ b}\mu _ v \leq \mathbf1_{D_ b}\mu _ u. \]

Here $\mu _{q_ b}$ is carried by $D_ b$. Choose $b$ so large that $(1-b^{-1})^ n>c$. On $D_ b\cap \{ u<v\} $ the hypothesis bounds the right-hand side by $c\mu _ v$, and subtracting finite measures shows that $\mu _{q_ b}$ vanishes there. Thus $\mu _{q_ b}$ is carried by $D_ b\cap \{ u=v\} \subset \{ q_ b=u\} $. Since $q_ b\leq u$, ordered contact for this pair and theorem 30.2.4, which gives $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q_ b)=\ell $, now yield

30.4
\begin{equation} \ell \leq \mu _{q_ b}(X)\leq \mu _ u(\{ q_ b=u\} ). \end{equation}

Suppose now that $u<v$ somewhere. Then there is $\delta >0$ such that $\{ u<v-\delta \} $ has positive Lebesgue measure; otherwise $u\geq v$ almost everywhere, and hence everywhere by lemma 1.3.3. On this set the convex inequality gives $q_ b\leq u-(b-1)\delta $. The family $q_ b$ decreases in $b$ and is bounded above by $u$. Its limit is $-\infty $ on a set of positive measure, so it cannot be $\theta $-plurisubharmonic and is therefore identically $-\infty $. Hence theorem 1.4.2 gives $\sup _ Xq_ b\to -\infty $. The sets $\{ q_ b=u\} \subset \{ u\leq \sup _ Xq_ b\} $ then have $\mu _ u$-mass tending to zero, because $\mu _ u$ is finite and puts no mass on the pole set of $u$. This contradicts (30.4). $\square$

Corollary 30.3.2. For $u,v$ as in theorem 30.3.1 and $\lambda >0$,

\[ \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda u}\mu _ u\leq \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda v}\mu _ v \quad \Longrightarrow \quad u\geq v. \]

If $\mu _ u\leq c\mu _ v$ on $X$ for a finite $c\geq 0$, then $c\geq 1$.

Proof. Fix $\delta >0$. On $\{ u<v-\delta \} $ the weighted inequality gives $\mu _ u\leq \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda \delta }\mu _ v=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda \delta }\mu _{v-\delta }$. Theorem 30.3.1, applied to $v-\delta $ with $c=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda \delta }<1$, gives $u\geq v-\delta $; now let $\delta \downarrow 0$. If the second assertion failed, the theorem would apply with the same $c<1$ to every translate $v+A$, $A\in \mathbb R$, and would give $u\geq v+A$ for all $A$. This is impossible at a point where $u$ and $v$ are both finite. $\square$

The next lemma concerns the envelope obtained by replacing the obstacle $0$ in definition 1.19.4 by a potential $w$.

Lemma 30.3.3. Let $u,w\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $P_\theta (u,w)\not\equiv -\infty $, and define the relative obstacle envelope

\[ P_\theta [u](w)=\left(\lim _{C\to \infty }P_\theta (u+C,w)\right)^*. \]

Then

\[ \mu _{P_\theta [u](w)} \leq \mathbf1_{\{ P_\theta [u](w)=w\} }\mu _ w. \]

Proof. Set $p_ C=P_\theta (u+C,w)$ for $C\geq 0$ and $p=P_\theta [u](w)$. The envelopes $p_ C$ increase with $C$, lie between $p_0$ and $w$, and converge to $p$ in capacity by lemma 1.8.12. By the contact theorem, $\mu _{p_ C}$ is carried by $\{ p_ C=\min (u+C,w)\} $. Assigning the points where $u+C=w$ to the second term, ordered contact gives

\[ \mu _{p_ C}\leq \mathbf1_{\{ p_ C=u+C<w\} }\mu _ u +\mathbf1_{\{ p_ C=w\} }\mu _ w. \]

The first measure has total mass at most

\[ \varepsilon _ C=\mu _ u(\{ u\leq \sup _ Xw-C\} )\longrightarrow 0, \]

because $\mu _ u$ is finite and non-pluripolar. Testing against nonnegative continuous functions and applying the lower semicontinuity of lemma 6.3.1 gives $\mu _ p\leq \mu _ w$.

The bounded quasi-continuous weight $\chi =\min (1,(w-p)_+)$, defined arbitrarily on the pluripolar set where $w-p$ is undefined, vanishes on $\{ p_ C=w\} $, since $p_ C\leq p\leq w$. Thus $\int _ X\chi \, \mu _{p_ C}\leq \varepsilon _ C$. Weighted lower semicontinuity gives $\int _ X\chi \, \mu _ p=0$, so $\mu _ p$ is carried by $\{ p=w\} $. Together with $\mu _ p\leq \mu _ w$, this proves the lemma. $\square$

Theorem 30.3.4. For $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and $p=P_\theta [u]$,

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u) =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p). \]

If this volume is positive, then also

\[ \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u) =\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p),\qquad u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,p),\qquad P_\theta [p]=p. \]

Proof. Since $u\preceq p$, monotonicity gives $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)\leq \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)$ and $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)\leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)$. In particular, the lower volumes agree if $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)=0$. Suppose henceforth that $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)>0$. Normalize $u\leq 0$, so that $u\leq p$, and choose any $w\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $w\sim p$. Fix $K\geq 0$ with $p-K\leq w\leq p+K$. The rooftop envelope $p_ C=P_\theta (u+C,w)$ satisfies $u-K\leq p_ C\leq u+C$ for each $C\geq 0$, so it is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic and equivalent to $u$. Its regularized increasing limit $r=P_\theta [u](w)$ satisfies

\[ P_\theta (u+C+K,0)-K\leq p_ C\leq w, \qquad p-K\leq r\leq w\leq p+K. \]

Indeed, the leftmost potential lies below $u+C$, and below $p-K\leq w$ because $P_\theta (u+C+K,0)\leq P_\theta [u]=p$; it is therefore a competitor for the rooftop. Letting $C\to \infty $ gives the lower bound for $r$. Thus $r\sim p$, and proposition 13.4.2 puts $r$ in $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,p)$.

By lemma 30.3.3, $\mu _ r$ vanishes on $\{ r<w\} $. Theorem 30.3.1, with coefficient $c=0$ and reference $p$, gives $r\geq w$; hence $r=w$. Since $p_ C\to r$ in capacity, lower semicontinuity of the total mass (lemma 6.3.1) and $p_ C\sim u$ yield

\[ \int _ X\mu _ w\leq \liminf _{C\to \infty }\int _ X\mu _{p_ C} \leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u). \]

Taking the supremum over $w\sim p$ gives $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)\leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)$, and hence equality of the upper volumes. Theorem 30.2.4 then gives $u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,p)$ and equality of the lower volumes. Finally, proposition 13.4.5 gives $P_\theta [p]=P_\theta [u]=p$. $\square$

The ordinary envelope $P_\theta [u]$ is in general less singular than $u$. When the pole set of $u$ is a compact set of Hausdorff dimension zero, however, the full local Monge–Ampère product of theorem 1.6.9 is defined, and the ordinary envelope preserves its restriction to the pole set. Bounded mass controls the local approximations in every degree, and the products of lower degree put no mass on the pole set.

Theorem 30.3.5. Let $\omega $ be a Hermitian form on $X$, and let $S\subset X$ be a compact set of Hausdorff dimension zero. Suppose $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\omega )$ is locally bounded on $X\setminus S$, has pole set $\{ u=-\infty \} =S$, and has positive lower singularity volume. Put $p=P_\omega [u]$ and $p_ m=P_\omega (u+m,0)$ for real $m>0$. For $v=u$, $v=p$ and $v=p_ m$, the weak limit

\[ \Phi _ v=\lim _{k\to \infty } (\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\max (v,-k))^ n \]

of the bounded truncations exists and is a finite positive measure, which we call the full Monge–Ampère measure of $v$. On each coordinate chart, its restriction $\mathbf1_ S\Phi _ v$ equals $\mathbf1_ S(\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(v+q))^ n_{\mathrm{full}}$ for every smooth function $q$ making $v+q$ plurisubharmonic there; in particular, it does not depend on $q$. Moreover,

\[ \mathbf1_ S\Phi _ p=\mathbf1_ S\Phi _ u, \]

and $\Phi _{p_ m}\to \Phi _ p$ weakly as $m\to \infty $.

Proof. Step 1: the rooftops. Adding a constant to $u$ changes neither $p$ nor the family $(p_ m)_{m>0}$, apart from a shift of the parameter $m$. We may therefore assume $u\leq 0$. Then

\[ u\leq p_ m\leq \min (u+m,0),\qquad p_ m\uparrow p, \]

where the increasing limit is understood after upper semicontinuous regularization. Thus $p_ m\sim u$, and $p_ m$ and $p$ are locally bounded off $S$. By theorem 30.3.4, $p$ is an ordinary model with the same volumes as $u$. By lemma 1.8.12, $p_ m\to p$ in capacity. On compact subsets of $X\setminus S$ the potentials have a common bounded range, so all bounded local products converge there, by the bounded product theory of section 1.6.

Step 2: full measures. Let $v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\omega )$ be locally bounded off $S$, and put $v_ k=\max (v,-k)$. On each chart choose a smooth $q$ with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}q\geq \omega $, so that the functions $v_ k+q$ are plurisubharmonic and decrease to $v+q$. Theorem 1.6.9 then gives full weak limits in every degree,

\[ W_ j(v)=\lim _{k\to \infty }(\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v_ k)^ j, \qquad \Phi _ v=\lim _{k\to \infty }(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v_ k)^ n. \]

Indeed, $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v_ k=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(v_ k+q)-\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}q$, and the powers can be expanded in the closed positive powers of $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(v_ k+q)$ and smooth forms. The local limits patch together because the approximating bounded currents are global. The limits are currents of order zero with finite mass on the compact manifold $X$, and $\Phi _ v$ is positive. Stokes' theorem for the bounded potentials $v_ k$ gives $\int _ XW_ n(v)=0$. Consequently

30.5
\begin{equation} J(v):=\Phi _ v(X) =\int _ X\omega ^ n+ \sum _{j=1}^{n-1}\binom nj \int _ XW_ j(v)\wedge \omega ^{n-j}. \end{equation}

The measure $\Phi _ v$ equals $\mathrm{MA}_\omega (v)$ off $S$. Every closed positive power of degree less than $n$ in this expansion has coefficient measures vanishing on $S$, by corollary 1.13.2. Thus only the top closed power contributes to $\mathbf1_ S\Phi _ v$. This identifies $\mathbf1_ S\Phi _ v$ with $\mathbf1_ S(\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(v+q))^ n_{\mathrm{full}}$ and proves independence of $q$. These assertions are local on each chart and do not require a single chart containing $S$. Finally, $J(v)\leq v_+(\omega )$, since every truncation $v_ k$ has mass at most $v_+(\omega )$.

Step 3: convergence of the lower-degree powers. We need local trace bounds for the closed powers of $p_ m$ that are uniform in $m$. Fix a relatively compact coordinate neighborhood and write $\kappa =\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}|z|^2$. Choose $B>0$ with $B\kappa \geq \omega $ there, and $A>1$ with $A\omega -B\kappa \geq \kappa $. For $p_{m,k}=\max (p_ m,-k)$, the closed current $T_{m,k}=B\kappa +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}p_{m,k}$ is positive and

\[ Q_{m,k}:=A\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}p_{m,k}\geq T_{m,k}+\kappa . \]

Expanding $Q_{m,k}^ n$ with the smooth positive difference $Q_{m,k}-T_{m,k}\geq \kappa $ gives, for $1\leq j\leq n$,

\[ \binom nj T_{m,k}^ j\wedge \kappa ^{n-j} \leq Q_{m,k}^ n, \qquad \int _ XQ_{m,k}^ n\leq A^ n v_+(\omega ). \]

The last bound holds because $Q_{m,k}=A(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(p_{m,k}/A))$ and $p_{m,k}/A$ is a bounded $\omega $-plurisubharmonic function. Letting $k\to \infty $ gives trace bounds, independent of $m$, for every full closed power of $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(p_ m+B|z|^2)$.

For $j<n$, every subsequential weak limit of these closed positive $(j,j)$-currents puts no mass on $S$, again by corollary 1.13.2. Off $S$ the bounded capacity convergence identifies the limit with the corresponding full power of $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(p+B|z|^2)$, which also puts no mass on $S$. Hence the limit is unique. Expanding back in the smooth correction and using a finite coordinate cover yields

\[ W_ j(p_ m)\longrightarrow W_ j(p),\qquad 1\leq j<n. \]

Hence (30.5) gives $J(p_ m)\to J(p)$.

Step 4: the residual measures. Let $R=\mathbf1_ S\Phi _ u$. Since $p_ m-u$ is bounded, the equality case of lemma 1.6.10, applied on each chart after a common smooth correction, gives $\mathbf1_ S\Phi _{p_ m}=R$ for every $m$. The total masses $J(p_ m)$ are bounded by $v_+(\omega )$, so along any sequence $m_ i\to \infty $ a subsequence of $\Phi _{p_{m_ i}}$ converges weakly to a measure $\eta $. Since $\Phi _{p_ m}-R$ is positive, so is $\eta -R$. Off $S$, $\eta $ equals $\mathrm{MA}_\omega (p)$, by the bounded convergence of Step 1, and its total mass is $\lim _ mJ(p_ m)=J(p)$ by Step 3. The measure $\Phi _ p$ has the same total mass and the same restriction to $X\setminus S$, so

\[ \Phi _ p(S)=\eta (S)\geq R(S). \]

On the other hand, $u\leq p$, so the inequality in lemma 1.6.10 gives $\mathbf1_ S\Phi _ p\leq R$ on each chart. Together with the previous inequality, this forces $\Phi _ p(S)=R(S)$, hence $\mathbf1_ S\Phi _ p=R$, which is the asserted equality of residual measures. It also gives $\mathbf1_ S\eta =R$, since $\eta -R\geq 0$ and $\eta (S)=R(S)$. As $\eta $ and $\Phi _ p$ also agree off $S$, we conclude that $\eta =\Phi _ p$. Every weak subsequential limit of $\Phi _{p_ m}$ is therefore $\Phi _ p$, which proves the convergence $\Phi _{p_ m}\to \Phi _ p$. $\square$

Corollary 30.3.6. If $\varphi $ is an ordinary model of positive lower volume, then

\[ \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,\varphi ) =\mathcal E_{\mathrm{vol}}(X,\theta ,\varphi ) =\{ u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta ):P_\theta [u]=\varphi \} . \]

Proof. The first equality is theorem 30.2.4. Membership in $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,\varphi )$ implies $P_\theta [u]=P_\theta [\varphi ]=\varphi $ by proposition 13.4.5. Conversely, if $P_\theta [u]=\varphi $, then theorem 30.3.4 puts $u$ in $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,\varphi )$. $\square$

30.4 Strict barriers and background variation

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Positive lower volume permits a small stretch away from any less singular potential, and the admissible stretch depends only on the lower volume and a mass bound. Mixing the stretched potential with a strict potential produces a strict potential lying below the original one. The same bound for mixed masses controls the change of the lower volume under a small smooth perturbation of the background. Throughout this section, $\omega $ is a fixed Hermitian form on $X$.

Lemma 30.4.1. Let $u,r\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, with $u\leq r$, and assume $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)>0$. For a Hermitian form $\Omega \geq \theta $, set

\[ b_0=1+\frac{\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)}{2^{n+1}n\, v_+(\Omega )}. \]

Then $\mathcal A_ b(u,r)\neq \varnothing $, so that $Q_ b(u,r)$ is defined, for $1\leq b\leq b_0$, and

\[ \lim _{b\downarrow 1}\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (Q_ b(u,r)) =\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u). \]

Proof. Put $K=v_+(\Omega )$. Since $0<\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)\leq K$, we have $1<b_0\leq 2$. After a common translation we may assume $u\leq r\leq 0$. Suppose, to reach a contradiction, that $\mathcal A_ b(u,r)=\varnothing $ for some $1<b\leq b_0$. Set

\[ u_ j=\max (u,r-j),\qquad v_ j=u+(b-1)u_ j, \qquad q_ j=P_\theta (v_ j-(b-1)r). \]

Here, as for stretching envelopes, a competitor $g$ for $q_ j$ is required to satisfy $g+(b-1)r\leq v_ j$, which is meaningful also at common poles. Off the poles the obstacle $v_ j-(b-1)r=u+(b-1)(u_ j-r)$ lies between $u-(b-1)j$ and $u$. Hence $q_ j$ is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic and $q_ j\sim u$. As $j$ increases, $q_ j$ decreases. A $\theta $-plurisubharmonic decreasing limit would belong to $\mathcal A_ b(u,r)$, so the limit is identically $-\infty $, and $\sup _ Xq_ j\to -\infty $ by theorem 1.4.2.

Let $C_ j=\{ q_ j+(b-1)r=v_ j\} $. By the contact theorem, $\mu _{q_ j}$ is carried by $C_ j$. The current $b\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(q_ j+(b-1)r)$ equals $(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}q_ j)+(b-1)(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}r)\geq \theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}q_ j$. Positivity of the mixed products and ordered contact in background $b\theta $ therefore give

\[ \mu _{q_ j}\leq \mathbf1_{C_ j}\bigl ((\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)+(b-1)(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u_ j)\bigr )^ n. \]

On $C_ j$ we have $q_ j=u+(b-1)(u_ j-r)\geq bu$, because $u_ j\geq u$ and $r\leq 0$. Fix $k>0$. For all sufficiently large $j$, $q_ j\leq -bk$ everywhere, so $C_ j\subset \{ u\leq -k\} $, and consequently

30.6
\begin{equation} \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u) \leq \mu _{q_ j}(X) \leq \int _{\{ u\leq -k\} }\bigl ((\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)+(b-1)(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u_ j)\bigr )^ n. \end{equation}

To estimate the right-hand side, note that for any $s,t\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and $0\leq i\leq n$, positivity of the non-pluripolar mixed products and proposition 6.5.3 imply

\[ \begin{aligned} & \binom ni\int _ X(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}s)^{n-i}\wedge (\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}t)^ i\\ & \quad \leq \binom ni\int _ X(\Omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}s)^{n-i}\wedge (\Omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}t)^ i\\ & \quad \leq 2^ n\int _ X\mathrm{MA}_\Omega ((s+t)/2)\leq 2^ nK. \end{aligned} \]

The second inequality follows by expanding $(\Omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\frac{s+t}2)^ n$; for the possibly unbounded average, apply this expansion to its truncations at constant levels, which remain $\Omega $-plurisubharmonic. Expanding the integrand in (30.6), and using $(b-1)^ i\leq b-1$ for $i\geq 1$, gives

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u) \leq \mu _ u(\{ u\leq -k\} )+n2^ nK(b-1). \]

The measure $\mu _ u$ is finite and puts no mass on the pole set of $u$, so the first term tends to zero as $k\to \infty $. By the choice of $b_0$, the remaining error is at most half of $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)>0$, a contradiction. Thus $\mathcal A_ b(u,r)\neq \varnothing $ for $1<b\leq b_0$; for $b=1$ the family contains $u$, and $Q_1(u,r)=u$.

Put $h_0=Q_{b_0}(u,r)$ and, for $1<b\leq b_0$, $t=(b-1)/(b_0-1)\in (0,1]$. The inequality $h_0\leq b_0u-(b_0-1)r$ shows that $(1-t)u+th_0$ is a competitor for $Q_ b(u,r)$, and $Q_ b(u,r)\leq bu-(b-1)r\leq u$. Hence

\[ (1-t)u+th_0\leq Q_ b(u,r)\leq u. \]

Addition, homogeneity and volume monotonicity give

\[ (1-t)^ n\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u) \leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (Q_ b(u,r)) \leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u). \]

Letting $b\downarrow 1$ proves the limit. Undoing the common translation changes neither the volumes nor the nonemptiness of the competitor families. $\square$

Corollary 30.4.2. If $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ has positive lower volume, then there are $w\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and $\varepsilon >0$ with

\[ w\leq u,\qquad \theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\geq \varepsilon \omega . \]

Proof. Translate $u$ so that $u\leq V_\theta $. By bigness, there are $\rho \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and $\delta >0$ with $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho \geq \delta \omega $; after a translation, $\rho \leq V_\theta $. By lemma 30.4.1 with $r=V_\theta $, there is $b>1$ for which $h=Q_ b(u,V_\theta )$ is defined. Put

\[ w=b^{-1}h+(1-b^{-1})\rho . \]

Since $b^{-1}h+(1-b^{-1})V_\theta \leq u$ and $\rho \leq V_\theta $, we have $w\leq u$; moreover $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\geq (1-b^{-1})\delta \omega $. Undoing the translation of $u$ by the same translation of $w$ gives the assertion with $\varepsilon =(1-b^{-1})\delta $. $\square$

Proposition 30.4.3. Assume in addition that $\theta \geq 0$. If $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)>0$, then $P_{\varepsilon \theta }(u)\not\equiv -\infty $ for some $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$.

Proof. By corollary 30.4.2, choose $w\leq u$ and $\delta >0$ with $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\geq \delta \omega $. Choose $C>0$ with $\theta \leq C\omega $, and $0<t<\min (1,\delta /C)$. Then

\[ (1-t)\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\geq (\delta -tC)\omega \geq 0. \]

Thus $w$ is a competitor for $P_{(1-t)\theta }(u)$, and $\varepsilon =1-t$ has the required property. $\square$

For a reference potential $\varphi $, the positive-volume model condition reads

30.10
\begin{equation} P_\theta [\varphi ]=\varphi ,\qquad \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\varphi )>0. \end{equation}

When $\theta \geq 0$, proposition 30.4.3 shows that (30.10) implies the smaller-background model condition (13.26) of section 13.5. If moreover $v_-(\theta )>0$, the converse holds. Indeed, if $P_{\varepsilon \theta }(\varphi )\not\equiv -\infty $, then a translate of the same competitor shows that $P_{\varepsilon \theta }(\psi )\not\equiv -\infty $ for every $\psi \sim \varphi $ in $\mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, and lemma 13.5.2 gives $\int _ X\mathrm{MA}_\theta (\psi )\geq (1-\varepsilon )^ nv_-(\theta )$. Hence

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\varphi ) \geq (1-\varepsilon )^ n v_-(\theta )>0. \]

Without the lower bound $v_-(\theta )>0$, the same lemma still shows that each potential equivalent to $\varphi $ has positive mass, but not that these masses are bounded away from zero.

A small smooth increase of the background changes the lower volume by at most a linear error, and a strict potential lying below $u$ gives a multiplicative comparison for both volumes.

Proposition 30.4.4. There is $C>0$, depending only on $\theta $ and $\omega $, such that, for every $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, every $\varepsilon \in [0,1]$ and every smooth real $(1,1)$-form $\eta $ with $\theta \leq \eta \leq \theta +\varepsilon \omega $,

\[ 0\leq \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\eta (u) -\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)\leq C\varepsilon . \]

If $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)>0$, there is also $\delta >0$, depending on $u$, such that, for every $\varepsilon \geq 0$ and every smooth $\eta $ with $\theta \leq \eta \leq \theta +\varepsilon \omega $, both singularity volumes satisfy

\[ \operatorname {vol}_\theta (u) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\eta (u) \leq (1+\varepsilon /\delta )^ n\operatorname {vol}_\theta (u). \]

Proof. Choose a Hermitian form $\Omega $ with $\Omega \geq \theta $ and $\Omega \geq \omega $, and set $C=n2^ n v_+(\Omega )$. For every $v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and $1\leq k\leq n$, the mixed-mass estimate in the proof of lemma 30.4.1, applied to $s=v$ and $t=0$, together with $\omega \leq \Omega $, gives

\[ \binom nk\int _ X(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v)^{n-k}\wedge \omega ^ k \leq 2^ n v_+(\Omega ). \]

Expanding in the smooth positive perturbation and using $\varepsilon ^ k\leq \varepsilon $ yields

\[ \int _ X\mathrm{MA}_\eta (v) \leq \int _ X\mathrm{MA}_{\theta +\varepsilon \omega }(v) \leq \int _ X\mathrm{MA}_\theta (v)+C\varepsilon . \]

Each $v\sim u$ in $\mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ is also a competitor for the lower volume in background $\eta $. Taking the infimum over these $v$ proves the upper bound for $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\eta (u)-\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)$. The lower bound is background monotonicity (theorem 30.1.1).

Now assume that $u$ has positive lower volume, and choose $w\leq u$ and $\delta >0$ with $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\geq \delta \omega $ by corollary 30.4.2. The case $\varepsilon =0$ is immediate. For $\varepsilon >0$ put $t=\varepsilon /\delta $. The potential $tw$ belongs to $\mathrm{PSH}(X,t(\theta -\delta \omega ))$, and the form $\eta +t(\theta -\delta \omega )$ is big, since adding $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}((1+t)w)$ to it gives a form at least $(1+t)\delta \omega -t\delta \omega =\delta \omega $. Addition and monotonicity give

\[ \begin{split} \operatorname {vol}_\eta (u) & \leq \operatorname {vol}_{\eta +t(\theta -\delta \omega )}(u+tw)\\ & \leq \operatorname {vol}_{(1+t)\theta }((1+t)u) =(1+t)^ n\operatorname {vol}_\theta (u). \end{split} \]

Here we used $\eta +t(\theta -\delta \omega )\leq (1+t)\theta $, which holds because $\eta \leq \theta +\varepsilon \omega $, and $u+tw\leq (1+t)u$. The lower bound is again background monotonicity. $\square$

30.5 Monotone limits

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

We now show that ordinary envelopes and singularity volumes are continuous along monotone sequences whose limit has positive lower volume. For increasing sequences, contact support identifies the limit of the envelopes before its volume is known. For decreasing sequences of models, a uniform small stretch keeps a positive lower-volume bound. Normalized compactness then preserves the convex inequalities given by the deficit lemma, and strict barriers allow the background to decrease at the same time.

Theorem 30.5.1. Let $u_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ be an increasing sequence such that $u=(\lim _ j u_ j)^*$ belongs to $\mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and has positive lower volume. Then

\[ \left(\lim _ jP_\theta [u_ j]\right)^*=P_\theta [u], \qquad P_\theta [u_ j]\longrightarrow P_\theta [u] \quad \text{in capacity}, \]

and

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u_ j)\nearrow \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u),\qquad \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u_ j)\nearrow \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u). \]

For every fixed smooth compact modification $\pi :Z\to X$,

\[ \sup _{z\in Z}\bigl |\nu (u_ j\circ \pi ,z)-\nu (u\circ \pi ,z)\bigr | \longrightarrow 0. \]

Proof. Step 1: the envelopes. Translate so that $u\leq 0$, and set $p_ j=P_\theta [u_ j]$, $p=P_\theta [u]$ and $s=(\lim _ jp_ j)^*$. The $p_ j$ increase, and $u\leq s\leq p\leq V_\theta $. By theorem 30.3.4, the lower volume of $p$ is positive and $u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,p)$. Upward stability (proposition 13.4.2) puts $s$ in the same class.

The ordinary envelope can use $V_\theta $ in place of zero as obstacle (see the remark after definition 1.19.4); that is, $p_ j=P_\theta [u_ j](V_\theta )$. Hence lemma 30.3.3 gives

\[ \mu _{p_ j}\leq \mathbf1_{\{ p_ j=V_\theta \} }\mu _{V_\theta }. \]

The fixed bounded quasi-continuous weight $\chi =\min (1,(p-s)_+)$, defined arbitrarily on the pluripolar set where $p-s$ is undefined, vanishes on $\{ p_ j=V_\theta \} $, because $p_ j\leq s\leq p\leq V_\theta $. Since $p_ j\to s$ in capacity by lemma 1.8.12, lemma 6.3.1 gives $\int _ X\chi \, \mu _ s=0$. Thus $\mu _ s$ vanishes on $\{ s<p\} $, and theorem 30.3.1, with reference $p$ and coefficient $c=0$, yields $s=p$. This proves the assertions about the envelopes.

Step 2: the upper volumes of the envelopes. Choose any $w\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $w\sim p$, and $K\geq 0$ with $p-K\leq w\leq p+K$. Define $r_ j=P_\theta [u_ j](w)$; this is meaningful because $u_ j-K\leq p-K\leq w$, so that $P_\theta (u_ j,w)\not\equiv -\infty $. For $C\geq 0$ the competitors compare as follows:

\[ P_\theta (u_ j+C+K,0)-K \leq P_\theta (u_ j+C,w)\leq p_ j+K. \]

The leftmost potential lies below $u_ j+C$ and below $p_ j-K\leq p-K\leq w$. For the upper bound, note that the middle term minus $K$ lies below $w-K\leq p\leq 0$ and is more singular than $u_ j$, so it is at most $p_ j$. Taking the increasing limit in $C$ gives

\[ p_ j-K\leq r_ j\leq p_ j+K,\qquad r_ j\leq w. \]

The $r_ j$ increase with $j$ and converge in capacity to $r=(\lim _ jr_ j)^*$, and $p-K\leq r\leq w\leq p+K$. Thus $r\sim p$, so $r\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,p)$. Lemma 30.3.3 gives $\mu _{r_ j}\leq \mathbf1_{\{ r_ j=w\} }\mu _ w$, and weighted lower semicontinuity with the weight $\min (1,(w-r)_+)$ shows that $\mu _ r$ vanishes on $\{ r<w\} $. Domination with reference $p$ gives $r=w$. Therefore

\[ \int _ X\mu _ w\leq \liminf _ j\int _ X\mu _{r_ j} \leq \lim _ j\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p_ j), \]

where $r_ j\sim p_ j$ justifies the last inequality. Taking the supremum over $w\sim p$ and using volume monotonicity proves

\[ \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p_ j)\nearrow \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p). \]

Step 3: the volumes of $u_ j$. Put $\delta =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)>0$ and $D_ j=\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)-\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p_ j)$. Since $D_ j\to 0$, we can choose $b_ j>1$ with $b_ j\to \infty $ and $b_ j^ nD_ j<\delta $ for all large $j$. With $a_ j=1-b_ j^{-1}$, lemma 30.2.1 gives $h_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ such that

\[ a_ jp+(1-a_ j)h_ j\leq p_ j \]

for all sufficiently large $j$. Addition, homogeneity and monotonicity of the lower volume, together with theorem 30.3.4, give

\[ a_ j^ n\delta \leq \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p_ j) =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u_ j)\leq \delta . \]

Since $a_ j\to 1$, this proves the convergence of the lower volumes, and shows that $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u_ j)>0$ for all large $j$. For such $j$, theorem 30.3.4 identifies the upper volumes of $u_ j$ and $p_ j$, and also those of $u$ and $p$; Step 2 therefore gives the asserted upper-volume limit. Finally, proposition 30.2.2, applied to $u_ j\preceq u$, gives the uniform convergence of Lelong numbers on each fixed modification. $\square$

For the results on decreasing sequences, fix smooth real $(1,1)$-forms $\theta _ j$ with $\theta \leq \theta _{j+1}\leq \theta _ j$ that converge uniformly to $\theta $; the constant sequence $\theta _ j=\theta $ is allowed. The proofs first treat a fixed background, using normalized auxiliary potentials, and then handle the varying forms by means of corrected envelopes of tail suprema.

Theorem 30.5.2. Suppose that $q_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta _ j)$ are ordinary models, $q_ j=P_{\theta _ j}[q_ j]$, decreasing to $q\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$. Then $P_\theta [q]=q$ and

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(q_ j)\searrow \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q). \]

If $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)>0$, then also

\[ \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(q_ j)\searrow \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q), \]

and, for every fixed smooth compact modification $\pi :Z\to X$,

\[ \sup _{z\in Z}\bigl |\nu (q_ j\circ \pi ,z)-\nu (q\circ \pi ,z)\bigr | \longrightarrow 0. \]

In this case all generic divisorial Lelong numbers converge.

Proof. Step 1: the fixed point and positivity of the limit volume. Since $q\leq q_ j\leq 0$, monotonicity of the envelope in the background and in the potential gives $q\leq P_\theta [q]\leq P_{\theta _ j}[q]\leq P_{\theta _ j}[q_ j]=q_ j$. Letting $j\to \infty $ proves $P_\theta [q]=q$. By theorem 30.1.1, the lower volumes $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(q_ j)$ decrease; let $\ell $ be their limit. Monotonicity also gives $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)\leq \ell $, so the lower-volume assertion holds if $\ell =0$.

Suppose $\ell >0$. We show that then $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)>0$; the lower-volume assertion will then follow from the volume limits proved below for limits of positive lower volume. Choose a Hermitian form $\Omega \geq \theta _1$, put $K=v_+(\Omega )$, and choose

\[ b=1+\frac{\ell }{2^{n+1}nK},\qquad a=1-b^{-1}. \]

Since $\Omega \geq \theta _ j$ and $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(q_ j)\geq \ell $, lemma 30.4.1, applied in background $\theta _ j$ with $r=V_{\theta _ j}$, shows that $Q_ b(q_ j,V_{\theta _ j})$ is defined for every $j$, with the same $b$. Put $h_ j=P_{\theta _ j}[Q_ b(q_ j,V_{\theta _ j})]$. For each competitor $s$ for this envelope ($s\leq 0$ and $s\preceq Q_ b(q_ j,V_{\theta _ j})$), the potential $aV_{\theta _ j}+(1-a)s$ lies below zero and is more singular than $aV_{\theta _ j}+(1-a)Q_ b(q_ j,V_{\theta _ j})\leq q_ j$. Since $q_ j=P_{\theta _ j}[q_ j]$, it lies below $q_ j$, and taking the envelope gives

\[ aV_{\theta _ j}+(1-a)h_ j\leq q_ j. \]

The functions $h_ j$ have supremum zero and are $\Omega $-plurisubharmonic. Normalized compactness and the uniform convergence of the backgrounds give a subsequential $L^1$ limit $h\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$. Also $V_{\theta _ j}\searrow V_\theta $, since the decreasing limit is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic and lies between $V_\theta $ and zero. Passing to the limit in the convex inequality and using lemma 1.3.3 gives

\[ aV_\theta +(1-a)h\leq q. \]

Moreover, $v_-(\Omega )=\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\Omega (0)\geq \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _1}(q_1)\geq \ell >0$ by volume monotonicity. By bigness, choose $\rho \leq V_\theta $ with $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho \geq \kappa \Omega $ for some $\kappa >0$. Proposition 30.1.2, applied to $0\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\kappa \Omega )$ and $\rho \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta -\kappa \Omega )$, gives $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\rho )\geq \kappa ^ n v_-(\Omega )$. Monotonicity and addition applied to the convex combination above now yield

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q) \geq a^ n\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (V_\theta ) \geq (a\kappa )^ n v_-(\Omega )>0. \]

It remains to prove the volume limits when $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)>0$.

Step 2: a fixed background. Suppose first that $\theta _ j=\theta $ for every $j$. Put

\[ \delta =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)>0, \qquad L=\lim _ j\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q_ j), \qquad e_ k=\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q_ k)-L. \]

The limit exists because the upper volumes decrease, by theorem 30.1.1, and are finite, by bounded mass; thus $e_ k\searrow 0$. After discarding finitely many indices, choose $b_ k>1$ tending to infinity with $b_ k^ n e_ k<\delta $, and set $a_ k=1-b_ k^{-1}$.

For every $j\geq k$, the upper-volume deficit $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q_ k)-\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q_ j)$ is at most $e_ k$, whereas the lower volume of $q_ k$ is at least $\delta $. Thus lemma 30.2.1 gives $h_{k,j}\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with

\[ a_ kq_ k+(1-a_ k)h_{k,j}\leq q_ j. \]

Since $q_ k\leq 0$ and $q_ j=P_\theta [q_ j]$, combining $q_ k$ with the competitors for $P_\theta [h_{k,j}]$, as in the proof of proposition 13.4.5, gives

\[ a_ kq_ k+(1-a_ k)P_\theta [h_{k,j}]\leq q_ j. \]

The potentials $P_\theta [h_{k,j}]$ have supremum zero. For fixed $k$, normalized compactness gives a subsequential $L^1$ limit $h_ k$ as $j\to \infty $. Passing to the limit and applying lemma 1.3.3 gives

\[ a_ kq_ k+(1-a_ k)h_ k\leq q. \]

For either volume, homogeneity, addition and monotonicity now yield

\[ a_ k^ n\operatorname {vol}_\theta (q_ k) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta \bigl (a_ kq_ k+(1-a_ k)h_ k\bigr ) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta (q) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta (q_ k). \]

Letting $k\to \infty $ proves both volume limits in the fixed background.

Step 3: varying backgrounds. Fix a Hermitian form $\omega $. By corollary 30.4.2, choose $w\leq q$ and $d>0$ with $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\geq d\omega $. Choose $\varepsilon _ j>0$ decreasing to zero with $\theta _ j\leq \theta +\varepsilon _ j\omega $, and put

\[ c_ j=\frac d{d+\varepsilon _ j},\qquad f_ j=c_ jq_ j+(1-c_ j)w, \]
\[ F_ j=\left(\sup _{k\geq j}f_ k\right)^*,\qquad r_ j=P_\theta [F_ j]. \]

The convex combination $f_ j$ is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic, because its background is bounded above by $\theta $:

\[ c_ j\theta _ j+(1-c_ j)(\theta -d\omega )\leq \theta . \]

Since $w\leq q\leq q_ k$, we have $f_ k\leq q_ k\leq q_ j$ for $k\geq j$, and upper regularization gives $F_ j\leq q_ j$. Also $f_ k\to q$ in $L^1$, so a subsequence converges almost everywhere, and $q\leq F_ j$ by lemma 1.3.3.

Background monotonicity now gives

\[ q\leq r_ j=P_\theta [F_ j]\leq P_{\theta _ j}[F_ j] \leq P_{\theta _ j}[q_ j]=q_ j. \]

The $r_ j$ decrease to $q$. Their lower volumes are at least $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)>0$, so theorem 30.3.4 makes them ordinary models, and Step 2 applies to the sequence $r_ j$ in the fixed background $\theta $. On the other hand, addition (with $\eta =(1-c_ j)(\theta -d\omega )$) and background monotonicity give, for either volume,

\[ \operatorname {vol}_\theta (q) \leq \operatorname {vol}_{\theta _ j}(q_ j) \leq c_ j^{-n}\operatorname {vol}_\theta (f_ j) \leq c_ j^{-n}\operatorname {vol}_\theta (r_ j). \]

Since $c_ j\to 1$, Step 2 shows that the outer terms tend to the same limit. The volumes $\operatorname {vol}_{\theta _ j}(q_ j)$ decrease by monotonicity in both background and potential, which proves the asserted decreasing convergence.

Step 4: Lelong numbers. By proposition 30.4.4, the volumes of the fixed potential $q$ in background $\theta _ j$ tend to its volumes in background $\theta $. Hence the upper-volume deficit between $q$ and $q_ j$, measured in $\theta _ j$, tends to zero, while the lower volume of $q_ j$ is at least $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)>0$. Apply proposition 30.2.2 in each background $\theta _ j$. Since $\theta _ j\leq \theta _1$, its proof works with one Lelong bound $C_\pi $ for $\pi ^*\theta _1$, independent of $j$. This proves the uniform convergence on each fixed modification, and taking generic values gives the divisorial assertion. $\square$

For an arbitrary decreasing sequence, convergence of the ordinary envelopes is equivalent to continuity of the upper volumes.

Proposition 30.5.3. Suppose that $u_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta _ j)$ decrease to $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, and that $u$ has positive lower volume. Then

\[ P_{\theta _ j}[u_ j]\searrow P_\theta [u] \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(u_ j)\searrow \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u). \]

When these conditions hold, the lower volumes converge to that of $u$ in background $\theta $, and the Lelong numbers converge uniformly on every fixed smooth compact modification.

Proof. Normalize $u_ j\leq 0$ by a common translation. Set $p_ j=P_{\theta _ j}[u_ j]$, $p=P_\theta [u]$ and $q=\lim _ jp_ j$. Since $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(u_ j)\geq \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)>0$, theorem 30.3.4 in background $\theta _ j$ shows that every $p_ j$ is an ordinary model with the same volumes as $u_ j$. The envelopes decrease, and the uniform convergence of the backgrounds gives $q\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$. Since $u\leq p\leq q$, the limit $q$ has positive lower volume, and theorem 30.5.2 gives, for either volume,

\[ \operatorname {vol}_\theta (q) =\lim _ j\operatorname {vol}_{\theta _ j}(p_ j) =\lim _ j\operatorname {vol}_{\theta _ j}(u_ j), \qquad P_\theta [q]=q. \]

If $q=p$, these equalities and theorem 30.3.4 show that both volumes of $u_ j$ converge to those of $u$; in particular the upper volumes do. Conversely, if the upper volumes converge, then $u$ and $q$ have the same upper volume, so $u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{vol}}(X,\theta ,q)$. By corollary 30.3.6, $P_\theta [u]=q$, that is, $q=p$. For the Lelong assertion, proposition 30.4.4 shows that the upper-volume deficit between $u$ and $u_ j$ in background $\theta _ j$ tends to zero. Apply proposition 30.2.2 with the common lower bound $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)>0$ and the uniform Lelong bound for $\pi ^*\theta _1$. $\square$

Corollary 30.5.4. For every $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$,

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(u)\searrow \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u). \]

If this limiting volume is positive, then also

\[ \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(u)\searrow \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u),\qquad P_{\theta _ j}[u]\searrow P_\theta [u]. \]

Proof. Fix a Hermitian form $\omega $. Uniform convergence gives $\theta _ j\leq \theta +\varepsilon _ j\omega $ with $\varepsilon _ j\to 0$, so the volume limits follow from the two estimates in proposition 30.4.4; monotonicity makes them decreasing. In the positive-lower-volume case, apply proposition 30.5.3 to the constant sequence $u_ j=u$. $\square$

As an application, we show that trace envelopes realize their trace data exactly. Let $Y\subset X$ be a connected compact complex submanifold of positive dimension, and let $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ satisfy $\nu (u,Y)=0$. We use the notation of chapter 19: the restricted multiplier ideals $\mathfrak a_ m(u;Y)=\mathcal I(mu)\mathcal O_ Y$, the divisorial trace data $\tau _{Y,u}(E)=\lim _ mm^{-1}\operatorname {ord}_ E\mathfrak a_ m(u;Y)$ of theorem 19.1.1, the restrictions $u_ m|_ Y$ of the analytic approximants $u_ m$ of $u$, and, for a smooth real $(1,1)$-form $\Omega $ on $Y$, the trace envelope $\mathcal T_\Omega (u;Y)$ of (19.7). The latter is the largest nonpositive $\Omega $-plurisubharmonic function on $Y$ whose divisorial Lelong numbers dominate $\tau _{Y,u}$. A majorant in a smaller background gives the trace envelope positive lower volume, so theorem 30.5.2 applies on $Y$.

Corollary 30.5.5. Suppose that $Y$ has the bounded mass property. Choose a Hermitian form $\Omega _0\geq \theta |_ Y$ on $Y$ and a Hermitian form $\Omega \geq 2\Omega _0$ on $Y$ with $v_-(\Omega )>0$. Then

\[ \nu \bigl (\mathcal T_\Omega (u;Y)\circ \pi ,E\bigr )=\tau _{Y,u}(E) \]

for every prime divisor $E$ on every smooth compact modification $\pi :Z\to Y$.

Proof. Put $T=\mathcal T_\Omega (u;Y)$ and $r=\dim Y$. Normalize the majorant $v$ of proposition 19.6.1 by $\sup _ Yv=0$. It belongs to $\mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta |_ Y)\subset \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\Omega /2)$ and is a competitor for $T$, so $v\leq T$. For every $w\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\Omega )$ with $w\sim T$, a constant translate of $v$ lies below $w$, so $P_{\Omega /2}(w)\not\equiv -\infty $. By lemma 13.5.2, applied on $Y$ with $a=1/2$,

\[ \int _ Y\mathrm{MA}_\Omega (w)\geq 2^{-r}v_-(\Omega ), \qquad \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\Omega (T) \geq 2^{-r}v_-(\Omega )>0. \]

Since $\Omega -\theta |_ Y\geq \Omega _0$ is Hermitian and the curvature errors of the approximants tend to zero, the restrictions $u_ m|_ Y$ belong to $\mathrm{PSH}(Y,\Omega )$ for all sufficiently large $m$. By theorem 19.6.2, their dyadic ordinary envelopes $q_ j=P_\Omega [u_{2^ j}|_ Y]$ decrease to $T$. Theorem 30.5.2, applied on $Y$ in the fixed background $\Omega $, and the analytic type of $u_{2^ j}|_ Y$ give

\[ \nu (T\circ \pi ,E) =\lim _ j\nu (q_ j\circ \pi ,E) =\lim _ j2^{-j}\operatorname {ord}_ E\mathfrak a_{2^ j}(u;Y) =\tau _{Y,u}(E), \]

as required. $\square$

30.5.1 Recovery of envelope systems

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

We return to the big background $\theta $ on $X$ and apply the monotone limits above to the envelope systems of chapter 25. Fix real numbers $a<c$. Recall that $\mathscr T_{a,c}(\theta )$ consists of the pointwise concave, decreasing families $\Gamma =(\Gamma _\tau )_{\tau <c}$ of ideal models in background $\theta $ that are constant for $\tau \leq a$ (definition 25.1.1). An envelope system $\boldsymbol \Gamma \in \mathscr S_{a,c}(\theta )$ is a compatible family of such curves $\Gamma ^\eta =(\Gamma ^\eta _\tau )_{\tau <c}\in \mathscr T_{a,c}(\theta +\eta )$, indexed by the Hermitian forms $\eta $ (definition 25.2.2). The map $\mathsf E$ projects a curve in background $\theta $ to a system, and $\mathsf R$ takes a system to its limiting curve $(\mathsf R\boldsymbol \Gamma )_\tau =\lim _ j\Gamma ^{h/j}_\tau $, for any Hermitian form $h$ (theorem 25.3.1). The system is recovered from its limit if $\boldsymbol \Gamma =\mathsf E\mathsf R\boldsymbol \Gamma $.

Compatibility fixes every local divisorial Lelong number of the slices before the perturbation tends to zero, and recovery means that these numbers do not increase in the limit. A positive limiting lower volume rules out such an increase. Concavity propagates positivity from the constant left part to every finite parameter, so a single condition on the initial slice suffices for the whole system.

Theorem 30.5.6. Let $\boldsymbol \Gamma \in \mathscr S_{a,c}(\theta )$, fix a Hermitian form $h$, and put $\Gamma =\mathsf R\boldsymbol \Gamma $. Then

\[ \lim _{j\to \infty }\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta +h/j} (\Gamma ^{h/j}_ a) =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\Gamma _ a). \]

In particular this limit is independent of $h$. If it is positive, then $\boldsymbol \Gamma =\mathsf E(\Gamma )$, and for every $\tau <c$ both singularity volumes satisfy

\[ \operatorname {vol}_{\theta +h/j}(\Gamma ^{h/j}_\tau ) \searrow \operatorname {vol}_\theta (\Gamma _\tau ), \qquad \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\Gamma _\tau )>0. \]

Proof. Every ideal model is an ordinary model by corollary 16.2.4, and the retraction theorem gives $\Gamma ^{h/j}_\tau \searrow \Gamma _\tau $ in the decreasing backgrounds $\theta +h/j$. Thus theorem 30.5.2 proves the identity for the initial lower volumes, including the case where the limit is zero.

Suppose that this limit $\ell $ is positive. For $\tau \leq a$, the slice is $\Gamma _ a$. For $a<\tau <c$, choose $r\in (\tau ,c)$ and put $s=(\tau -a)/(r-a)$, so that $\tau =(1-s)a+sr$. Concavity gives

\[ (1-s)\Gamma _ a+s\Gamma _ r\leq \Gamma _\tau . \]

Homogeneity, proposition 30.1.2 and volume monotonicity imply

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\Gamma _\tau ) \geq (1-s)^ n\ell >0. \]

Theorem 30.5.2 now gives both volume limits for every fixed $\tau <c$.

It remains to show that the slices keep their multiplier ideals in the limit. Fix $\tau <c$ and write $q=\Gamma _\tau $, $q_ j=\Gamma ^{h/j}_\tau $ and $\theta _ j=\theta +h/j$. By proposition 30.4.4, the upper volumes of the fixed potential $q$ in the backgrounds $\theta _ j$ converge to its upper volume in $\theta $, which is the limit of the upper volumes of the $q_ j$. Consequently

\[ D_ j=\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(q_ j) -\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(q)\longrightarrow 0, \qquad \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(q_ j) \geq \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)>0. \]

After discarding finitely many indices, choose $b_ j>1$ with $b_ j\to +\infty $ and $b_ j^ nD_ j<\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(q_ j)$. Since $q\leq q_ j$, the stretching lemma lemma 30.2.1 in background $\theta _ j$ gives $v_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta _ j)$ such that

\[ (1-b_ j^{-1})q_ j+b_ j^{-1}v_ j\leq q. \]

Choose a Hermitian form $\Omega \geq \theta +h$, so that all $v_ j$ are $\Omega $-plurisubharmonic. By lemma 1.5.1, there is $\alpha >0$ such that $\mathcal I(\alpha v_ j)=\mathcal O_ X$ for all $j$; additive normalization does not change these ideals. Let $E$ be a local divisor over a coordinate neighborhood $U$, with generic Lelong number $\nu _ E$ and log discrepancy $A_ U(E)$ as in section 1.17. Lemma 1.17.8, with $m=\alpha $, gives $\nu _ E(v_ j)\leq A_ U(E)/\alpha $. Taking Lelong numbers in the convex inequality therefore yields

\[ 0\leq \nu _ E(q)-\nu _ E(q_ j) \leq \frac{A_ U(E)}{\alpha b_ j}\longrightarrow 0. \]

Compatibility gives all components the same multiplier ideals, so $\nu _ E(q_ j)$ is independent of $j$, and hence equal to $\nu _ E(q)$. Blowing up a point on a local modification converts its pointwise Lelong number into such a divisorial number; on a curve the point itself is a divisor. Thus all Lelong numbers of $q$ and $q_ j$ on all local modifications agree, and theorem 1.16.3 gives $q\sim _{\mathcal I}q_ j$. This holds at every parameter $\tau <c$, so proposition 25.3.2 proves recovery. $\square$

The lower bound $(1-s)^ n\ell $ may tend to zero as $\tau \uparrow c$; the theorem gives no positive lower bound that is uniform up to the cutoff. The hypothesis is invariant under a smooth change of background $\theta '=\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f$, $f\in C^\infty (X,\mathbb R)$, through the gauge bijection of proposition 25.4.1: the estimate there makes the transported initial slice ordinarily equivalent to $\Gamma _ a-f$, and the map $u\mapsto u-f$ preserves the curvature currents and hence both singularity volumes. The ruled-surface system of theorem 25.5.2, which is not recovered from its limit, has zero initial limiting lower volume, so the theorem does not apply to it.

30.6 Cauchy convergence of model potentials

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

The maximum of two potentials is a common upper bound for both, and the sum of the two upper-volume deficits from this maximum measures how far apart their ordinary envelopes are. A uniform positive lower-volume bound controls how this quantity changes when a third potential is introduced, and this replaces the triangle inequality in the Cauchy argument below. From now on we write $U(u)=\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)$ and $M=U(V_\theta )$; by monotonicity, $U\leq M$ on $\mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$. We also fix a smooth positive probability volume form $\mathrm{d}V$ for $L^1$ norms.

Definition 30.6.1. For $u,v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, define

\[ d_\theta (u,v)=2U(\max (u,v))-U(u)-U(v). \]

For $\delta >0$, put

\[ \mathcal M_\delta (\theta )= \{ u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta ):P_\theta [u]=u, \ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)\geq \delta \} . \]

We call $d_\theta $ the volume comparison. A sequence is $d_\theta $-Cauchy if $d_\theta (u_ j,u_ k)\to 0$ as $j,k\to \infty $, and it converges to $u$ for $d_\theta $ if $d_\theta (u_ j,u)\to 0$.

The volume comparison is finite, nonnegative and symmetric. It need not satisfy the triangle inequality (example 30.11.1), which is why we do not call it a distance. It depends only on the ordinary singularity types, since replacing either input by an equivalent one changes the maximum by a bounded amount. Monotonicity also gives

30.13
\begin{equation} |U(u)-U(v)|\leq d_\theta (u,v),\qquad u\leq v\ \Longrightarrow \ d_\theta (u,v)=U(v)-U(u). \end{equation}

In the separation result below, the restriction to positive lower volume is necessary, even for a closed positive background.

Example 30.6.2. For every $n\geq 2$ there are a closed positive $(1,1)$-form $\theta $ on the projective manifold $\mathbb P^1\times \mathbb P^{n-1}$ and two ordinary models $a\not\sim b$ in $\mathrm{PSH}(\mathbb P^1\times \mathbb P^{n-1},\theta )$ such that

\[ P_\theta [a]_{\mathcal I}=b,\qquad \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (a) =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (b)=0,\qquad d_\theta (a,b)=0. \]

For every $n\geq 3$, the same assertions hold for a suitable nonclosed positive form $\theta $ on a projective manifold of dimension $n$ with the bounded mass property.

Proof. Take the closed positive product background of example 16.6.23 on $\mathbb P^1\times \mathbb P^{n-1}$. Its models satisfy $a\leq b$ and $U(a)=U(b)=0$. Thus $\max (a,b)=b$, and the defining formula gives

\[ d_\theta (a,b)=2U(b)-U(a)-U(b)=0. \]

The other assertions are part of that example. For the nonclosed case use example 16.6.24; its models are again ordered and have zero upper volumes, so the same calculation applies. $\square$

Proposition 30.6.3. For potentials $u,v$ of positive lower volume,

\[ d_\theta (u,v)=d_\theta (P_\theta [u],P_\theta [v]),\qquad d_\theta (u,v)=0\ \Longleftrightarrow \ P_\theta [u]=P_\theta [v]. \]

There is $C_\theta >0$, depending on $\theta $ and $\mathrm{d}V$, such that, for $u,v\in \mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$,

\[ \| u-v\| _{L^1}\leq 2C_\theta \min \left\{ 1, \left(\frac{2d_\theta (u,v)}\delta \right)^{1/n}\right\} . \]

Proof. Translate the inputs below zero, and put $p=P_\theta [u]$, $q=P_\theta [v]$, $m=\max (u,v)$ and $r=P_\theta [m]$. Since $u\leq p$, $v\leq q$ and the envelope is monotone,

\[ m\leq \max (p,q)\leq r. \]

All these potentials have positive lower volume. Theorem 30.3.4 and monotonicity therefore give $U(m)=U(\max (p,q))=U(r)$, while $U(u)=U(p)$ and $U(v)=U(q)$. This proves the first identity. If $d_\theta (u,v)=0$, then $U(u)=U(v)=U(m)$, since both are at most $U(m)$. Thus $u,v\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{vol}}(X,\theta ,m)$, and theorem 30.2.4, proposition 13.4.5 give $P_\theta [u]=P_\theta [m]=P_\theta [v]$. Conversely, if $p=q$, the first identity gives $d_\theta (u,v)=d_\theta (p,p)=0$.

Now let $u,v\in \mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ and set $s=d_\theta (u,v)$. By theorem 1.4.2, there is $C_\theta $ bounding the $L^1$ norm of every $\theta $-plurisubharmonic potential with supremum zero. Since ordinary models have supremum zero, the estimate holds if $s\geq \delta /2$. Suppose $0<s<\delta /2$ and put $t=(2s/\delta )^{1/n}$. The upper-volume deficit from $u$ to $m$ is at most $s$, and the lower volume of $m$ is at least $\delta $. Thus lemma 30.2.1, with $b=t^{-1}$, gives $h\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ such that

\[ (1-t)m+th\leq u. \]

For each competitor $g$ for $P_\theta [h]$ (so $g\leq 0$ and $g\preceq h$), the potential $(1-t)m+tg$ lies below zero and is more singular than $u$. Since $u=P_\theta [u]$, it lies below $u$, and taking the envelope gives

\[ (1-t)m+tP_\theta [h]\leq u. \]

Hence $0\leq m-u\leq t(m-P_\theta [h])\leq -tP_\theta [h]$, and $\int _ X(m-u)\, \mathrm{d}V\leq tC_\theta $ because $P_\theta [h]$ has supremum zero. The same argument applies to $v$, and adding the two estimates gives the assertion, since $|u-v|\leq (m-u)+(m-v)$. If $s=0$, the argument works for every $t\in (0,1)$; let $t\downarrow 0$. $\square$

Although the triangle inequality fails, the volume comparison satisfies it up to an error that is controlled uniformly on $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$.

Proposition 30.6.4. For $u,v,w\in \mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$,

30.14
\begin{equation} d_\theta (u,w)\leq d_\theta (u,v)+d_\theta (v,w) +2nM\min \left\{ 1, \left(\frac{2d_\theta (v,w)}\delta \right)^{1/n}\right\} . \end{equation}

Consequently every $d_\theta $-convergent sequence in $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ is $d_\theta $-Cauchy, and its limit is unique.

Proof. Put $s=d_\theta (v,w)$. If $s\geq \delta /2$, the inequality follows from $d_\theta (u,w)\leq 2M$. If $0<s<\delta /2$, set $t=(2s/\delta )^{1/n}$. Lemma 30.2.1, applied to $v\preceq \max (v,w)$, gives $h\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with

\[ (1-t)\max (v,w)+th\leq v. \]

This inequality also gives $h\leq v$. Set $r=\max (u,v)$ and $z=\max (u,v,w)$. Where $z=\max (v,w)$, the inequality above bounds $(1-t)z+th$ by $v\leq r$; where $z=u$, both $u$ and $h$ lie below $r$. Thus

\[ (1-t)z+th\leq r. \]

Addition, homogeneity and volume monotonicity give

\[ U(z)-U(r)\leq \bigl (1-(1-t)^ n\bigr )U(z)\leq nMt. \]

Since $\max (u,w)\leq z$, it follows that

\[ \begin{split} d_\theta (u,w) & \leq 2U(r)+2nMt-U(u)-U(w)\\ & =d_\theta (u,v)+U(v)-U(w)+2nMt\\ & \leq d_\theta (u,v)+s+2nMt. \end{split} \]

Here the last step uses (30.13). For $s=0$, the same proof works with every $t\in (0,1)$; let $t\downarrow 0$. This proves (30.14). Applying it with the limit as the middle point shows that a convergent sequence is Cauchy. If a sequence converges to two limits, applying it with a member of the sequence as the middle point shows that the two limits have volume comparison zero; by proposition 30.6.3, they are equal. $\square$

Theorem 30.6.5. Every $d_\theta $-Cauchy sequence in $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ converges for $d_\theta $ to a unique element of $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$. It also converges to that element in $L^1(X,\mathrm{d}V)$.

Proof. The plan is to dominate a fast subsequence by the ordinary envelopes of its tail suprema; these decrease to the limit, and the stretching lemma controls the volume they add. Choose a subsequence $a_ j$ of the given sequence such that

\[ d_\theta (a_ j,a_{j+1})<\delta \, 2^{-(j+1)n} \qquad (j\geq 1). \]

For $k\geq 0$, set

\[ v_ j^ k=\max (a_ j,\ldots ,a_{j+k}),\qquad v_ j=\left(\sup _{l\geq j}a_ l\right)^*. \]

For every $l\geq j$, lemma 30.2.1 with $b=2^ l$, applied to $a_ l\preceq \max (a_ l,a_{l+1})$, gives $h_ l\leq a_ l$ satisfying

\[ (1-2^{-l})\max (a_ l,a_{l+1})+2^{-l}h_ l\leq a_ l. \]

The argument by cases in the proof of proposition 30.6.4 gives

\[ (1-2^{-l})v_ j^{l+1-j}+2^{-l}h_ l\leq v_ j^{l-j}. \]

Consequently

\[ U(v_ j^{l+1-j})-U(v_ j^{l-j})\leq nM2^{-l}. \]

Summing from $l=j$ to $j+k-1$ and then letting $k\to \infty $, using theorem 30.5.1, yields

\[ 0\leq U(v_ j)-U(a_ j)\leq nM2^{1-j}. \]

Here $v_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ has lower volume at least $\delta $, because $a_ j\leq v_ j\leq 0$; this also justifies the use of that theorem.

Put $q_ j=P_\theta [v_ j]$. By theorem 30.3.4, $q_ j$ is an ordinary model with the same two volumes as $v_ j$. The sequence $q_ j$ decreases, and each $q_ j$ has supremum zero. By normalized compactness, its decreasing limit $q$ is not identically $-\infty $, so $q\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$. By theorem 30.5.2,

\[ q=P_\theta [q],\qquad \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q) =\lim _ j\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q_ j)\geq \delta , \qquad U(q_ j)\longrightarrow U(q). \]

Both $a_ j$ and $q$ lie below $q_ j$. Hence

\[ \begin{split} d_\theta (a_ j,q) & \leq \bigl (U(q_ j)-U(a_ j)\bigr )+\bigl (U(q_ j)-U(q)\bigr )\\ & \leq nM2^{1-j}+U(q_ j)-U(q)\longrightarrow 0. \end{split} \]

To treat the whole sequence, apply (30.14) with a late member $a_ j$ of the subsequence as the middle point. The Cauchy property makes the first term small for all sufficiently late members of the original sequence, while $d_\theta (a_ j,q)$ makes the other two terms small. Thus the original sequence converges to $q$. Uniqueness follows from proposition 30.6.4, and the $L^1$ estimate in proposition 30.6.3 proves the last assertion. $\square$

The non-strict inequality $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta \geq \delta $ in the definition of $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ is essential for completeness.

Example 30.6.6. Let $\theta $ be a smooth positive $(1,1)$-form of integral one on a compact Riemann surface, and let $0<\delta <1$. The ordinary models of mass strictly greater than $\delta $ do not form a complete space for $d_\theta $.

Proof. Fix a point $x$, and let $g_ x\leq 0$ be the Green function used in theorem 19.3.1, with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}g_ x=\delta _ x-\theta $, where $\delta _ x$ is the Dirac measure at $x$. For $0\leq a<1$, put $q_ a=P_\theta [ag_ x]$. The current $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(ag_ x)$ equals $a\delta _ x+(1-a)\theta $, with non-pluripolar mass $1-a$. Since the background is closed, both singularity volumes of $ag_ x$ equal this mass, and theorem 30.3.4 gives

\[ q_ a=P_\theta [q_ a],\qquad \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q_ a)=U(q_ a)=1-a. \]

Since $g_ x\leq 0$, the potentials $q_ a$ decrease as $a$ increases, and (30.13) gives $d_\theta (q_ a,q_ b)=|a-b|$. Choose $a_ j<1-\delta $ with $a_ j\uparrow 1-\delta $. The sequence $q_{a_ j}$ is $d_\theta $-Cauchy, and every term has mass $1-a_ j>\delta $. By (30.13), any $d_\theta $-limit has upper volume $\lim _ j(1-a_ j)=\delta $. In this closed background the two volumes coincide, so no such limit has mass strictly greater than $\delta $. $\square$

30.7 Rooftops and capacity convergence

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

A small upper-volume deficit also controls the rooftop envelope of two potentials. For a finite family it suffices that the sum of the deficits stays below the lower volume of a common upper bound. Applying this criterion to stretched potentials gives increasing lower bounds for a suitable subsequence of models. Together with the decreasing upper bounds of the preceding section, they turn convergence for the volume comparison into convergence in capacity.

Lemma 30.7.1. Let $u_1,\ldots ,u_ N,w\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, with $N\geq 1$ and $u_ i\preceq w$. If

\[ \sum _{i=1}^ N\bigl (U(w)-U(u_ i)\bigr ) <\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (w), \]

then the rooftop envelope $P_\theta (u_1,\ldots ,u_ N)=P_\theta (\min _ iu_ i)$ is not identically $-\infty $.

Proof. Whether a finite rooftop envelope is identically $-\infty $ does not change under bounded changes of its obstacles. By the definition of $U(u_ i)$, we can choose $v_ i\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $v_ i\sim u_ i$ whose masses satisfy

\[ \sum _{i=1}^ N\left(U(w)-\int _ X\mu _{v_ i}\right) <\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (w). \]

Translate so that $v_ i\leq w\leq 0$, and put

\[ v_{i,k}=\max (v_ i,w-k),\qquad p_ k=P_\theta (v_{1,k},\ldots ,v_{N,k}). \]

The common competitor $w-k$ gives $w-k\leq p_ k\leq w$, so $p_ k\sim w$. The $p_ k$ decrease as $k$ increases, and a $\theta $-plurisubharmonic limit would be a competitor for $P_\theta (v_1,\ldots ,v_ N)$. Suppose, to reach a contradiction, that $P_\theta (u_1,\ldots ,u_ N)\equiv -\infty $, so that $P_\theta (v_1,\ldots ,v_ N)\equiv -\infty $ as well. Then the $p_ k$ decrease to $-\infty $, and normalized compactness gives $\sup _ Xp_ k\to -\infty $.

Fix $s>0$ and choose $k>s$ so large that $p_ k\leq -s$. Iterating proposition 13.1.5, which bounds the measure of a rooftop by the measures of its obstacles on the contact sets, and using locality on $\{ v_ i>-s\} $, we obtain

\[ \begin{split} \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (w) & \leq \int _ X\mu _{p_ k} \leq \sum _{i=1}^ N\int _{\{ v_{i,k}\leq -s\} }\mu _{v_{i,k}}\\ & \leq \sum _{i=1}^ N\left(U(w)-\int _{\{ v_ i>-s\} }\mu _{v_ i}\right). \end{split} \]

Here we used $v_{i,k}\sim w$, and $w-k<-s$, which gives $v_{i,k}=v_ i$ on $\{ v_{i,k}>-s\} =\{ v_ i>-s\} $. Let $s\to \infty $. The measures $\mu _{v_ i}$ are finite and put no mass on pluripolar sets, so the resulting inequality contradicts the choice of the $v_ i$. $\square$

To control the volume of a rooftop envelope, we first stretch each obstacle away from the maximum of the two, and then move both stretched potentials a fixed fraction back toward this maximum. By the lemma, the resulting potentials have a rooftop envelope that is not identically $-\infty $.

Proposition 30.7.2. Let $u_ j,u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, with $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)>0$, and suppose that $d_\theta (u_ j,u)\to 0$. For all sufficiently large $j$, the rooftop envelope $p_ j=P_\theta (u_ j,u)$ is not identically $-\infty $, and

\[ d_\theta (p_ j,u)\longrightarrow 0,\qquad \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p_ j) \longrightarrow \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u). \]

Proof. Put $\delta =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)$, $m_ j=\max (u_ j,u)$ and $s_ j=d_\theta (u_ j,u)$. After discarding finitely many indices, choose $b_ j\to \infty $ with $b_ j>1$ and $b_ j^ ns_ j<\delta $. The upper-volume deficits of both $u_ j$ and $u$ from $m_ j$ are at most $s_ j$, while $m_ j$ has lower volume at least $\delta $. Thus lemma 30.2.1 shows that the stretching envelopes

\[ G_ j=Q_{b_ j}(u_ j,m_ j),\qquad H_ j=Q_{b_ j}(u,m_ j) \]

are defined. Choose $a\in (0,1)$ with $2nMa<\delta $, and set

\[ g_ j=(1-a)m_ j+aG_ j,\qquad h_ j=(1-a)m_ j+aH_ j. \]

These potentials lie below $m_ j$, because $G_ j\leq u_ j$ and $H_ j\leq u$. Addition and monotonicity give

\[ 2U(m_ j)-U(g_ j)-U(h_ j) \leq 2\bigl (1-(1-a)^ n\bigr )U(m_ j) \leq 2nMa<\delta . \]

Lemma 30.7.1, with $w=m_ j$, therefore shows that $r_ j=P_\theta (g_ j,h_ j)$ is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic. For large $j$, put $t_ j=(ab_ j)^{-1}<1$. Since $r_ j\leq g_ j$ and $r_ j\leq h_ j$, the defining inequalities for $G_ j$ and $H_ j$ show that

\[ (1-t_ j)m_ j+t_ jr_ j\leq u_ j,u. \]

The left-hand side is therefore a competitor for $p_ j$, so $p_ j\not\equiv -\infty $. For either volume, addition and monotonicity give

\[ (1-t_ j)^ n\operatorname {vol}_\theta (u) \leq (1-t_ j)^ n\operatorname {vol}_\theta (m_ j) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta (p_ j) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta (u). \]

Since $t_ j\to 0$, this proves $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p_ j)\to \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)$; and since $p_ j\leq u$, (30.13) gives $d_\theta (p_ j,u)=U(u)-U(p_ j)\to 0$. $\square$

For ordinary models, finite rooftop envelopes are again ordinary models. Their normalization prevents collapse when the number of obstacles tends to infinity.

Theorem 30.7.3. Suppose $u_ j,u\in \mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ and $d_\theta (u_ j,u)\to 0$. After passing to a subsequence and relabeling it, there are ordinary model potentials $v_ j,w_ j\in \mathcal M_{\delta /2}(\theta )$ such that

\[ w_ j\leq u_ j\leq v_ j,\qquad w_ j\leq u\leq v_ j, \]

$v_ j$ decreases to $u$, $w_ j$ increases with $(\lim _ jw_ j)^*=u$, and

\[ d_\theta (v_ j,u)+d_\theta (w_ j,u)\longrightarrow 0. \]

The original sequence converges to $u$ in capacity.

Proof. Step 1: upper bounds. For large $j$, the rooftop envelope $r_ j=P_\theta (u_ j,u)$ is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic by proposition 30.7.2. It is an ordinary model: since $r_ j\leq 0$ and the envelope is monotone, $r_ j\leq P_\theta [r_ j]\leq P_\theta [u_ j]=u_ j$ and likewise $P_\theta [r_ j]\leq u$, so $P_\theta [r_ j]$ is a competitor for the rooftop and equals $r_ j$. The sequence $u_ j$ is $d_\theta $-Cauchy by proposition 30.6.4, and $d_\theta (r_ j,u)=U(u)-U(r_ j)\to 0$ by proposition 30.7.2. Choose a subsequence so that

\[ d_\theta (u_ j,u_{j+1})<\delta \, 2^{-(j+1)n},\qquad U(u)-U(r_ j)<\delta \, 4^{-(j+1)n}. \]

The tail-maximum construction in the proof of theorem 30.6.5 gives

\[ v_ j=P_\theta \left[\left(\sup _{l\geq j}u_ l\right)^*\right] \searrow u,\qquad d_\theta (v_ j,u)\longrightarrow 0. \]

Indeed, that construction produces the unique $d_\theta $-limit, which here is $u$. The models $v_ j$ satisfy $v_ j\geq u_ j$ and $v_ j\geq u$, and have lower volume at least $\delta $.

Step 2: lower bounds. By lemma 30.2.1 and the choice of the subsequence, the stretching envelopes

\[ H_ l=Q_{4^ l}(r_ l,u),\qquad K_ l=Q_{2^ l}(r_ l,u) \]

are defined. Their defining inequalities show that $(1-2^{-l})u+2^{-l}H_ l$ is a competitor for $K_ l$ and that $K_ l\leq u$. Together with addition and monotonicity, this gives

\[ (1-2^{-l})u+2^{-l}H_ l\leq K_ l\leq u, \qquad U(u)-U(K_ l)\leq nM2^{-l}. \]

Choose $j$ so large that $nM2^{1-j}<\delta $. For every $k\geq 0$, the sum of the deficits of $K_ j,\ldots ,K_{j+k}$ from $u$ is less than $\delta $, so lemma 30.7.1 shows that their rooftop envelope is not identically $-\infty $. Since $r_ l\leq u$, the stretching envelope $Q_ b(r_ l,u)$ decreases as $b$ increases; hence, for $l\geq j$,

\[ Q_{2^ j}(r_ l,u)\geq K_ l. \]

Thus $R_ j^ k=P_\theta (Q_{2^ j}(r_ j,u),\ldots ,Q_{2^ j}(r_{j+k},u))$ is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic. The convex inequality for each obstacle gives

\[ (1-2^{-j})u+2^{-j}R_ j^ k \leq w_ j^ k:=P_\theta (r_ j,\ldots ,r_{j+k})\leq u. \]

For either volume,

30.22
\begin{equation} (1-2^{-j})^ n\operatorname {vol}_\theta (u) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta (w_ j^ k) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta (u). \end{equation}

The argument used for $r_ j$ shows that $w_ j^ k$ is an ordinary model. For fixed $j$ it decreases in $k$ and has supremum zero, so normalized compactness shows that its limit $w_ j$ is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic. The lower-volume conclusion of theorem 30.5.2 passes the lower bound in (30.22) to $w_ j$. This bound is positive, so the upper-volume conclusion of the same theorem passes the bound for the upper volume as well.

Step 3: convergence. The $w_ j$ increase, since each is defined by fewer obstacles than the previous one, and $w_ j\leq r_ j\leq u_ j$, $w_ j\leq u$. After discarding finitely many indices, they have lower volume at least $\delta /2$, and (30.22) gives $d_\theta (w_ j,u)\leq nM2^{-j}$. By proposition 30.6.3, $w_ j\to u$ in $L^1$, so $(\lim _ jw_ j)^*=u$. The two monotone sequences $v_ j$ and $w_ j$ converge to $u$ in capacity by lemma 1.8.12, and the bounds $w_ j\leq u_ j\leq v_ j$ give $u_ j\to u$ in capacity along the chosen subsequence. Since every subsequence of the original sequence has a further subsequence of this kind, the original sequence converges to $u$ in capacity. $\square$

30.8 Volumes of multiplier-ideal envelopes

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Analytic regularizations recover the multiplier-ideal envelope, whose volumes may exceed those of the original potential. Continuity along decreasing models computes this excess, and a strict analytic potential absorbs the curvature error of the regularization, so that the comparison can be carried out entirely in the fixed background. For $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, write

\[ \mathcal V^-_{\mathcal I,\theta }(u) =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}),\qquad \mathcal V^+_{\mathcal I,\theta }(u) =\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}) \]

for the two volumes of its ideal envelope. They depend only on the multiplier-ideal class of $u$. By proposition 16.3.1 and the gauge invariance of singularity volumes, they are also unchanged under a smooth change of decomposition $(\theta ,u)\mapsto (\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f,u-f)$, $f\in C^\infty (X,\mathbb R)$. Moreover, proposition 16.2.4, theorem 30.1.1 give

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u) \leq \mathcal V^-_{\mathcal I,\theta }(u),\qquad \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u) \leq \mathcal V^+_{\mathcal I,\theta }(u). \]

The ideal envelope is an ordinary model by corollary 16.2.4, and its volumes are limits of the volumes of the dyadic analytic approximations.

Corollary 30.8.1. Let $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, fix a Hermitian form $h$, and let $a_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta _ j)$, $\theta _ j=\theta +\epsilon _ jh$, be the dyadic analytic regularizations of $u$ of analytic type $(2^{-j},\mathcal I(2^ ju))$, with errors $\epsilon _ j\downarrow 0$, as in (16.3). Then

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(a_ j) \searrow \mathcal V^-_{\mathcal I,\theta }(u). \]

If $\mathcal V^-_{\mathcal I,\theta }(u)>0$, then also

\[ \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta _ j}(a_ j) \searrow \mathcal V^+_{\mathcal I,\theta }(u). \]

Proof. By theorem 16.5.4, the analytic ordinary models $q_ j=P_{\theta _ j}[a_ j]$ decrease to $P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}$, and the backgrounds $\theta _ j$ decrease to $\theta $. Thus theorem 30.5.2 applies to the $q_ j$. Since $q_ j\sim a_ j$ by proposition 16.2.7, the volumes of $q_ j$ and $a_ j$ agree. $\square$

When the ideal envelope has positive lower volume, the relative full-mass class detects whether $u$ and its ideal envelope have the same upper volume. Here $P_{\theta ,F}[u]$ and $P_{\theta ,F}[u]_{\mathcal I}$ denote the ordinary and the multiplier-ideal envelopes with the constraint $h\leq F$ in place of $h\leq 0$ (definition 1.19.4 and section 16.6).

Proposition 30.8.2. Let $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, put $q=P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}$, and suppose that $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)>0$. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

  1. $P_\theta [u]=P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}$;

  2. $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)=\mathcal V^+_{\mathcal I,\theta }(u)$;

  3. $u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\theta ,q)$;

  4. $P_{\theta ,F}[u]=P_{\theta ,F}[u]_{\mathcal I}$ for every $F\in C^\infty (X,\mathbb R)$.

If they hold, then also $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u)=\mathcal V^-_{\mathcal I,\theta }(u)$.

Proof. Recall that $q$ is an ordinary model and $u\preceq P_\theta [u]\leq q$. If (i) holds, then $P_\theta [u]=q$ has positive lower volume, and theorem 30.3.4 shows that both volumes of $u$ equal those of $q$; in particular (ii) holds, together with the final assertion.

Conversely, (ii) and $u\preceq q$ give $u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{vol}}(X,\theta ,q)$, and corollary 30.3.6 gives $P_\theta [u]=q$, which is (i). The same corollary shows that (i) is equivalent to (iii). By proposition 16.6.8, (iii) implies (iv), and (iv) with $F=0$ is (i). $\square$

Under the hypotheses of the proposition, corollary 16.5.5 also identifies equality of the upper volumes with convergence of the dyadic ordinary envelopes $P_{\theta _ j}[a_ j]$ to $P_\theta [u]$. These envelopes live in the larger backgrounds $\theta _ j$. The next construction mixes the $a_ j$ with a strict analytic potential, which gives analytic approximants in the fixed background $\theta $ with the same limiting upper volume; their singularity types, however, need no longer be ordered.

Theorem 30.8.3. Let $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$, put $q=P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}$, and suppose $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)>0$. There are analytic $b_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $b_ j\to u$ in $L^1$ such that the ordinary models $r_ j=P_\theta [b_ j]$ satisfy

30.23
\begin{equation} d_\theta (r_ j,q)\longrightarrow 0,\qquad d_\theta (r_ j,u)\longrightarrow \mathcal V^+_{\mathcal I,\theta }(u) -\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (u). \end{equation}

Their lower volumes converge to $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)$, and $r_ j\to q$ in $L^1$ and capacity. In particular $d_\theta (r_ j,u)\to 0$ if and only if $P_\theta [u]=P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}$.

Proof. Step 1: the approximants. Fix a Hermitian form $h$. Bigness and theorem 1.15.1 give an analytic $\rho \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $\rho \leq 0$ and a rational $\kappa >0$ such that $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho \geq \kappa h$. Choose the dyadic regularizations $a_ j$ as in (16.3), enlarging their errors to positive rational numbers $\epsilon _ j\downarrow 0$. Put

\[ c_ j=\frac{\kappa }{\kappa +\epsilon _ j},\qquad b_ j=c_ ja_ j+(1-c_ j)\rho . \]

Then $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}b_ j\geq ((1-c_ j)\kappa -c_ j\epsilon _ j)h=0$. The coefficients are rational, so $b_ j$ has analytic singularities by proposition 1.13.1. Since $a_ j\to u$ in $L^1$ and $c_ j\to 1$, also $b_ j\to u$ in $L^1$.

Step 2: two-sided bounds. Choose $A>0$ with $\theta \leq Ah$, and set

\[ \widehat\theta _ j=\theta +(1+A/\kappa )\epsilon _ jh, \qquad t_ j=P_{\widehat\theta _ j}[a_ j]. \]

These backgrounds decrease to $\theta $ and dominate $\theta +\epsilon _ jh$, so the $a_ j$ also satisfy (16.3) with the larger errors $(1+A/\kappa )\epsilon _ j$. Theorem 16.5.4 therefore gives $t_ j\searrow q$. Moreover

\[ c_ j\widehat\theta _ j-\theta =(1-c_ j)(Ah-\theta )+c_ j\epsilon _ jh\geq 0. \]

By proposition 16.2.7, $r_ j\sim b_ j$. Hence $r_ j/c_ j$ is a nonpositive $\widehat\theta _ j$-plurisubharmonic function that is more singular than $a_ j$, because $b_ j\leq c_ ja_ j$; it is therefore a competitor for $t_ j$, and $r_ j\leq c_ jt_ j$. Also $q\leq t_ j\leq c_ jt_ j$.

For a lower bound, recall that $q\sim _{\mathcal I}u$, and $u\preceq _{\mathcal I}a_ j$ because $a_ j\geq u$. Addition and scaling preserve this order by theorem 1.16.3, lemma 1.16.4. Thus $c_ jq+(1-c_ j)\rho \preceq _{\mathcal I}b_ j$. This potential lies below zero, so envelope order and proposition 16.2.7 give

\[ c_ jq+(1-c_ j)\rho \leq P_\theta [b_ j]_{\mathcal I}=r_ j \leq c_ jt_ j. \]

Step 3: volumes. For either singularity volume, homogeneity, addition and monotonicity now yield

\[ c_ j^ n\operatorname {vol}_\theta (q) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta (r_ j) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\theta (\max (r_ j,q)) \leq c_ j^ n\operatorname {vol}_{\widehat\theta _ j}(t_ j). \]

The last comparison uses both $\theta \leq c_ j\widehat\theta _ j$ and $\max (r_ j,q)\leq c_ jt_ j$. By theorem 30.5.2, the outer terms tend to $\operatorname {vol}_\theta (q)$. This proves the convergence of both volumes of $r_ j$ and the first limit in (30.23). In particular, after discarding finitely many indices, the ordinary models $r_ j$ and $q$ have a common positive lower-volume bound, so $r_ j\to q$ in $L^1$ and in capacity by proposition 30.6.3, theorem 30.7.3.

For the second limit, translate $u$ so that $u\leq q$; this does not change $d_\theta (r_ j,u)$. Then

\[ \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (r_ j) \leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\max (r_ j,u)) \leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\max (r_ j,q)). \]

Both bounds tend to $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)$, so $d_\theta (r_ j,u)=2U(\max (r_ j,u))-U(r_ j)-U(u)$ tends to $U(q)-U(u)$, as claimed. The last equivalence follows from proposition 30.8.2. $\square$

For a Hermitian background, the simpler rescaling in corollary 16.5.6 gives decreasing analytic models, with the same limits by theorem 30.5.2. In a general big background, the strict correction above may introduce additional poles, but the volume comparison still measures exactly the excess of the ideal envelope.

Theorem 30.8.4. Let $p$ be an ordinary model of positive lower volume. Then $p=P_\theta [p]_{\mathcal I}$ if and only if there are analytic ordinary models $p_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $d_\theta (p_ j,p)\to 0$.

More generally, if $p_ j=P_\theta [p_ j]_{\mathcal I}$ and $d_\theta (p_ j,p)\to 0$, then $p=P_\theta [p]_{\mathcal I}$.

Proof. If $p=P_\theta [p]_{\mathcal I}$, apply theorem 30.8.3 with $u=p$. For the converse it suffices to prove the more general assertion, because analytic ordinary models are ideal models by proposition 16.2.7.

Put $q=P_\theta [p]_{\mathcal I}$ and $\delta =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)>0$. Choose $s_ j\in (0,1)$ tending to zero with $d_\theta (p_ j,p)<\delta s_ j^ n$, after discarding finitely many indices. For $m_ j=\max (p_ j,p)$ one has

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (m_ j)\geq \delta , \qquad U(m_ j)-U(p_ j)\leq d_\theta (p_ j,p). \]

By lemma 30.2.1 with $b=s_ j^{-1}$, there is $h_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $(1-s_ j)m_ j+s_ jh_ j\leq p_ j$. Set $H_ j=P_\theta [h_ j]_{\mathcal I}$. Since $m_ j\geq p$, monotonicity, addition and scaling of ideal envelopes (proposition 16.2.6) give

\[ (1-s_ j)q+s_ jH_ j \leq P_\theta [(1-s_ j)m_ j+s_ jh_ j]_{\mathcal I} \leq P_\theta [p_ j]_{\mathcal I}=p_ j. \]

Consequently $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p_ j)\geq (1-s_ j)^ n\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q)\geq (1-s_ j)^ n\delta $ by addition and volume monotonicity, since $q\geq p$. The models $p_ j,p$ therefore lie in $\mathcal M_{\delta /2}(\theta )$ for large $j$, so proposition 30.6.3 gives $p_ j\to p$ in $L^1$.

The $H_ j$ have supremum zero, so normalized compactness bounds $\int _ X(-H_ j)\, \mathrm{d}V$ by a constant $C_\theta $. Since $q\leq 0$, the convex inequality above gives $q-p_ j\leq s_ j(q-H_ j)\leq -s_ jH_ j$, and hence

\[ \int _ X(q-p_ j)_+\, \mathrm{d}V \leq s_ j\int _ X(-H_ j)\, \mathrm{d}V \leq C_\theta s_ j\longrightarrow 0. \]

Passing to the $L^1$ limit gives $q\leq p$ almost everywhere, and hence everywhere by lemma 1.3.3. The opposite inequality $p=P_\theta [p]\leq P_\theta [p]_{\mathcal I}=q$ holds by proposition 16.2.4, so $p=q$. $\square$

Thus the ideal models of positive lower volume are exactly the $d_\theta $-limits of analytic ordinary models. On each $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ they form a closed set for this convergence and inherit Cauchy completeness from theorem 30.6.5. The approximants constructed above have a common positive lower-volume bound, which need not equal the lower volume of the limiting model.

30.8.1 A countable divisorial positivity criterion

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

A prescribed divisorial part can determine an ideal envelope's ordinary type even when it has infinitely many components. On the projective plane with a pluriclosed background, the smooth residual determines the mass of every representative of that type. This gives a positivity test directly from divisorial orders.

Let $X=\mathbb P^2$, and let $\kappa $ be the Fubini–Study form with $\int _ X\kappa ^2=1$. Choose distinct hyperplanes $H_ j$ and coefficients $a_ j>0$ with $A=\sum _{j\geq 1}a_ j<\infty $. For unit defining sections $s_ j$, put

\[ \ell _ j(Z)=\log \frac{|s_ j(Z)|^2}{\| Z\| ^2}\leq 0, \qquad u_0=\sum _{j\geq 1}a_ j\ell _ j, \qquad u=u_0-\sup _ Xu_0. \]

Let $\theta $ be a smooth real $(1,1)$-form satisfying $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\theta =0$ and $R=\theta -A\kappa >0$.

Lemma 30.8.5. The series defines a quasi-plurisubharmonic function, and

\[ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u=S-A\kappa ,\qquad S=\sum _{j\geq 1}a_ j[H_ j],\qquad \theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u=R+S>0. \]

For every prime divisor $E$ on every smooth compact modification $\pi :Z\to X$, the convergent order profile is

\[ b(E):=\sum _{j\geq 1}a_ j\operatorname {ord}_ E(\pi ^*H_ j) =\nu (u\circ \pi ,E). \]

Proof. The Poincaré–Lelong formula gives $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\ell _ j=[H_ j]-\kappa $. Unitary invariance reduces its integral to the distribution of $x=|Z_0|^2/\| Z\| ^2$, whose density on $[0,1]$ is $2(1-x)$. Thus

\[ \int _ X\ell _ j\kappa ^2=\int _0^1 2(1-x)\log x\, \mathrm dx=-\frac32. \]

The decreasing series has finite $L^1$ norm and a common lower curvature bound $-A\kappa $. Its canonical limit is quasi-plurisubharmonic. Distributional convergence gives the current identities and strict positivity.

Fix $\pi $ and $E$, and choose a Gauduchon form $\gamma $ on the compact surface $Z$. For the tail $r_ N=\sum _{j>N}a_ j\ell _ j$ of total coefficient $A_ N=\sum _{j>N}a_ j$, the closed positive current

\[ T_ N=A_ N\pi ^*\kappa +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(r_ N\circ \pi ) \]

has trace mass $A_ N\int _ Z\pi ^*\kappa \wedge \gamma $, by distributional Stokes. The pullback of $r_ N$ is quasi-plurisubharmonic, since $\pi $ is an isomorphism on a dense open set where $r_ N$ is finite almost everywhere. Divisorial removal gives

\[ 0\leq \nu (r_ N\circ \pi ,E) \leq A_ N\frac{\int _ Z\pi ^*\kappa \wedge \gamma }{\int _ E\gamma }. \]

The denominator is positive and the ratio is finite. Finite additivity of Lelong numbers now identifies the partial profile with $\nu (u\circ \pi ,E)$ up to an error tending to zero. This proves convergence and the asserted equality. $\square$

Proposition 30.8.6. Let $v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$. The conditions

\[ \nu (v\circ \pi ,E)=b(E)\text{ for every }\pi ,E, \qquad v\sim _{\mathcal I}u \]

are equivalent. Under either condition,

\[ q=P_\theta [v]_{\mathcal I}=P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}\sim u, \]

and every $w\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $w\sim q$ satisfies

30.24
\begin{equation} \int _ X\mathrm{MA}^{\mathrm{ext}}_\theta (w) =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q) =\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (q) =\int _ XR^2>0. \end{equation}

The background is big and $X$ has the bounded mass property.

Proof. Ideal profile and envelope. Choose an integer $C>A$ with $C\kappa \geq \theta $. Both $u$ and $v$ are $C\kappa $-plurisubharmonic. The projective valuative characterization of all-weight ideal types applies in this closed background: generic orders on all smooth projective modifications recover point Lelong numbers by further point blowups, and equality of those numbers is equivalent to ideal equivalence. The stated profile therefore implies $v\sim _{\mathcal I}u$. Conversely, ideal equivalence pulls back under every compact modification by lemma 1.22.16; the local valuative criterion theorem 1.16.3 then gives equality of generic coefficients on every such modification. The common factor between growth against $\log |z|$ and the book's $\log |z|^2$ convention does not affect these equalities.

The potential $u$ has complex Hessian $S-A\kappa $. By corollary 16.2.8, its ordinary and ideal envelopes agree and are ordinarily equivalent to $u$. Ideal equivalence identifies the defining families for the two ideal envelopes in the statement, proving the assertion about $q$. The inequality $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u\geq R>0$ proves bigness. For any Hermitian form $h$ on $X$, choose $D$ with $D\kappa \geq h$. Bounded $h$-plurisubharmonic potentials are $D\kappa $-plurisubharmonic, and positivity of bounded mixed products bounds their masses by $\int _ X(D\kappa )^2=D^2$. This proves the bounded mass property.

The bounded residual. Fix $w\sim u$ in $\mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ and put $T=C\kappa +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w$. Its base divisorial coefficients are $a_ j$, so divisorial removal and passage through increasing finite sums give

\[ K=T-S=(C-A)\kappa +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h\geq 0,\qquad h=w-u. \]

The distributional difference has a canonical quasi-plurisubharmonic representative, which is bounded by ordinary equivalence and almost-everywhere order. Thus $K$ has bounded local potentials, and its trace charges no pluripolar set. The current $S$ is carried by the actual pole union $\bigcup _ jH_ j\subset \{ w=-\infty \} $. Thus $\langle T\rangle =K$. For every smooth real $(1,1)$-form $L$ with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}L=0$, distributional Stokes gives

\[ \int _ X L\wedge \langle T\rangle =(C-A)\int _ XL\wedge \kappa . \]

Closed top mass and smooth correction. Put $T_ u=C\kappa +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u=S+(C-A)\kappa $. For the finite partial sums $u_ N=\sum _{j\leq N}a_ j\ell _ j$, the closed non-pluripolar mass is $(C-\sum _{j\leq N}a_ j)^2$: outside the finite divisor union the current is the smooth residual, and the measure gives that union zero mass. Since $u_0\leq u_ N$, closed-background mass monotonicity, the closed case of theorem 30.1.1, gives

\[ \int _ X\langle T_ u^2\rangle \leq \left(C-\sum _{j\leq N}a_ j\right)^2. \]

For the reverse inequality, locally write $S=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}b$ with $b$ plurisubharmonic. The currents $(C-A)\kappa +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\max (b,-k)$ have bounded local potentials and dominate $(C-A)\kappa $. Their squared products, restricted to $\{ b>-k\} $, therefore dominate $(C-A)^2\kappa ^2$ on that set. Letting $k$ increase and using the local truncation definition gives $\langle T_ u^2\rangle \geq (C-A)^2\kappa ^2$. The two bounds prove equality of total masses. Closed mass invariance under bounded changes then gives

\[ \int _ X\langle T^2\rangle =(C-A)^2. \]

Set $L=C\kappa -\theta \geq 0$, so $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}L=0$. Expanding the bounded products of $\max (w,-k)$ in the two backgrounds and restricting to $\{ w>-k\} $ gives, in the limit,

\[ \mathrm{MA}^{\mathrm{ext}}_\theta (w) =\langle T^2\rangle -2L\wedge \langle T\rangle +L^2. \]

All correction measures are finite: the closed top measure has the mass just computed, the degree-one limit is $K$, and the smooth measure $L^2$ gives the pole set zero mass. The same truncations are used in both backgrounds, so this identity is an equality of Borel measures. Integrating yields

\[ \int _ X\mathrm{MA}^{\mathrm{ext}}_\theta (w) =(C-A)^2-2(C-A)\int _ XL\wedge \kappa +\int _ XL^2 =\int _ X(\theta -A\kappa )^2. \]

This holds throughout the ordinary type, proving both singularity-volume identities and their strict positivity. $\square$

30.9 Model comparison under background changes

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

A smooth change of decomposition $(\theta ,u)\mapsto (\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f,u-f)$ preserves the curvature current but changes the zero obstacle used to normalize models. Taking the ordinary envelope again gives an exact correspondence that preserves the volume comparison. An increase of the background changes the volumes; a common positive lower-volume bound controls this change uniformly, and this allows analytic approximants whose curvature error tends to zero.

Proposition 30.9.1. Let $f\in C^\infty (X,\mathbb R)$ and $\theta '=\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f$. For every $\delta >0$, the map

\[ G_ f:\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )\longrightarrow \mathcal M_\delta (\theta '), \qquad G_ f(p)=P_{\theta '}[p-f], \]

is a bijection with inverse $q\mapsto P_\theta [q+f]$. It preserves both singularity volumes, and

\[ d_{\theta '}(G_ f(p),G_ f(q))=d_\theta (p,q). \]

It takes analytic models to analytic models and ideal models to ideal models.

Proof. The map $u\mapsto u-f$ is a bijection from $\mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ to $\mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta ')$ that preserves curvature currents, singularity types and hence both singularity volumes. Also $\max (u-f,v-f)=\max (u,v)-f$, so

\[ d_{\theta '}(u-f,v-f)=d_\theta (u,v). \]

By proposition 16.3.1, $G_ f(p)+f\sim p$, that is, $G_ f(p)\sim p-f$. Thus $G_ f(p)$ has the same volumes as $p$, and it is an ordinary model by theorem 30.3.4; in particular $G_ f(p)\in \mathcal M_\delta (\theta ')$. Since the volume comparison depends only on ordinary types, the displayed identity follows. Taking the ordinary envelope of $G_ f(p)+f\sim p$ gives $P_\theta [G_ f(p)+f]=P_\theta [p]=p$. The same argument with the two backgrounds interchanged proves that the map is bijective with the stated inverse.

Subtraction of a smooth function and bounded changes preserve analytic singularities. If $p=P_\theta [p]_{\mathcal I}$, proposition 16.3.1 also gives

\[ P_{\theta '}[p-f]_{\mathcal I}\sim p-f\sim G_ f(p). \]

The ideal envelope is an ordinary model by corollary 16.2.4, and ordinary models of the same ordinary type coincide; hence $P_{\theta '}[G_ f(p)]_{\mathcal I}=P_{\theta '}[p-f]_{\mathcal I}=G_ f(p)$. The inverse map gives the converse. $\square$

For the rest of this section and the next, fix a Hermitian form $h$ on $X$, a Hermitian form $\Omega \geq \theta +2h$, a rational number $\kappa >0$ and an analytic $\rho \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with $\rho \leq 0$ and $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho \geq \kappa h$; such $\kappa $ and $\rho $ exist by bigness and theorem 1.15.1. Put $K=v_+(\Omega )$. For $\delta >0$, set

\[ b_\delta =1+\frac{\delta }{2^{n+1}nK},\qquad \kappa _\delta =(1-b_\delta ^{-1})\kappa . \]

These constants stay fixed while the potentials and backgrounds vary.

Proposition 30.9.2. Let $\beta $ be a smooth real $(1,1)$-form with $\theta \leq \beta \leq \theta +h$, and let $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\beta )$ satisfy $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\beta (u)\geq \delta >0$. For every $\varepsilon \in [0,1]$ and every smooth real $(1,1)$-form $\gamma $ with $\beta \leq \gamma \leq \beta +\varepsilon h$, both singularity volumes satisfy

\[ \operatorname {vol}_\beta (u) \leq \operatorname {vol}_\gamma (u) \leq (1+\varepsilon /\kappa _\delta )^ n\operatorname {vol}_\beta (u). \]

Proof. Normalize $u\leq 0$, so that $u\leq V_\beta $, and note that $\rho \leq V_\beta $. Since $\Omega \geq \beta $ and $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\beta (u)\geq \delta $, lemma 30.4.1, applied in the big background $\beta $, shows that $H=Q_{b_\delta }(u,V_\beta )$ is defined. The potential

\[ w=b_\delta ^{-1}H+(1-b_\delta ^{-1})\rho \]

satisfies $w\leq u$ and $\beta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\geq (1-b_\delta ^{-1})(\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho )\geq \kappa _\delta h$. The addition argument in the proof of proposition 30.4.4, with $h$, $\kappa _\delta $ and $t=\varepsilon /\kappa _\delta $ in place of $\omega $, $\delta $ and $t$, therefore gives the upper bound, with constants independent of $\beta $ and $u$. The lower bound is volume monotonicity. $\square$

Corollary 30.9.3. Let $u,v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ have lower volume at least $\delta >0$, and let $\eta $ be a smooth real $(1,1)$-form with $\theta \leq \eta \leq \theta +\varepsilon h$, where $0\leq \varepsilon \leq 1$. Then

30.25
\begin{equation} \left|d_\eta (P_\eta [u],P_\eta [v])-d_\theta (u,v)\right| \leq 2K\left((1+\varepsilon /\kappa _\delta )^ n-1\right). \end{equation}

Proof. The preceding proposition, with $\beta =\theta $ and $\gamma =\eta $, applies to $u$, $v$ and $m=\max (u,v)$, and their upper volumes in background $\theta $ are at most $v_+(\Omega )=K$. Thus, for $x=u,v,m$, the errors

\[ e_ x=\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\eta (x) -\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (x) \]

lie between zero and $K((1+\varepsilon /\kappa _\delta )^ n-1)$. The difference $d_\eta (u,v)-d_\theta (u,v)$ equals $2e_ m-e_ u-e_ v$, whose absolute value is at most twice this bound. Finally, the projection invariance in proposition 30.6.3, applied in background $\eta $, gives $d_\eta (u,v)=d_\eta (P_\eta [u],P_\eta [v])$, which is the comparison appearing in (30.25). $\square$

In particular, for $p_ j,p\in \mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ and $\theta \leq \eta _ j\leq \theta +\varepsilon _ jh$ with $\varepsilon _ j\to 0$,

\[ d_\theta (p_ j,p)\longrightarrow 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad d_{\eta _ j}(P_{\eta _ j}[p_ j],P_{\eta _ j}[p])\longrightarrow 0. \]

Here the backgrounds $\eta _ j$ need not be monotone in $j$. The next result allows the analytic approximants themselves to belong only to the larger backgrounds $\eta _ j$.

Theorem 30.9.4. Let $p$ be an ordinary model of positive lower volume, and let $\eta _ j$ be smooth real $(1,1)$-forms with $\theta \leq \eta _ j\leq \theta +\varepsilon _ jh$, where $\varepsilon _ j\geq 0$ tends to zero. Then $p=P_\theta [p]_{\mathcal I}$ if and only if there are analytic $a_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\eta _ j)$ with

\[ d_{\eta _ j}(a_ j,p)\longrightarrow 0. \]

The $a_ j$ may be required to be ordinary models in their respective backgrounds. Given any such analytic approximants, convex combinations with the fixed potential $\rho $, with rational coefficients, produce analytic $b_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ whose ordinary envelopes satisfy

\[ d_\theta (P_\theta [b_ j],p)\longrightarrow 0, \qquad P_\theta [b_ j]\longrightarrow p \quad \text{in }L^1\text{ and capacity}. \]

Proof. Suppose first that $p=P_\theta [p]_{\mathcal I}$. By theorem 30.8.3, there are analytic ordinary models $r_ j$ in background $\theta $ with $d_\theta (r_ j,p)\to 0$ and a common positive lower-volume bound. Corollary 30.9.3 and projection invariance in background $\eta _ j$ give $d_{\eta _ j}(r_ j,p)\to 0$, so $a_ j=r_ j$ works. One can also use the ordinary models $a_ j=P_{\eta _ j}[r_ j]$, since $P_{\eta _ j}[r_ j]\sim r_ j$ by proposition 16.2.7.

Conversely, suppose that such $a_ j$ exist. Normalize them below zero, and put $m_ j=\max (a_ j,p)$ and $\delta =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)>0$. Inequality (30.13) and proposition 30.9.2 give

\[ \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\eta _ j}(a_ j),\quad \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\eta _ j}(m_ j) \longrightarrow \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p). \]

Indeed, the upper volume of $p$ in background $\eta _ j$ tends to its upper volume in background $\theta $, and the differences of $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\eta _ j}(a_ j)$ and $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\eta _ j}(m_ j)$ from $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\eta _ j}(p)$ are bounded in absolute value by $d_{\eta _ j}(a_ j,p)$.

Enlarge the errors $\varepsilon _ j$ to positive rational numbers tending to zero, and put

\[ c_ j=\frac{\kappa }{\kappa +\varepsilon _ j},\qquad b_ j=c_ ja_ j+(1-c_ j)\rho ,\qquad r_ j=P_\theta [b_ j]. \]

As in the proof of theorem 30.8.3, $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}b_ j\geq 0$ and $b_ j$ has analytic singularities. Choose $A>0$ with $\theta \leq Ah$, and set

\[ \widehat\theta _ j=\theta +(1+A/\kappa )\varepsilon _ jh, \qquad \widehat m_ j=P_{\widehat\theta _ j}[m_ j]. \]

Then $\widehat\theta _ j\geq \eta _ j$ and $c_ j\widehat\theta _ j\geq \theta $. Since $r_ j\sim b_ j$ and $\rho \leq 0$, the potential $r_ j/c_ j$ is a nonpositive $\widehat\theta _ j$-plurisubharmonic function more singular than $a_ j$, hence more singular than $m_ j$. Therefore $r_ j\leq c_ j\widehat m_ j$. Also $p\leq \widehat m_ j\leq c_ j\widehat m_ j$.

The lower volume of $m_ j$ in background $\eta _ j$ is at least $\delta $. For large $j$, proposition 30.9.2 applies with $\beta =\eta _ j$, $\gamma =\widehat\theta _ j$ and $\varepsilon =(1+A/\kappa )\varepsilon _ j$. Consequently

\[ \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\widehat\theta _ j}(m_ j) \longrightarrow \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p). \]

By theorem 30.3.4, $\widehat m_ j$ has the same upper volume in background $\widehat\theta _ j$ as $m_ j$. On the other hand,

\[ c_ j\eta _ j+(1-c_ j)(\theta -\kappa h)\leq \theta . \]

Addition, applied to $c_ ja_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,c_ j\eta _ j)$ and $(1-c_ j)\rho \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,(1-c_ j)(\theta -\kappa h))$, and background monotonicity therefore bound the upper volume of $b_ j$ below by $c_ j^ n\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\eta _ j}(a_ j)$. Since $r_ j\sim b_ j$, the two comparisons yield

\[ \begin{split} c_ j^ n\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\eta _ j}(a_ j) & \leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (r_ j) \leq \overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (\max (r_ j,p))\\ & \leq c_ j^ n\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\widehat\theta _ j}(m_ j). \end{split} \]

Here the last inequality uses $\theta \leq c_ j\widehat\theta _ j$ and $\max (r_ j,p)\leq c_ j\widehat m_ j$. Both outer terms tend to $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)$, which proves $d_\theta (r_ j,p)\to 0$. By proposition 16.2.7, $r_ j=P_\theta [b_ j]_{\mathcal I}$ is an ideal model, so theorem 30.8.4 gives $p=P_\theta [p]_{\mathcal I}$.

For large $j$, the upper-volume deficit from $r_ j$ to $\max (r_ j,p)$ is small, and lemma 30.2.1 with $b=2$ gives a convex lower bound for $r_ j$ whose first term is $\frac12\max (r_ j,p)$. Volume addition gives $\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (r_ j)\geq 2^{-n}\delta $. Thus proposition 30.6.3, theorem 30.7.3 give the asserted $L^1$ and capacity convergence of the models $r_ j$. $\square$

Since the volume comparison depends only on singularity types, the theorem asserts convergence of the ordinary models $P_\theta [b_ j]$, not of the analytic potentials $b_ j$ themselves. When the background errors may have either sign, $p$ need not be admissible in the perturbed backgrounds; the next section constructs reference models there.

Then the volume comparison in background $\eta _ j$ between the two transports of $p$ tends to zero.

Proof. Write $c=c_\varepsilon $, $s=1-c$, $v=cp+s\rho $ and $T(p)=T^\rho _{\eta ,\varepsilon }(p)$. Since $s\rho \in \mathrm{PSH}(X,s(\theta -\kappa h))$, addition gives $\operatorname {vol}_{\theta -s\kappa h}(v)\geq c^ n\operatorname {vol}_\theta (p)$, and since $\theta -s\kappa h=\theta -\varepsilon h\leq \eta $, monotonicity gives the lower bound in (30.29) for $v$. In particular $v$ has positive lower volume in background $\eta $, so theorem 30.3.4 shows that $T(p)=P_\eta [v]$ is an ordinary model with the same two volumes as $v$.

Since $\varepsilon <\kappa /2$, we have $c\geq 1/2$, and the choice of $B$ gives

\[ c\widehat\theta _\varepsilon -(\theta +\varepsilon h) \geq \varepsilon (cB-1-A/\kappa )h\geq 0. \]

Since $\eta \leq \theta +\varepsilon h\leq c\widehat\theta _\varepsilon $, every competitor $w$ for $T(p)$, that is, every nonpositive $\eta $-plurisubharmonic $w\preceq v$, satisfies $w/c\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\widehat\theta _\varepsilon )$ and $w/c\preceq p$, because $\rho \leq 0$. Taking envelopes, and using that $v\leq 0$ is itself a competitor, yields

30.33
\begin{equation} cp+s\rho \leq T(p)\leq cP_{\widehat\theta _\varepsilon }[p]. \end{equation}

Volume monotonicity, together with theorem 30.3.4 in the larger background $\widehat\theta _\varepsilon $, proves the upper bound in (30.29).

Now let $q\in \mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ and $m=\max (p,q)$. The same pointwise bounds give

\[ cm+s\rho \leq \max (T(p),T(q)) \leq cP_{\widehat\theta _\varepsilon }[m]. \]

Consequently the upper volumes of $T(p)$, $T(q)$ and of their maximum lie between $c^ n$ times the respective upper volumes of $p$, $q$, $m$ in the backgrounds $\theta $ and $\widehat\theta _\varepsilon $. The lower volumes of $p$, $q$ and $m$ are at least $\delta $, and their upper volumes are at most $K$. By proposition 30.9.2 with $\beta =\theta $ and $\gamma =\widehat\theta _\varepsilon $, each of the three errors $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\eta (T(x))-c^ n\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (x)$, $x=p,q,m$ (with $T(m)$ read as $\max (T(p),T(q))$), is nonnegative and at most $c^ nK((1+B\varepsilon /\kappa _\delta )^ n-1)$. As in the proof of corollary 30.9.3, this proves (30.30).

For fixed $p$, corollary 30.5.4, lemma 1.8.12 give $P_{\widehat\theta _\varepsilon }[p]\to p$ in $L^1$ and in capacity along every decreasing sequence $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$. An arbitrary sequence $\varepsilon _ j\to 0$ is handled by comparing with the decreasing sequence $\sup _{k\geq j}\varepsilon _ k$, since the envelopes increase with the background and lie above $p$. Quasi-continuity and integrability give $cp+s\rho \to p$ in both senses. The bounds (30.33) prove the asserted limits.

To compare two choices along a sequence $\eta _ j$, denote their coefficients by $c_ j^{(i)}$ and their upper backgrounds by $\theta +B_ i\varepsilon _ j^{(i)}h$, $i=0,1$. Put $c_ j=\min _ i c_ j^{(i)}$ and $a_ j=\max _ i B_ i\varepsilon _ j^{(i)}$. Since the envelopes are nonpositive, both transported models lie below $c_ jP_{\theta +a_ jh}[p]$. Moreover $\eta _ j\leq c_ j(\theta +a_ jh)$, by the form inequality above for an index attaining the smaller coefficient. The upper volume of the maximum of the two transported models is therefore at most $c_ j^ n\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\theta +a_ jh}(p)$, which tends to $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)$. Each of their individual upper volumes is at least $(c_ j^{(i)})^ n\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)$ and at most the upper volume of the maximum, so it has the same limit. Hence their $d_{\eta _ j}$-comparison tends to zero. $\square$

For $p_ j,p\in \mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$, (30.30) shows that $d_\theta (p_ j,p)\to 0$ if and only if the comparisons of the transported models tend to zero along backgrounds whose errors tend to zero. The next criterion allows approximants chosen directly in those backgrounds.

Theorem 30.10.3. Let $p$ be an ordinary model of positive lower volume. Let $\varepsilon _ j$ be rational numbers with $0<\varepsilon _ j<\min (\kappa /2,1/B)$ tending to zero, let $\eta _ j$ be smooth real $(1,1)$-forms with $\theta -\varepsilon _ jh\leq \eta _ j\leq \theta +\varepsilon _ jh$, and put $p_ j=T^\rho _{\eta _ j,\varepsilon _ j}(p)$. Then $p=P_\theta [p]_{\mathcal I}$ if and only if there are analytic $a_ j\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\eta _ j)$ with

\[ d_{\eta _ j}(a_ j,p_ j)\longrightarrow 0. \]

The approximants may be required to be ordinary models. This condition does not depend on the choice of the strict analytic potential and of the admissible error bounds used to transport $p$. For every such analytic approximation, the convex combinations with $\rho $ of theorem 30.9.4 give analytic potentials in the original background whose ordinary envelopes converge to $p$ for $d_\theta $, in $L^1$ and in capacity.

Proof. Suppose that $p=P_\theta [p]_{\mathcal I}$. By theorem 30.8.3, there are analytic ordinary models $r_ j$ in background $\theta $ with $d_\theta (r_ j,p)\to 0$ and a common positive lower-volume bound. The transports $a_ j=T^\rho _{\eta _ j,\varepsilon _ j}(r_ j)$ have analytic singularities: the convex coefficients are rational, and ordinary projection preserves the singularity type of an analytic potential by proposition 16.2.7. The estimate (30.30) gives $d_{\eta _ j}(a_ j,p_ j)\to 0$.

Conversely, assume that such analytic approximants exist, and put $\delta =\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)$. By theorem 30.10.2, the lower volume of $p_ j$ is at least $c_{\varepsilon _ j}^ n\delta \geq 2^{-n}\delta $. The upper-volume deficit from $a_ j$ to $\max (a_ j,p_ j)$ is at most $d_{\eta _ j}(a_ j,p_ j)$, which tends to zero. Applying lemma 30.2.1 with $b=2$ in background $\eta _ j$, and then addition, gives

\[ \underline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\eta _ j}(a_ j)\geq 2^{-2n}\delta \]

for all sufficiently large $j$.

Put $\beta _ j=\theta +\varepsilon _ jh$. Lemma 30.10.1, with $\beta =\eta _ j$ and $\gamma =\beta _ j$, and projection invariance in background $\beta _ j$ imply $d_{\beta _ j}(a_ j,p_ j)\to 0$. Also $d_{\beta _ j}(p_ j,p)\to 0$. Indeed, (30.33) bounds $\max (p_ j,p)$ above by $c_{\varepsilon _ j}P_{\widehat\theta _{\varepsilon _ j}}[p]$, and $c_{\varepsilon _ j}\widehat\theta _{\varepsilon _ j}\geq \beta _ j$ by the form inequality in the proof of theorem 30.10.2. Hence the upper volume of this maximum in background $\beta _ j$ is at most $c_{\varepsilon _ j}^ n\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_{\widehat\theta _{\varepsilon _ j}}(p)$, which tends to $\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)$. The upper volumes of $p_ j$ and $p$ in background $\beta _ j$ lie between $c_{\varepsilon _ j}^ n\overline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)$ and this bound, so they have the same limit.

Project $a_ j$, $p_ j$ and $p$ to ordinary models in background $\beta _ j$. Their lower volumes have a common positive bound, and their upper volumes are bounded by $v_+(\Omega )$, since $\beta _ j\leq \Omega $. The three-point estimate (30.14) in background $\beta _ j$ therefore gives $d_{\beta _ j}(a_ j,p)\to 0$, with constants independent of $j$. Now theorem 30.9.4, applied with the backgrounds $\beta _ j$, shows that $p$ is an ideal model and gives the stated approximation in the original background.

For another choice of transport, theorem 30.10.2 shows that the two reference models in background $\eta _ j$ have volume comparison tending to zero. The lower-volume bound just proved and the same uniform three-point estimate transfer the convergence condition in both directions. This proves independence of the choice. $\square$

30.11 A complete metric on positive-volume models

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

The three-point modulus controls convergence even though $d_\theta $ need not satisfy the triangle inequality; two lines in the projective plane already show the failure. A complete metric can nevertheless be constructed from finite chains, as in the metrization of uniform structures. Continuity of the lower volume controls the chain estimates locally, and the reciprocal of the lower volume prevents Cauchy sequences from escaping to the zero-volume boundary.

Example 30.11.1. Let $\theta $ be the Fubini–Study form on $\mathbb P^2$, normalized by $\int _{\mathbb P^2}\theta ^2=1$. There are analytic ordinary models $p,q,r$ of positive lower volume such that

\[ d_\theta (p,q)>d_\theta (p,r)+d_\theta (r,q). \]

Proof. Write $|Z|^2=|Z_0|^2+|Z_1|^2+|Z_2|^2$ in homogeneous coordinates. Fix a rational $a\in (0,1/2)$ and put

\[ u=a\log \frac{|Z_0|^2}{|Z|^2},\qquad v=a\log \frac{|Z_1|^2}{|Z|^2},\qquad w=u+v. \]

With $H_ i=\{ Z_ i=0\} $, the corresponding currents are $a[H_0]+(1-a)\theta $, $a[H_1]+(1-a)\theta $ and $a[H_0]+a[H_1]+(1-2a)\theta $. The background is closed, so each singularity volume equals the non-pluripolar mass, which is the mass of the smooth part:

\[ U(u)=U(v)=(1-a)^2,\qquad U(w)=(1-2a)^2. \]

The maximum $\max (u,v)$ has the ordinary type of

\[ z=a\log \frac{|Z_0|^2+|Z_1|^2}{|Z|^2}. \]

To compute its volume, blow up the common point $[0:0:1]$ of $H_0$ and $H_1$. Denote the blowup by $\pi :Y\to \mathbb P^2$, its exceptional divisor by $E$, and the pencil map $[Z_0:Z_1]$ by $f:Y\to \mathbb P^1$. For the Fubini–Study form $\omega _1$ of mass one on $\mathbb P^1$, the Poincaré–Lelong formula gives

\[ \pi ^*\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(z\circ \pi )=a[E]+\beta , \qquad \beta =(1-a)\pi ^*\theta +af^*\omega _1. \]

Here $\beta $ is smooth and semipositive. Also

\[ \int _ Y(\pi ^*\theta )^2=1,\qquad \int _ Y\pi ^*\theta \wedge f^*\omega _1=1,\qquad (f^*\omega _1)^2=0. \]

The mixed integral is one because every fiber of the pencil maps onto a line. By lemma 28.7.1 and change of variables off $E$,

\[ U(\max (u,v))=\int _ Y\beta ^2=1-a^2. \]

Since $w\leq u$ and $w\leq v$, (30.13) and the definition of $d_\theta $ give

\[ \begin{split} d_\theta (u,v)& =4a-4a^2,\\ d_\theta (u,w)+d_\theta (w,v)& =4a-6a^2. \end{split} \]

Set $p=P_\theta [u]$, $q=P_\theta [v]$ and $r=P_\theta [w]$. By proposition 16.2.7, these models have the same analytic singularity types as $u$, $v$ and $w$, and their lower volumes are at least $(1-2a)^2$. By projection invariance (proposition 30.6.3), they have the same comparisons, and the difference between the two sides is $2a^2>0$. $\square$

We return to the big background $\theta $ on the $n$-dimensional manifold $X$. Write $L(p)=\underline{\operatorname {vol}}_\theta (p)$, and let

\[ \mathcal M_+(\theta )=\bigcup _{\delta >0}\mathcal M_\delta (\theta ) \]

be the space of ordinary models of positive lower volume. We keep the notation $U$ and $M$ of section 30.6.

Proposition 30.11.2. For $p,q\in \mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$,

30.36
\begin{equation} |L(p)-L(q)|\leq nM\min \left\{ 1, \left(\frac{2d_\theta (p,q)}\delta \right)^{1/n}\right\} . \end{equation}

If $p_ j,p\in \mathcal M_+(\theta )$ and $d_\theta (p_ j,p)\to 0$, then $L(p_ j)\to L(p)>0$.

Proof. Put $s=d_\theta (p,q)$ and $m=\max (p,q)$. If $s\geq \delta /2$, the bound follows from $|L(p)-L(q)|\leq M$, and if $s=0$, then $p=q$ by proposition 30.6.3. Suppose $0<s<\delta /2$ and take $t=(2s/\delta )^{1/n}$. The upper-volume deficit from either $p$ or $q$ to $m$ is at most $s$, while $L(m)\geq \delta $. Thus lemma 30.2.1 gives a potential $h\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\theta )$ with

\[ (1-t)m+th\leq p. \]

Volume addition and monotonicity imply $L(p)\geq (1-t)^ nL(m)\geq (1-t)^ nL(q)$, so $L(q)-L(p)\leq (1-(1-t)^ n)M\leq nMt$. Interchanging $p$ and $q$ proves the bound.

For the last assertion, put $s_ j=d_\theta (p_ j,p)$. Whenever $0<s_ j<L(p)/2$, the same stretching argument applied to $\max (p_ j,p)$ gives

\[ L(p_ j)\geq (1-t_ j)^ nL(p),\qquad t_ j=\left(\frac{2s_ j}{L(p)}\right)^{1/n}. \]

If $s_ j=0$, then $p_ j=p$. Hence $L(p_ j)\geq L(p)/2$ for all sufficiently large $j$, and (30.36) with $\delta =L(p)/2$ gives $L(p_ j)\to L(p)$. $\square$

Theorem 30.11.3. There is a complete metric $\mathfrak d_\theta $ on $\mathcal M_+(\theta )$ such that

30.37
\begin{equation} \mathfrak d_\theta (p_ j,p)\longrightarrow 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad d_\theta (p_ j,p)\longrightarrow 0. \end{equation}

A sequence $p_ j$ is $\mathfrak d_\theta $-Cauchy if and only if it is $d_\theta $-Cauchy and the sequence $1/L(p_ j)$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb R$. On each $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$, the metric $\mathfrak d_\theta $ and the comparison $d_\theta $ define the same uniform structure, and the restriction of $\mathfrak d_\theta $ is complete.

Proof. Step 1: a decreasing family of symmetric relations. Set $C=1+M$ and $m(p)=L(p)/C$, so that $0<m(p)<1$. Put $a_1=1$, $a_{k+1}=na_ k+1$, and choose $0<\varepsilon _1<1$. Recursively choose $\varepsilon _{k+1}>0$ so small that, with $r_ k=4^{a_{k+1}}\varepsilon _{k+1}$,

\[ 3\varepsilon _{k+1}<\varepsilon _ k,\qquad 3r_ k+4nM\left(\frac{2r_ k}{C}\right)^{1/n}<\varepsilon _ k. \]

Let $\mathcal U_0=\mathcal M_+(\theta )^2$ and, for $k\geq 1$, set

\[ \mathcal U_ k=\left\{ (p,q): \left|\frac1{m(p)}-\frac1{m(q)}\right|<\varepsilon _ k, \quad d_\theta (p,q)<\varepsilon _ k\min \{ m(p),m(q)\} ^{a_ k}\right\} . \]

These symmetric sets contain the diagonal and decrease with $k$, and their intersection is the diagonal by proposition 30.6.3. We claim that

30.38
\begin{equation} \mathcal U_{k+1}\circ \mathcal U_{k+1}\circ \mathcal U_{k+1} \subset \mathcal U_ k\qquad (k\geq 0), \end{equation}

where composition of relations means joining successive pairs. For $k=0$ this is immediate. For $k\geq 1$, suppose $(p_{i-1},p_ i)\in \mathcal U_{k+1}$ for $i=1,2,3$, and put $m_*=\min _{0\leq i\leq 3}m(p_ i)$. The reciprocal differences along this chain have total less than $3\varepsilon _{k+1}<1$, so $1/m(p_ i)>1/m_*-1$. Hence $m(p_ i)\leq 4m_*$ for every $i$: if $m_*\leq 3/4$, this follows from $1/m_*-1\geq 1/(4m_*)$, and otherwise from $m(p_ i)<1$. Each successive comparison is therefore less than

\[ e=r_ k m_*^{a_{k+1}}. \]

All four models have lower volume at least $Cm_*$. Applying (30.14) with $\delta =Cm_*$ twice, first with middle point $p_2$ and then with middle point $p_1$, yields

\[ \begin{split} d_\theta (p_0,p_3) & \leq 3e+4nM\left(\frac{2e}{Cm_*}\right)^{1/n}\\ & \leq \left(3r_ k+4nM\left(\frac{2r_ k}{C}\right)^{1/n}\right)m_*^{a_ k}\\ & <\varepsilon _ k\min \{ m(p_0),m(p_3)\} ^{a_ k}. \end{split} \]

Here $(a_{k+1}-1)/n=a_ k$, $a_{k+1}\geq a_ k$ and $m_*<1$. The endpoint reciprocal difference is less than $3\varepsilon _{k+1}<\varepsilon _ k$, proving (30.38).

Step 2: the chain metric. Define

30.42
\begin{equation} \mathfrak d_\theta (p,q)=\inf \sum _{i=1}^ N2^{-k_ i}, \end{equation}

where the infimum runs over finite chains $p=p_0,p_1,\ldots ,p_ N=q$ and integers $k_ i\geq 0$ with $(p_{i-1},p_ i)\in \mathcal U_{k_ i}$; we call $\sum _ i2^{-k_ i}$ the cost of the chain. A single edge in $\mathcal U_0$ gives $\mathfrak d_\theta \leq 1$. Reversing and concatenating chains gives symmetry and the triangle inequality, while diagonal edges with arbitrarily large indices give $\mathfrak d_\theta (p,p)=0$.

We next prove the inclusions

30.43
\begin{equation} \{ \mathfrak d_\theta <2^{-(k+1)}\} \subset \mathcal U_ k\subset \{ \mathfrak d_\theta \leq 2^{-k}\} \qquad (k\geq 1). \end{equation}

The second inclusion follows by using a single edge. For the first, we prove by induction on the number of edges that every chain of cost less than $2^{-k}$ has its endpoints in $\mathcal U_{k-1}$, simultaneously for all $k\geq 1$. For one edge this follows because the $\mathcal U_ k$ decrease. For a chain of cost $A<2^{-k}$, choose $l\geq k$ with

\[ 2^{-(l+1)}\leq A<2^{-l}. \]

Split the chain at the first edge at which the cumulative cost reaches $2^{-(l+1)}$. The subchains before and after this edge both have cost less than $2^{-(l+1)}$, so by induction their endpoint pairs lie in $\mathcal U_ l$; an empty subchain is interpreted as a diagonal pair. Every edge cost is a power of two smaller than $2^{-l}$, so the middle edge lies in $\mathcal U_{l+1}\subset \mathcal U_ l$. By (30.38), the endpoints of the whole chain form a pair in $\mathcal U_{l-1}\subset \mathcal U_{k-1}$. This completes the induction and proves (30.43). Since the $\mathcal U_ k$ intersect in the diagonal, $\mathfrak d_\theta $ separates points, so it is a metric.

Step 3: convergence and completeness. By (30.43), $\mathfrak d_\theta (p_ j,p)\to 0$ if and only if $(p_ j,p)$ eventually lies in every $\mathcal U_ k$. This implies $d_\theta (p_ j,p)\to 0$. Conversely, if $d_\theta (p_ j,p)\to 0$, then proposition 30.11.2 gives $L(p_ j)\to L(p)>0$, so for each fixed $k$ both inequalities defining $\mathcal U_ k$ hold eventually. This proves (30.37).

The same inclusions show that a $\mathfrak d_\theta $-Cauchy sequence is $d_\theta $-Cauchy and has Cauchy reciprocal lower volumes. These reciprocals are bounded, so a tail of the sequence lies in some $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$. By theorem 30.6.5, the sequence converges there for $d_\theta $, and hence for $\mathfrak d_\theta $. This proves completeness. Conversely, a $d_\theta $-Cauchy sequence with Cauchy reciprocal lower volumes has a common positive lower-volume bound on a tail, and then both inequalities defining each $\mathcal U_ k$ hold for all pairs of sufficiently late terms. This proves the Cauchy criterion.

On $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$, (30.36) controls the reciprocal differences uniformly in terms of $d_\theta $, while $m(p)\geq \delta /C$ controls the second threshold in $\mathcal U_ k$. Thus $\mathfrak d_\theta $ and $d_\theta $ define the same uniform structure there. Continuity of the lower volume makes $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ closed for $\mathfrak d_\theta $, so the restriction of $\mathfrak d_\theta $ to it is complete. $\square$

Corollary 30.11.4. The ideal models in $\mathcal M_+(\theta )$ form a closed, hence complete, subspace of $(\mathcal M_+(\theta ),\mathfrak d_\theta )$, and the analytic ordinary models of positive lower volume are dense in it. Equivalently, this subspace is the metric completion of the analytic ordinary models of positive lower volume with the restricted metric.

Proof. By theorem 30.8.4, an ordinary model of positive lower volume that is a $d_\theta $-limit of ideal models is again an ideal model. The same theorem, which relies on theorem 30.8.3, gives the density of analytic models. Now apply (30.37) and the completeness of $\mathfrak d_\theta $. $\square$

30.12 Model potentials on curves

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

On a curve the preceding theory becomes explicit. The part of the curvature current carried by the pole set determines the ordinary envelope, and its atomic part determines the ideal envelope. Comparing these two measures describes both the zero-volume boundary and the failure of analytic approximation for diffuse polar singularities. Total variation of the polar measures keeps this singularity data and at the same time controls convergence of the model potentials in capacity.

In this section, $Y$ is a compact connected Riemann surface, $\theta $ is a smooth positive $(1,1)$-form on $Y$, and $A=\int _ Y\theta $. By Stokes' theorem every bounded $\theta $-plurisubharmonic function has mass $A$; thus $Y$ has the bounded mass property, $\theta $ is big, and the preceding results apply with $Y$ in place of $X$. For $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )$ write $T_ u=\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$, and let $\mu _ u=\mathbf1_{\{ u=-\infty \} }T_ u=T_ u^{\mathrm{pol}}$ be the polar curvature of $u$ (section 1.6.1); in this and the following sections, $\mu _ u$ no longer denotes $\mathrm{MA}_\theta (u)$. A positive measure carried by a polar set will be called a polar measure. Since $\theta $ is closed, the two singularity volumes of $u$ coincide with

\[ U(u)=\int _ Y\mathrm{MA}_\theta (u)=A-\mu _ u(Y). \]

Fix a nonpositive Green kernel $G$ for $\eta =\theta /A$, as in section 19.3, so that $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}_ xG(x,y)=\delta _ y-\eta $, where $\delta _ y$ is the Dirac measure at $y$. We write $\| \cdot \| _{\mathrm{TV}}$ for the total variation norm of finite signed measures.

Lemma 30.12.1. For a polar measure $\mu $ on $Y$ with $\mu (Y)\leq A$, put

\[ w_\mu (x)=\int _ YG(x,y)\, \mathrm{d}\mu (y),\qquad p_\mu =P_\theta [w_\mu ]. \]

Then $p_\mu \sim w_\mu $, $\mu _{p_\mu }=\mu $ and $U(p_\mu )=A-\mu (Y)$. Every $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )$ satisfies

30.44
\begin{equation} P_\theta [u]=p_{\mu _ u}. \end{equation}

Consequently $\mu \mapsto p_\mu $ is a bijection from the polar measures of mass at most $A$ onto the set of all ordinary models on $Y$.

Proof. The bounds for the Green kernel give $w_\mu \in L^1(Y)$ and

\[ T_{w_\mu }=\mu +(A-\mu (Y))\eta . \]

In particular $w_\mu $ is $\theta $-plurisubharmonic, and its polar curvature is $\mu $. We claim that, for every $v\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )$,

30.45
\begin{equation} v\preceq w_\mu \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad T_ v\geq \mu . \end{equation}

The forward implication follows from corollary 1.6.8, which gives $T_ v\geq \mu _ v\geq \mu _{w_\mu }=\mu $. Conversely, if $T_ v\geq \mu $, the distribution $v-w_\mu $ satisfies $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(v-w_\mu )\geq -(A-\mu (Y))\eta $, so its quasi-subharmonic representative is bounded above on the compact curve $Y$. By lemma 1.3.3, $v\preceq w_\mu $.

Thus $p_\mu $ is the upper envelope of all $v\leq 0$ with $T_ v\geq \mu $. This family is closed under maxima by (1.14). By Choquet's lemma the envelope is the limit of an increasing sequence of such maxima, which converges in $L^1$; hence the current inequality $T_{p_\mu }\geq \mu $ passes to the limit. Also $p_\mu \geq w_\mu -\sup _ Yw_\mu $. Hence $b=p_\mu -w_\mu $ has a quasi-subharmonic representative that is bounded below, and compactness bounds it above. Therefore $p_\mu \sim w_\mu $. The positive measure

\[ T_{p_\mu }-\mu =(A-\mu (Y))\eta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}b \]

charges no polar set because $b$ is bounded. This proves $\mu _{p_\mu }=\mu $ and $U(p_\mu )=A-\mu (Y)$, and ordinary equivalence gives $P_\theta [p_\mu ]=P_\theta [w_\mu ]=p_\mu $.

For arbitrary $u$, take $\mu =\mu _ u$. Then $T_ u\geq \mu $, so (30.45) gives $u\preceq p_\mu $, and $U(u)=A-\mu (Y)=U(p_\mu )$. If this mass is positive, corollary 30.3.6 gives (30.44). If it is zero, then $\mu (Y)=A$ and $T_ u=\mu =T_{w_\mu }$. The difference $u-w_\mu $ is then harmonic on $Y$ and hence constant, which gives the same conclusion. Finally, every ordinary model $p$ equals $P_\theta [p]=p_{\mu _ p}$, and distinct polar measures give distinct models because $\mu _{p_\mu }=\mu $. $\square$

Theorem 30.12.2. For every $u,v\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )$,

30.46
\begin{equation} d_\theta (u,v)=\| \mu _ u-\mu _ v\| _{\mathrm{TV}}. \end{equation}

In particular, $d_\theta $ is a metric on the set of ordinary models on $Y$. For positive atomic measures $D,E$ of mass at most $A$, let $q_ D,q_ E$ be the ideal models of lemma 19.5.2, with polar curvatures $D$ and $E$. Then

30.47
\begin{equation} d_\theta (q_ D,q_ E)=\| D-E\| _{\mathrm{TV}}. \end{equation}

Proof. By (1.14), the polar curvature of $\max (u,v)$ is the minimum $\mu _ u\wedge \mu _ v$ of the two measures. The formula $U=A-\mu (Y)$ therefore gives

\[ \begin{split} d_\theta (u,v)& =2U(\max (u,v))-U(u)-U(v)\\ & =\mu _ u(Y)+\mu _ v(Y)-2(\mu _ u\wedge \mu _ v)(Y)\\ & =\| \mu _ u-\mu _ v\| _{\mathrm{TV}}. \end{split} \]

The classification in lemma 30.12.1 shows that $d_\theta $ separates ordinary models, and the triangle inequality follows from that of the total variation norm. By lemma 19.5.2, the ideal models are exactly the models whose polar measures are atomic, which gives (30.47). $\square$

Corollary 30.12.3. The ordinary models on $Y$ form a complete geodesic metric space for $d_\theta $. The ideal models form a closed geodesically convex subspace, which is the metric completion of the analytic ordinary models of positive mass.

Proof. By lemma 30.12.1, theorem 30.12.2, the space of ordinary models is isometric to the set of polar measures of mass at most $A$ with the total variation norm. This set is closed in total variation: a limit is positive, satisfies the mass bound, and is carried by the countable union of polar carriers of the sequence. Completeness therefore follows from that of finite signed measures. For two such measures $\mu ,\nu $, the measures $\mu _ t=(1-t)\mu +t\nu $, $0\leq t\leq 1$, remain in this set and satisfy

\[ \| \mu _ t-\mu _ s\| _{\mathrm{TV}}=|t-s|\, \| \mu -\nu \| _{\mathrm{TV}}. \]

Their models give a constant-speed geodesic.

The atomic measures form a closed convex subset, since a total-variation limit is carried by the countable union of the sets of atoms of the sequence. Restricting to finitely many atoms and approximating the coefficients from below by rational numbers, with an arbitrarily small further decrease if needed, approximates every such measure by finite atomic measures with rational coefficients and mass strictly less than $A$. Their models have analytic singularities by lemma 19.5.2, and (30.47) gives the density. $\square$

The models of positive mass do not form a complete space for $d_\theta $: for a fixed $x\in Y$, the models of $tA\delta _ x$ form a Cauchy family as $t\uparrow 1$, with a limit of zero mass. For the complete metric of theorem 30.11.3, this family is not Cauchy, because its reciprocal lower volumes diverge. The two metrics induce the same topology on models of positive mass but have different Cauchy sequences near the boundary.

The metric $d_\theta $ also controls convergence of the potentials at the zero-mass boundary. There the polar part exhausts the limiting curvature current, so its total variation controls the whole current.

Theorem 30.12.4. Let $p_ j,p$ be ordinary models on $Y$. If $d_\theta (p_ j,p)\to 0$, then $p_ j\to p$ in $L^1$ and in capacity. If $U(p)=0$, then in addition

\begin{equation} \| T_{p_ j}-T_ p\| _{\mathrm{TV}}\leq 2d_\theta (p_ j,p)\longrightarrow 0. \end{equation}

Proof. Write $\mu _ j=\mu _{p_ j}$ and $\mu =\mu _ p$. By (30.46), $\mu _ j\to \mu $ in total variation. Thus $U(p_ j)=A-\mu _ j(Y)\to U(p)$. If $U(p)>0$, the models lie in a common $\mathcal M_\delta (\theta )$ for all large $j$, and proposition 30.6.3, theorem 30.7.3 apply.

Suppose that $U(p)=0$. Then $T_ p=\mu $ and $\mu (Y)=A$, while $T_{p_ j}-\mu _ j$ is a positive measure of mass $A-\mu _ j(Y)=\mu (Y)-\mu _ j(Y)$. Consequently

\[ \| T_{p_ j}-T_ p\| _{\mathrm{TV}} \leq \| \mu _ j-\mu \| _{\mathrm{TV}}+A-\mu _ j(Y) \leq 2\| \mu _ j-\mu \| _{\mathrm{TV}}, \]

which proves (30.51). By normalized compactness, each subsequence has a further subsequence converging in $L^1$ to a function $q$ with supremum zero. Then $T_{p_ j}\to T_ q$ weakly, so $T_ q=T_ p$ and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(q-p)=0$. Hence $q-p$ is constant, and the normalization gives $q=p$. This proves $L^1$ convergence of the whole sequence. Finally, apply lemma 1.6.6 on finitely many nested coordinate disks: both terms on its right-hand side tend to zero, and the comparison between local and global capacities in section 1.8 gives convergence in capacity. $\square$

For potentials with supremum zero, weak convergence $T_{p_ j}\rightharpoonup T_ p$ is equivalent to $p_ j\to p$ in $L^1$, by theorem 5.1.1 and normalized compactness. It need not imply convergence in capacity, even when the sequence and the limit are ideal models. Indeed, scaling the area-one construction in theorem 16.4.2 gives zero-mass ideal models converging to zero in $L^1$. Convergence in capacity would contradict theorem 7.3.1, since their damped measures vanish while the limiting measure is $\theta $.

Conversely, convergence in capacity does not imply convergence of the polar parts in total variation. Moving a single pole gives an example, even within the zero-mass boundary.

Example 30.12.5. On $\mathbb P^1$ with the Fubini–Study form $\theta $ of area one, there are zero-mass ideal models $p_ j,p$ with $p_ j\to p$ in capacity but $d_\theta (p_ j,p)=2$ for every $j$.

On the complement of any fixed disk about zero the convergence is uniform, including near infinity. Small disks about zero lie in sublevel sets $\{ p_0<-t\} $ with $t$ large, so their capacities tend to zero as the radius tends to zero, by (1.26). Therefore $p_ j\to p$ in capacity. Example 30.12.7. There is an ordinary model $p$ on $Y$ with zero mass whose multiplier ideals $\mathcal I(tp)$ are trivial for every $t>0$. It satisfies

30.12.1 The ordinary-type topology on a curve

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

We now compare the polar metric with the topology on ordinary singularity types introduced in section 16.6. That topology also uses the ordinary envelope, but it measures the total variation only of the atomic part of the curvature. On ideal models the atomic part is the whole polar measure, so the two topologies agree there. On arbitrary ordinary models, diffuse polar mass can concentrate without being detected by the atomic total variation.

We keep $Y$, $\theta $, $A$ and the decomposition $\mu _ u=D_ u+\sigma _ u$, and we use $\eta =\theta /A$ for $L^1$ norms. Let $\mathfrak S(Y,\theta )=\mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )/{\sim }$ be the space of ordinary singularity types, and write $[u]$ for the class of $u$. Recall from proposition 16.6.2 that $\tau _{\theta ,0}$ is the uniform topology on $\mathfrak S(Y,\theta )$ generated by the $L^1$ distances between ordinary envelopes and between ideal envelopes, by the total variation of divisorial Lelong numbers on each fixed modification, and by the controlled ideal relations $\mathcal U(t,a)$ of (16.5). For ordinary classes put

30.54
\begin{equation} d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta ([u],[v]) =\| P_\theta [u]-P_\theta [v]\| _{L^1(\eta )} +\| D_ u-D_ v\| _{\mathrm{TV}}. \end{equation}

Theorem 30.12.8. The finite pseudometric $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta $ induces $\tau _{\theta ,0}$ on $\mathfrak S(Y,\theta )$. For $0\leq s\leq A$, the classes of ideal models

\[ \mathcal C_ s=\{ [q_ D]:D\text{ positive atomic},\ D(Y)\leq s\} \]

form a complete metric space both for $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta $ and for the distance $\| D-E\| _{\mathrm{TV}}$ between $[q_ D]$ and $[q_ E]$, and these two metrics induce the same topology. The space $\mathcal C_ s$ is not totally bounded if $s>0$, and $\mathcal C_0=\{ [0]\} $.

Proof. A smooth compact modification of a smooth curve is an isomorphism, and the divisor weight of a point is one. Hence the divisorial distance in proposition 16.6.2 is exactly the atomic total variation, and the two summands of (30.54) are among the coordinates defining $\tau _{\theta ,0}$.

Conversely, suppose that a net $[u_ j]$ converges to $[u]$ for $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta $. The ideal envelope of $u$ is $q_{D_ u}$, by lemma 19.5.2. Convergence of the atomic parts in total variation implies $L^1$ convergence of these envelopes, by theorem 30.12.2, theorem 30.12.4. These results also apply to nets: the map $D\mapsto q_ D$ goes between metric spaces, so sequential continuity is equivalent to continuity. It remains to check the controlled ideal relations.

At a point $x$ with local coordinate $z$, the multiplier ideals on the curve are

\[ \mathcal I(tu)_ x=(z^{\lfloor tD_ u(\{ x\} )\rfloor }). \]

This follows from theorem 1.16.3, lemma 1.16.1: on a smooth curve the Lelong number at $x$ determines the ideal, and the logarithmic germ with that Lelong number has the displayed ideal. Write $D_ j=D_{u_ j}$ and $D=D_ u$, and fix $t>0$ and $a>1$. The set $F=\{ x:D(\{ x\} )\geq 1/(2t)\} $ is finite. At its points, $aD_ j(\{ x\} )\geq D(\{ x\} )$ and $aD(\{ x\} )\geq D_ j(\{ x\} )$ eventually. Outside $F$, the bound

\[ \sup _ x|D_ j(\{ x\} )-D(\{ x\} )|\leq \| D_ j-D\| _{\mathrm{TV}}\longrightarrow 0 \]

makes both $\mathcal I(tu)_ x$ and $\mathcal I(tu_ j)_ x$ unit ideals for large $j$. Hence both inclusions in (16.5) hold eventually at every point. This proves that $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta $ induces $\tau _{\theta ,0}$.

The map $D\mapsto q_ D$ is injective, so $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta $ is a metric on $\mathcal C_ s$. The positive atomic measures of mass at most $s$ are closed in total variation: a limit is positive, satisfies the mass bound, and is carried by the countable union of the sets of atoms of the sequence. Completeness of the space of finite signed measures therefore gives completeness for total variation. A $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta $-Cauchy sequence in $\mathcal C_ s$ is Cauchy in total variation; its limit is again atomic, and theorem 30.12.4 gives convergence of the envelope coordinate. This proves completeness for $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta $ as well as the equality of the two topologies.

If $s>0$, fix $0<b\leq s$ and infinitely many distinct points $x_ j$. The measures $b\delta _{x_ j}$ have pairwise total-variation distance $2b$, so $\mathcal C_ s$ is not totally bounded for either metric. For $s=0$ only the zero measure remains. $\square$

By lemma 19.5.2, for $0\leq \delta \leq A$ the set $\mathcal C_{A-\delta }$ consists exactly of the classes of ideal models of mass at least $\delta $. The whole set $\mathcal C_ A$, including the models of zero mass, is complete. Its subset of positive mass is incomplete: for a point $x$, the measures $D_ j=A(1-1/j)\delta _ x$ form a Cauchy sequence whose limit $A\delta _ x$ has zero mass.

For general ordinary classes, the corresponding statement concerns a pseudometric. Put

\[ \mathcal G_{\theta ,\delta } =\left\{ [u]:P_\theta [u]=P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I},\quad \int _ Y\mathrm{MA}_\theta (P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I})\geq \delta \right\} . \]

For $0\leq \delta \leq A$ this set is complete for $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta $, since every class $[u]$ in it is at distance zero from $[q_{D_ u}]\in \mathcal C_{A-\delta }$; limits need not be unique as ordinary classes. The set is not totally bounded when $\delta <A$. At $\delta =A$ the pseudometric vanishes identically on it, although it contains distinct classes by proposition 16.6.4. The union of these sets over all $\delta >0$ is incomplete, by the same one-atom sequence.

30.12.2 Concentration of diffuse polar curvature

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

The atomic total variation cannot be omitted from theorem 30.12.8. On $\mathbb P^1$ with its Fubini–Study form $\theta $ of area one, fix a rational $0<\tau <1$ and take the atomic probability measures $\nu _ j$ and the ideal models $r_ j=P_\theta [(1-\tau )q_ j]_{\mathcal I}$ of proposition 16.4.3, choosing the rational approximations so that the largest atom of $\nu _ j$ tends to zero. The ordinary and ideal envelopes of $r_ j$ both tend to zero in $L^1$, and for each $t>0$ and $a>1$, the pair $([r_ j],[0])$ eventually lies in $\mathcal U(t,a)$. The currents $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}r_ j$ converge weakly to $\theta $. Nevertheless,

\[ d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta ([r_ j],[0]) \geq \| D_{r_ j}\| _{\mathrm{TV}}=1-\tau >0. \]

Thus these weaker conditions do not imply convergence for $\tau _{\theta ,0}$, even at a fixed positive mass.

The opposite difficulty concerns diffuse polar mass: the pseudometric controls the ordinary envelope in $L^1$, but not the total variation of the diffuse measure itself.

Proposition 30.12.9. For the Fubini–Study form $\theta $ of area one on $\mathbb P^1$, the space $(\mathfrak S(\mathbb P^1,\theta ),d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta )$ is incomplete. In fact, there is a $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta $-Cauchy sequence without limit consisting of ordinary models of one fixed positive non-pluripolar mass, all with trivial multiplier ideals.

Proof. Choose a nonatomic polar probability measure $\sigma $ with compact support in an affine chart, using the Cantor construction in example 30.12.7. Fix $0<c<1$ and a point $x$ in that chart. Translating and shrinking the support towards $x$ gives nonatomic polar probability measures $\sigma _ j$ supported in disks about $x$ with radii tending to zero. Let $p_ j=p_{c\sigma _ j}$ be the ordinary models of lemma 30.12.1. Their polar measures are $c\sigma _ j$, their masses are $1-c$, and $D_{p_ j}=0$. Hence their Lelong numbers vanish, their multiplier ideals are trivial, and their ideal envelopes are zero.

We claim that $p_ j\to q_{c\delta _ x}$ in $L^1$. Write $q_ y=q_{c\delta _ y}$. Rotations of $\mathbb P^1$ preserving $\theta $ and sending $x$ to $y$ can be chosen to depend continuously on $y$ near $x$. They transport $q_ x$ to $q_ y$, so continuity of translations in $L^1$ gives $q_ y\to q_ x$ in $L^1$ as $y\to x$. Set

\[ w_ j=\int _{\mathbb P^1}q_ y\, \mathrm d\sigma _ j(y). \]

The common $L^1$ bound for normalized potentials makes this integral well defined. The function $w_ j$ is nonpositive, its curvature current dominates $c\sigma _ j$, and $w_ j\to q_ x$ in $L^1$. By (30.45), $p_ j$ is the upper envelope of all $v\leq 0$ with $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v\geq c\sigma _ j$, so $w_ j\leq p_ j$.

By normalized compactness, every subsequence of $p_ j$ has a further subsequence converging in $L^1$ to some $v\leq 0$. The inequalities $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}p_ j\geq c\sigma _ j$ pass to the limit and give $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v\geq c\delta _ x$. Since $q_ x=p_{c\delta _ x}$, the same envelope description gives $v\leq q_ x$. The lower bound $w_ j\leq p_ j$ gives $v\geq q_ x$ almost everywhere, hence everywhere. This identifies every cluster point and proves the claim.

Since $P_\theta [p_ j]=p_ j$ and $D_{p_ j}=0$, the classes $[p_ j]$ form a $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta $-Cauchy sequence. A limit $[v]$ would satisfy $D_ v=0$ and $P_\theta [v]=q_ x$. This is impossible: the ordinary envelope preserves all multiplier ideals and hence all atoms, whereas $q_ x$ has an atom of mass $c$ at $x$. The sequence therefore has no limit in $\mathfrak S(\mathbb P^1,\theta )$. $\square$

On ordinary models, $\tau _{\theta ,0}$ and the topology of the polar metric $d_\theta $ are also different. Choose the Cantor support of $\sigma $ inside a positive real interval in the affine coordinate $z$, away from zero. The rotations $R_ j(z)=\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\varepsilon _ j}z$, with $0<\varepsilon _ j\to 0$, move this support off itself. Put $p=p_{c\sigma }$ and $\widetilde p_ j=p\circ R_ j^{-1}$. By rotation invariance of the envelope, the $\widetilde p_ j$ are ordinary models with polar measures $c(R_ j)_*\sigma $, which have no atoms. Continuity of rotations in $L^1$ yields $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta ([\widetilde p_ j],[p])\to 0$, whereas the polar measures have disjoint supports, so $d_\theta (\widetilde p_ j,p)=2c$ by theorem 30.12.2. Conversely, polar total variation controls both terms of (30.54), by theorem 30.12.4. Thus $\tau _{\theta ,0}$ is strictly weaker than the polar metric topology on ordinary models, although the two agree on ideal models. The failure of continuity under smooth gauge changes with a fixed zero obstacle (proposition 16.6.6) gives a further difference: the polar metric is invariant under such changes, by section 30.13.

30.12.3 Convergence for all obstacles

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

We keep the compact curve $Y$ and its positive form $\theta $ of area $A$. The topology $\tau ^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $ of 16.6.4 is generated by the topologies $\tau _{\theta ,F}$ for all smooth obstacles $F$, in which the ordinary and ideal envelopes below $F$ replace those below zero. On a curve the envelopes below $F$ are still determined by the polar measure, and their ideal counterparts by its atomic part. This gives a description of $\tau ^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $ by a single pseudometric and allows a comparison with polar total variation.

Choose, as in proposition 16.6.7, a countable family $\{ F_ k\} _{k\geq 1}\subset C^\infty (Y,\mathbb R)$ that is dense for the uniform norm, with $F_1=0$. Write $d_{O,F}$ and $d_{I,F}$ for the $L^1$ distances between the ordinary envelopes $P_{\theta ,F}[\cdot ]$ and between the ideal envelopes $P_{\theta ,F}[\cdot ]_{\mathcal I}$, which define $\tau _{\theta ,F}$ (section 16.6), and put

30.55
\begin{equation} \begin{split} d^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta ([u],[v]) & =\| D_ u-D_ v\| _{\mathrm{TV}}\\ & \quad +\sum _{k=1}^\infty 2^{-k}\bigl (\min \{ 1,d_{O,F_ k}([u],[v])\} \\ & \qquad \qquad +\min \{ 1,d_{I,F_ k}([u],[v])\} \bigr ). \end{split} \end{equation}

Proposition 30.12.10. The pseudometric $d^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $ induces the uniformity of $\tau ^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $. Two classes have distance zero if and only if they have the same polar curvature. The closure of the analytic classes is $\{ [u]:\mu _ u=D_ u\} =\mathcal G_\theta $, where $\mathcal G_\theta =\{ [u]:P_\theta [u]=P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}\} $ as in (16.6). On each set $\mathcal C_ s$, $0\leq s\leq A$, the pseudometric $d^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $ is a complete metric inducing the topology of total variation of the atomic measures. On ordinary models, polar total variation implies convergence for $d^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $.

Proof. Step 1: envelopes below an obstacle. For any smooth $F$, translating the competitors by $F$ gives

\[ P_{\theta ,F}[u]-F=P_{\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F}[u-F], \]

and likewise for the ideal envelope. The background $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F$ on the right is a smooth real form of area $A$, though it need not be positive. We claim that these two envelopes are the models of $\mu _ u$ and of $D_ u$, respectively, in that background, in the sense of proposition 30.13.1. Set $\mu =\mu _ u$ and let $w_\mu $ be its Green potential, and put $r=P_{\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F}[w_\mu -F]$. By the gauge bounds of proposition 16.3.1, $r+F$ differs from $P_\theta [w_\mu ]=p_\mu \sim w_\mu $ by a bounded function, so $r\sim w_\mu -F$. Hence $r=P_{\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F}[r]$ is an ordinary model, and its polar measure is $\mu $. Moreover $u-F\preceq r$, and both have mass $A-\mu (Y)$. If this mass is positive, corollary 30.3.6, applied in the big background $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F$, shows that $P_{\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F}[u-F]=r$. If it is zero, the curvature measures of $u-F$ and $w_\mu -F$ both equal $\mu $, so these functions differ by a constant, and again $P_{\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F}[u-F]=r$. The assertion for the ideal envelope follows in the same way from ideal projection and the classification of atomic models. It also follows that the envelope $P_{\theta ,F}[u]$ is the upper envelope of all $v\leq F$ with $\theta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v\geq \mu $.

Step 2: the uniformity. By proposition 30.13.1, polar total variation gives $L^1$ convergence of these models for every fixed $F$. The same assertion for atomic measures gives continuity of every ideal-envelope coordinate with respect to the total variation of the atomic parts. The controlled ideal relations are also implied, uniformly, by atomic total variation: for fixed $t>0$ and $a>1$, the bound $\| D-E\| _{\mathrm{TV}}<(a-1)/(at)$ guarantees both inclusions in (16.5), by the formula for multiplier ideals on a curve. Indeed, if $tE(\{ x\} )\geq 1$, the bound gives $aD(\{ x\} )>E(\{ x\} )$; otherwise the target ideal is the unit ideal. Interchange $D$ and $E$ for the other inclusion. Each divisorial coordinate is the atomic total variation. Therefore the envelope terms in the series, together with the atomic term, generate exactly the uniformity of $\tau ^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $: finitely many coordinates control the sum up to its uniformly small tail, and the sum controls each fixed coordinate.

Step 3: zero distance and analytic closure. By Step 1 with $F=0$, distance zero forces equal polar measures; the classification at every obstacle proves the converse. The same classification shows that the ordinary and ideal envelopes agree at every $F$ if and only if $\mu _ u=D_ u$. Hence theorem 16.6.9 identifies the closure of the analytic classes.

Step 4: the sets $\mathcal C_ s$. For a $d^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $-Cauchy sequence in $\mathcal C_ s$, the atomic measures converge in total variation to a measure of mass at most $s$, which is again atomic. The convergence of models in Step 2 gives convergence of every obstacle coordinate, and the summable tail then gives convergence for $d^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $. Conversely, convergence for $d^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $ implies convergence of the atomic parts, because of the first summand. This proves completeness and the equality of topologies on $\mathcal C_ s$. Finally, polar total variation controls the atomic part and all ordinary-envelope coordinates, which proves the last assertion. $\square$

On a curve, membership in the relative envelope class of the ideal envelope has the same polar criterion, including for ideal envelopes of zero mass.

Corollary 30.12.11. For $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\theta )$ put $q=P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I}$. The following are equivalent:

\[ \begin{gathered} u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(Y,\theta ,q),\qquad \mu _ u=D_ u,\\ P_\theta [u]=P_\theta [u]_{\mathcal I},\\ P_{\theta ,F}[u]=P_{\theta ,F}[u]_{\mathcal I} \quad \text{for every smooth real }F. \end{gathered} \]

Step 1 of the proof of proposition 30.12.10 shows that this is equivalent to equality of the envelopes at every smooth obstacle. Membership of $u$ in $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(Y,\theta ,q)$ implies the latter by proposition 16.6.8.

Conversely, assume that $\mu _ u=D_ u$. By lemma 19.5.2, $\mu _ q=D_ u$. Ordinary equivalence preserves polar curvature by corollary 1.6.8, so every potential equivalent to $q$ has non-pluripolar mass $A-D_ u(Y)$, by Stokes' theorem. Hence

Proposition 30.12.12. Proof. Use the diffuse polar probability measures $\sigma _ j$ concentrating at a point $x$ and the models $p_ j=p_{c\sigma _ j}$ from the proof of proposition 30.12.9, with $0<c<1$. Fix a smooth obstacle $F$ and put $q_{y,F}=P_{\theta ,F}[q_{c\delta _ y}]$. Rotations preserving $\theta $ and sending $x$ to $y$ can be chosen to depend continuously on $y$ near $x$; they transport the obstacle $F$ to its composition with the rotation. The uniform $L^1$ bound near $x$ makes the average $w_{j,F}=\int q_{y,F}\, \mathrm d\sigma _ j(y)$ well defined. It lies below $F$, its curvature current dominates $c\sigma _ j$, and it converges to $q_{x,F}$ in $L^1$. Every cluster point of the envelopes has curvature at least $c\delta _ x$, so it is at most $q_{x,F}$, and the lower bound gives the reverse inequality. Hence $P_{\theta ,F}[p_ j]\to q_{x,F}$ for every $F$. The ideal envelopes of the $p_ j$ below $F$ all equal $P_\theta (F)$, since their multiplier ideals are trivial, and their atomic parts vanish. Thus $[p_ j]$ is a $d^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $-Cauchy sequence. Since $F_1=0$, a limit would also be a $d^{\mathrm{ot}}_\theta $-limit, which proposition 30.12.9 excludes.

For the strict comparison with polar total variation, use the rotated diffuse models $\widetilde p_ j=p\circ R_ j^{-1}$ from the end of 30.12.1. For every smooth $F$,

\[ P_{\theta ,F}[\widetilde p_ j] =P_{\theta ,F\circ R_ j}[p]\circ R_ j^{-1}. \]

The same obstacle bound and the $L^1$ continuity of rotations give convergence to $P_{\theta ,F}[p]$. All atomic parts vanish, so $d^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta ([\widetilde p_ j],[p])\to 0$, whereas the polar total-variation distance remains $2c$. Proposition 30.12.10 gives the implication in the other direction.

Finally, the sequence in proposition 16.6.6 converges for the zero obstacle but not for some smooth obstacle. By Step 1 of the proof of proposition 30.12.10, replacing its members by their ordinary models does not change any obstacle envelope, so $d^{\mathrm{all}}_\theta $ is strictly stronger than $\tau _{\theta ,0}$ already on ordinary models. $\square$

30.13 Background changes on curves

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

On a curve, the polar curvature separates the singularity data of a model from the area of the background. A smooth gauge change reduces any background of positive area to a positive form. Keeping the polar measure fixed preserves distances whenever both backgrounds admit it, while rescaling by the ratio of the areas identifies the entire model spaces; the gauge function controls the normalized representatives. At zero area the whole model space collapses to the unique normalized potential.

We keep the curve $Y$ of section 30.12, and we fix a smooth positive $(1,1)$-form $\eta $ on $Y$ with $\int _ Y\eta =1$, which we use for $L^1$ norms and capacities. In this section any smooth real $(1,1)$-form $\sigma $ on $Y$ with $A_\sigma =\int _ Y\sigma \geq 0$ is allowed as a background. By lemma 1.8.14, these are exactly the forms for which $\mathrm{PSH}(Y,\sigma )$ is nonempty, and $\sigma =A_\sigma \eta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h$ for a smooth real function $h$. Let $\mathcal M(\sigma )$ be the set of ordinary models in $\mathrm{PSH}(Y,\sigma )$, and put $U_\sigma (p)=\int _ Y\mathrm{MA}_\sigma (p)$. The volume comparison $d_\sigma $ is defined by the formula of definition 30.6.1; at zero area it vanishes identically. Polar curvatures, their atomic parts and ideal models are defined as before, with $\sigma $ in place of $\theta $.

Proposition 30.13.1. Polar curvature identifies $\mathcal M(\sigma )$ with the set of polar measures of mass at most $A_\sigma $. Write $p_\sigma (\mu )$ for the model with polar measure $\mu $. Then

\[ U_\sigma (p_\sigma (\mu ))=A_\sigma -\mu (Y),\qquad d_\sigma (p_\sigma (\mu ),p_\sigma (\nu ))=\| \mu -\nu \| _{\mathrm{TV}}, \]

and ideal projection corresponds to taking the atomic part. The space $(\mathcal M(\sigma ),d_\sigma )$ is complete and geodesic, and convergence for $d_\sigma $ implies convergence in $L^1$ and in capacity. If $A_\sigma =0$, its only member is the smooth potential $u_\sigma $ of lemma 1.8.14.

Proof. Suppose first that $A=A_\sigma >0$, and write $\sigma =A\eta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h$. For $v\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,A\eta )$, the inequalities for ordinary envelopes in proposition 16.3.1 give

\[ P_{A\eta }[v]+\min _ Yh \leq P_\sigma [v-h]+h \leq P_{A\eta }[v]+\max _ Yh. \]

For a model $p$ in background $A\eta $, the potential $q=P_\sigma [p-h]$ therefore satisfies $q+h\sim p$, and since the envelope depends only on the ordinary type, $P_\sigma [q]=P_\sigma [p-h]=q$. Conversely, for $q\in \mathcal M(\sigma )$, the same bounds with $v=q+h$ show that $P_{A\eta }[q+h]\sim q+h$, and taking the envelope back recovers $q$. Thus $p\mapsto P_\sigma [p-h]$ is a bijection from $\mathcal M(A\eta )$ onto $\mathcal M(\sigma )$.

Smooth gauge changes preserve curvature currents, and ordinary equivalence preserves their polar parts by corollary 1.6.8. Consequently the bijection preserves polar curvature. It also preserves singularity volumes, and subtracting the same smooth function commutes with maxima. The classification and the distance formula of lemma 30.12.1, theorem 30.12.2, applied to the positive form $A\eta $, now give the first assertions. The same gauge bounds for ideal envelopes show that the transported ideal projections are ordinarily equivalent. Both are ordinary models by corollary 16.2.4, and ordinary models of the same type are equal. Thus the bijection commutes with ideal projection, which still takes the atomic part. Completeness and the geodesics follow from the identification with polar measures, as in corollary 30.12.3.

For convergence to a model of positive mass, note that $\sigma $ is big because $\sigma +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(-h)=A\eta >0$, and that the convergence of the polar masses gives a common positive lower-volume bound; then apply proposition 30.6.3, theorem 30.7.3. For a limit of zero mass, the current estimate (30.51) follows from the same polar decomposition as before. Normalized compactness and the uniqueness of harmonic functions give $L^1$ convergence, and lemma 1.6.6 on a finite cover gives convergence in capacity.

If $A_\sigma =0$, lemma 1.8.14 leaves only $u_\sigma $ after normalization. Its curvature current vanishes, and both envelope operations fix it. All assertions reduce to statements about the zero measure and the one-point metric space. $\square$

The criterion for equality of the ordinary and ideal envelopes also depends only on the polar data, including at zero area.

Corollary 30.13.2. For $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\sigma )$ put

\[ q=P_\sigma [u]_{\mathcal I},\qquad \mu _ u=\mathbf1_{\{ u=-\infty \} }(\sigma +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u),\qquad D_ u=\sum _{y\in Y}\nu (u,y)\delta _ y. \]

The following are equivalent:

\[ \begin{gathered} u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(Y,\sigma ,q),\qquad \mu _ u=D_ u,\\ P_\sigma [u]=P_\sigma [u]_{\mathcal I},\\ P_{\sigma ,F}[u]=P_{\sigma ,F}[u]_{\mathcal I} \quad \text{for every smooth real }F. \end{gathered} \]

If $A_\sigma =0$, all four conditions hold for every $u\in \mathrm{PSH}(Y,\sigma )$.

Proof. Suppose first that $A=A_\sigma >0$, and write $\sigma =A\eta +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h$. Put $v=u+h$ and $q_+=P_{A\eta }[v]_{\mathcal I}$. The gauge change preserves the curvature current and its pole set, hence both $\mu _ u$ and $D_ u$. By proposition 16.3.1, $q+h\sim q_+$. The gauge invariance of the relative envelope class and its invariance under equivalent references (proposition 13.4.2) give

\[ u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(Y,\sigma ,q) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad v\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(Y,A\eta ,q_+). \]

By corollary 30.12.11, this is equivalent to $\mu _ u=D_ u$.

The gauge bounds for the ordinary and ideal envelopes, corollary 1.6.8 and the classifications in the positive background $A\eta $ identify the polar curvatures of $P_\sigma [u]$ and $P_\sigma [u]_{\mathcal I}$ with $\mu _ u$ and $D_ u$, respectively. Both envelopes are ordinary models, the ideal one by corollary 16.2.4. The classification of models in proposition 30.13.1 therefore shows that they are equal if and only if $\mu _ u=D_ u$.

For a smooth real $F$, the bijection $a\mapsto a+h$ between competitors gives

\[ P_{\sigma ,F}[u]+h=P_{A\eta ,F+h}[v], \]

and the same identity for the ideal envelopes. Indeed, this bijection preserves the obstacle bound, the ordinary order and multiplier-ideal equivalence, and addition of $h$ commutes with upper regularization. Since $F\mapsto F+h$ is a bijection of the smooth obstacles, corollary 30.12.11 gives the remaining equivalence.

If $A_\sigma =0$, lemma 1.8.14 gives $u=-h+c$ for a constant $c$, and the curvature current of $u$ vanishes. Thus $\mu _ u=D_ u=0$. All potentials in this background are smooth and ordinarily equivalent, so their ordinary and ideal competitor families below every obstacle coincide, and they are nonempty after a constant translation. The reference $q$ is ordinarily equivalent to $u$, and proposition 13.4.2 gives $u\in \mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(Y,\sigma ,q)$. $\square$

Theorem 30.13.3. Let $\theta _0,\theta _1$ be smooth real $(1,1)$-forms on $Y$ with positive areas $A_ i=A_{\theta _ i}$, and put $\lambda =A_1/A_0$. The map

\[ F_{10}:\mathcal M(\theta _0)\longrightarrow \mathcal M(\theta _1), \qquad F_{10}(p_{\theta _0}(\mu ))=p_{\theta _1}(\lambda \mu ), \]

is a bijection and satisfies

30.59
\begin{equation} \begin{split} d_{\theta _1}(F_{10}(p),F_{10}(q))& =\lambda d_{\theta _0}(p,q),\\ U_{\theta _1}(F_{10}(p))& =\lambda U_{\theta _0}(p),\\ P_{\theta _1}[F_{10}(p)]_{\mathcal I} & =F_{10}(P_{\theta _0}[p]_{\mathcal I}). \end{split} \end{equation}

For a third smooth background $\theta _2$ of positive area, the same construction gives $F_{21}F_{10}=F_{20}$.

Proof. Multiplication by $\lambda $ is a bijection from the polar measures of mass at most $A_0$ onto those of mass at most $A_1$. By proposition 30.13.1, it gives the asserted bijection, and the distances satisfy

\[ \| \lambda \mu -\lambda \nu \| _{\mathrm{TV}} =\lambda \| \mu -\nu \| _{\mathrm{TV}}. \]

The volume identity follows from $A_1-\lambda \mu (Y)=\lambda (A_0-\mu (Y))$. Taking the atomic part commutes with multiplication by $\lambda $, which gives the ideal-envelope identity. The area ratios multiply, proving the composition rule. $\square$

Thus, for $A_\sigma >0$, the rescaled distances $d_\sigma /A_\sigma $ make all these model spaces isometric. The unscaled spaces have diameter $2A_\sigma $: this is an upper bound for the total variation of the difference of two positive measures of mass at most $A_\sigma $, and the models of $A_\sigma \delta _ x$ and $A_\sigma \delta _ y$, $x\ne y$, attain it. Consequently the unscaled spaces can be isometric only when their areas agree.

Alternatively, $p_{\theta _0}(\mu )\mapsto p_{\theta _1}(\mu )$ is an isometry on the polar measures with $\mu (Y)\leq \min (A_0,A_1)$. The two models have the same ordinary type. Indeed, the Green representatives have the same polar term, so their difference has smooth $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$ and is smooth by elliptic regularity, while lemma 30.12.1, proposition 30.13.1 show that each model differs from its Green representative, corrected by the smooth gauge function, by a bounded function. Under this correspondence the non-pluripolar volume changes by $A_1-A_0$.

The rescaling map preserves analytic models when $\lambda $ is rational, by the convention of section 1.13: on a curve, the polar measures of analytic models are finite atomic measures with rational coefficients. If $\lambda $ is irrational, an atom $a\delta _ x$ with rational $0<a<A_0$ acquires an irrational Lelong number, so its image is not analytic in this convention. Ideal models are preserved for every $\lambda $, by (30.59).

Proposition 30.13.4. Choose a smooth real $h$ with $\theta _1=\lambda \theta _0+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h$. For $p\in \mathcal M(\theta _0)$ and $q=F_{10}(p)$,

30.63
\begin{equation} \begin{gathered} q=P_{\theta _1}[\lambda p-h],\\ \lambda p+\min _ Yh\leq q+h\leq \lambda p+\max _ Yh. \end{gathered} \end{equation}

If $U_{\theta _0}(p)=0$, then

\[ q=\lambda p-h-\sup _ Y(\lambda p-h). \]

Proof. The form $\theta _1-\lambda \theta _0$ has integral zero, so the scalar Poisson equation gives $h$, as in lemma 1.8.14. Write $p=p_{\theta _0}(\mu )$. Scaling the competitors in the ordinary envelope shows that $\lambda p$ is an ordinary model for $\lambda \theta _0$. The gauge construction in the proof of proposition 30.13.1 therefore makes $P_{\theta _1}[\lambda p-h]$ an ordinary model in background $\theta _1$. The curvature current of $\lambda p-h$ in this background is $\lambda (\theta _0+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}p)$, so its polar measure is $\lambda \mu $. The construction preserves this measure, which gives the first identity in (30.63).

The function $\lambda p-h+\min _ Yh$ is a competitor for $q$, which gives the lower bound. If $v\leq 0$ is any competitor for $q$, then $v+h-\max _ Yh$ is $\lambda \theta _0$-plurisubharmonic, nonpositive and more singular than $\lambda p$, so it is at most $\lambda p$. Taking the upper envelope gives the upper bound. These inequalities hold also at the poles, and off the common pole set they imply $|q-\lambda p|\leq \operatorname {osc}_ Yh$.

At zero mass, the polar measures exhaust the curvature currents of both $p$ and $q$. Hence $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(q-\lambda p+h)=0$, and the uniqueness of harmonic functions together with the normalization $\sup _ Yq=0$ gives the last formula. $\square$

Corollary 30.13.5. Suppose that smooth real $(1,1)$-forms $\theta _ j$ on $Y$ of positive area converge uniformly to a smooth form $\theta _0$ of positive area. Put $\lambda _ j=A_{\theta _ j}/A_{\theta _0}$, and let $F_{j0}:\mathcal M(\theta _0)\to \mathcal M(\theta _ j)$ be the correspondence of theorem 30.13.3. For every $t>0$,

30.64
\begin{equation} \begin{split} \sup _{p\in \mathcal M(\theta _0)} \| F_{j0}(p)-p\| _{L^1(Y)}& \longrightarrow 0,\\ \sup _{p\in \mathcal M(\theta _0)} \operatorname {Cap}_\eta (\{ |F_{j0}(p)-p|>t\} )& \longrightarrow 0. \end{split} \end{equation}

More generally, let $q_ j\in \mathcal M(\theta _ j)$ have polar measures $\nu _ j$, and let $p=p_{\theta _0}(\mu )$. If $\lambda _ j^{-1}\nu _ j\to \mu $ in total variation, then $q_ j\to p$ in $L^1$ and in capacity.

Proof. The inverse of the scalar Laplacian for $\eta $ on functions of mean zero has a symmetric Green kernel $G$. Set

\[ h_ j(x)=\int _ YG(x,y)\, (\theta _ j-\lambda _ j\theta _0)(y). \]

The integrand form has integral zero, so $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}h_ j=\theta _ j-\lambda _ j\theta _0$, and elliptic regularity makes $h_ j$ smooth. Symmetry and the uniform $L^1$ bound from section 19.3 give

\[ \| h_ j\| _{L^\infty (Y)} \leq C\left\| \frac{\theta _ j-\lambda _ j\theta _0}{\eta }\right\| _{L^\infty (Y)} \longrightarrow 0. \]

Here $C$ depends only on $\eta $; the symmetric kernel may be made nonpositive by subtracting a constant, which does not change $h_ j$.

By (30.63), applied with $h=h_ j$, we have outside the common poles

\[ |F_{j0}(p)-p|\leq \operatorname {osc}_ Yh_ j+|\lambda _ j-1|\, |p|. \]

Normalized compactness bounds $\| p\| _{L^1}$ uniformly over $\mathcal M(\theta _0)$, which proves the first limit. Choose $C_0>0$ with $\theta _0\leq C_0\eta $. Then $p/C_0$ is $\eta $-plurisubharmonic with supremum zero, so the uniform sublevel estimate (1.26) bounds the capacity of $\{ |\lambda _ j-1|\, |p|>t/2\} $, uniformly in $p$; since pole sets have zero capacity, this proves the second limit.

For the last assertion put $p_ j=F_{0j}(q_ j)$, so that $q_ j=F_{j0}(p_ j)$. The polar measure of $p_ j$ is $\lambda _ j^{-1}\nu _ j$, so proposition 30.13.1 gives $p_ j\to p$ in $L^1$ and in capacity. The two uniform limits in (30.64), applied to $p_ j$, give $q_ j-p_ j\to 0$ in the same senses. The triangle inequality in $L^1$ and the subadditivity of capacity complete the proof. $\square$

Finally, suppose that $\theta _ j\to \theta _0$ uniformly, where $A_{\theta _ j}\geq 0$ and $A_{\theta _0}=0$. The limiting model space is $\{ u_{\theta _0}\} $, and theorem 1.8.15 gives $L^1$ and capacity convergence to $u_{\theta _0}$, uniformly over all normalized potentials and hence over all models. The model spaces also collapse for their unscaled metrics, since their diameters are $2A_{\theta _ j}\to 0$. On the other hand, for $A_{\theta _ j}>0$ the rescaled metric $d_{\theta _ j}/A_{\theta _ j}$ makes $\mathcal M(\theta _ j)$ isometric to $\mathcal M(\eta )$, by theorem 30.13.3. Thus the rescaled metric keeps singularity data that disappear in the limiting potentials.

30.14 A complete space over varying curve backgrounds

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

The polar description also compares models in different backgrounds. Using the background as an additional coordinate gives a complete space that includes the zero-area boundary. The singularity coordinate still records the diffuse polar curvature, and taking its atomic part still gives the nearest ideal model.

We keep the compact connected curve $Y$ and the smooth positive form $\eta $ of area one. Let

\[ \mathcal B(Y)=\{ \sigma \in C^\infty (Y,\Lambda ^{1,1}_{\mathbb R}): A_\sigma =\textstyle \int _ Y\sigma \geq 0\} \]

be the set of admissible backgrounds. Choose $C^ k$ norms on smooth forms, defined by a fixed smooth metric on $Y$, and put

30.67
\begin{equation} b(\sigma ,\tau )=\sum _{k=0}^\infty 2^{-k-1} \min \{ 1,\| \sigma -\tau \| _{C^ k}\} . \end{equation}

This is a complete metric for the $C^\infty $ topology on $\mathcal B(Y)$. Indeed, a $b$-Cauchy sequence is Cauchy in every $C^ k$ norm; the limits agree and define a smooth form, whose area is nonnegative by $C^0$ convergence. Conversely, convergence in every $C^ k$ norm gives convergence for $b$: a finite part of the sum becomes small, and the tail is uniformly small.

Let $\mathcal M(\sigma )$ be the space of ordinary models of proposition 30.13.1, and define

\[ \mathscr M(Y)=\{ (\sigma ,p):\sigma \in \mathcal B(Y), p\in \mathcal M(\sigma )\} . \]

For such a pair write $\mu _ p^\sigma =\mathbf1_{\{ p=-\infty \} }(\sigma +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}p)$, and set

30.68
\begin{equation} D((\sigma ,p),(\tau ,q)) =b(\sigma ,\tau )+\| \mu _ p^\sigma -\mu _ q^\tau \| _{\mathrm{TV}}. \end{equation}

Let $\mathscr I(Y)\subset \mathscr M(Y)$ consist of the pairs $(\sigma ,p)$ with $P_\sigma [p]_{\mathcal I}=p$, and let $\mathscr A_+(Y)$ consist of the pairs $(\sigma ,p)$ for which $p$ has analytic singularities and positive non-pluripolar mass in background $\sigma $. For a finite measure $\mu $ on $Y$, write $\mu =\mu ^{\mathrm{at}}+\mu ^{\mathrm{na}}$ for its decomposition into an atomic and a nonatomic part.

Theorem 30.14.1. The metric space $(\mathscr M(Y),D)$ is complete. Its restriction to each fixed-background fiber is the polar metric $d_\sigma $. The map

\[ R(\sigma ,p)=(\sigma ,P_\sigma [p]_{\mathcal I}) \]

is a $1$-Lipschitz retraction onto the closed subspace $\mathscr I(Y)$, and $R(\sigma ,p)$ is the unique ideal pair nearest to $(\sigma ,p)$. Moreover,

30.69
\begin{equation} \overline{\mathscr A_+(Y)}^{\, D}=\mathscr I(Y),\qquad \operatorname {dist}_ D((\sigma ,p),\mathscr I(Y)) =(\mu _ p^\sigma )^{\mathrm{na}}(Y). \end{equation}

If $(\sigma _ j,p_ j)\to (\sigma ,p)$ for $D$, then $p_ j\to p$ in $L^1(Y,\eta )$ and in capacity for $\eta $, including when $A_\sigma =0$.

Proof. By proposition 30.13.1, polar curvature identifies $\mathscr M(Y)$ with the set of pairs $(\sigma ,\mu )$ in which $\sigma \in \mathcal B(Y)$ and $\mu $ is a polar measure of mass at most $A_\sigma $. In these coordinates, (30.68) is the restriction of the product metric $b+\| \cdot \| _{\mathrm{TV}}$, so it separates pairs and restricts to $d_\sigma $ on every fiber.

Suppose that $(\sigma _ j,\mu _ j)$ is a Cauchy sequence. Completeness of $b$ gives $\sigma _ j\to \sigma $ in the $C^\infty $ topology, and completeness of the space of finite signed measures gives $\mu _ j\to \mu $ in total variation. The limit $\mu $ is positive and satisfies $\mu (Y)\leq A_\sigma $. Choose polar sets $E_ j$ carrying the $\mu _ j$. Their union $E$ is polar, and

\[ \mu (Y\setminus E)\leq \| \mu -\mu _ j\| _{\mathrm{TV}}\longrightarrow 0. \]

Thus $\mu $ is a polar measure and defines a model in background $\sigma $. This proves completeness.

Ideal projection takes the atomic part, by proposition 30.13.1. Taking the atomic part does not increase total variation, so $R$ is $1$-Lipschitz. The positive atomic measures are closed in total variation, since a limit is carried by the countable union of the sets of atoms of the sequence. Therefore $\mathscr I(Y)$ is closed. Let $\mu $ be the polar measure of $p$. For every ideal pair $(\tau ,q)$, whose polar measure $\nu $ is atomic, mutual singularity gives

\[ D((\sigma ,p),(\tau ,q)) =b(\sigma ,\tau )+\mu ^{\mathrm{na}}(Y) +\| \mu ^{\mathrm{at}}-\nu \| _{\mathrm{TV}}. \]

Its unique minimum is attained at $\tau =\sigma $ and $\nu =\mu ^{\mathrm{at}}$, that is, at $R(\sigma ,p)$. This proves the retraction and distance assertions.

For the analytic closure, let $(\sigma ,q)$ be an ideal pair. If $A_\sigma >0$, finite atomic measures with rational coefficients and total mass strictly less than $A_\sigma $ approximate every positive atomic measure of mass at most $A_\sigma $ in total variation. Their models in background $\sigma $ are analytic, by the classification on curves and the gauge transport of proposition 30.13.1, and they have positive non-pluripolar mass. If $A_\sigma =0$, the polar measure of $q$ is zero. The pairs with backgrounds $\sigma +j^{-1}\eta $ and zero polar measure converge to $(\sigma ,q)$ for $D$. Their models are bounded, so they have analytic singularities with trivial ideal, and their non-pluripolar masses are $j^{-1}>0$. Hence every ideal pair lies in the closure of $\mathscr A_+(Y)$. Conversely, an analytic model is an ideal model by proposition 16.2.7, and $\mathscr I(Y)$ is closed. This proves (30.69).

Finally, let $(\sigma _ j,p_ j)\to (\sigma ,p)$, and write $\mu _ j=\mu _{p_ j}^{\sigma _ j}$ and $\mu =\mu _ p^\sigma $. If $A_\sigma >0$, put $\lambda _ j=A_{\sigma _ j}/A_\sigma $. For all sufficiently large $j$ these numbers are positive and tend to one, and

\[ \| \lambda _ j^{-1}\mu _ j-\mu \| _{\mathrm{TV}} \leq \lambda _ j^{-1}\| \mu _ j-\mu \| _{\mathrm{TV}} +|\lambda _ j^{-1}-1|\, \mu (Y)\longrightarrow 0. \]

Now apply corollary 30.13.5. If $A_\sigma =0$, the backgrounds converge uniformly and all areas are nonnegative, and the uniform collapse of theorem 1.8.15 gives $L^1$ and capacity convergence to the unique normalized potential $u_\sigma =p$ in background $\sigma $. $\square$

The $C^\infty $ topology on backgrounds is needed for completeness: a sequence of smooth forms that is Cauchy for the uniform norm can have a limit that is not smooth. On a fixed background of positive area, $D$ induces on models of positive mass the topology of theorem 30.11.3, but it allows sequences to converge to models of zero mass. In particular, the Cauchy sequences for $D$ need not satisfy the condition on reciprocal volumes in that theorem.

Smooth gauge changes and positive scaling act compatibly on all fibers of the joint space.

Corollary 30.14.2. For $f\in C^\infty (Y,\mathbb R)$, the map

\[ \mathcal G_ f(\sigma ,p) =(\sigma +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f,P_{\sigma +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f}[p-f]) \]

is an isometry of $\mathscr M(Y)$, with inverse $\mathcal G_{-f}$ and $\mathcal G_ g\mathcal G_ f=\mathcal G_{f+g}$. It preserves analytic models and commutes with $R$.

For $\lambda >0$, the bijection $\mathcal H_\lambda (\sigma ,p)=(\lambda \sigma ,\lambda p)$ commutes with $R$ and satisfies

\[ \min \{ 1,\lambda \} D(x,y) \leq D(\mathcal H_\lambda x,\mathcal H_\lambda y) \leq \max \{ 1,\lambda \} D(x,y). \]

Proof. The gauge transport in proposition 30.13.1 preserves the polar measure and, after subtraction of $f$, the ordinary type. In the coordinates $(\sigma ,\mu )$ of the proof of theorem 30.14.1, $\mathcal G_ f$ is therefore the map $(\sigma ,\mu )\mapsto (\sigma +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f,\mu )$. It does not change the area, and $b(\sigma +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f,\tau +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}f)=b(\sigma ,\tau )$. This proves that $\mathcal G_ f$ is an isometry and the laws for inverses and compositions. Taking the atomic part commutes with this map, and subtraction of a smooth function and bounded changes preserve analytic singularities.

Scaling the competitors of the ordinary envelope shows that $\lambda p$ is an ordinary model for $\lambda \sigma $. In the coordinates $(\sigma ,\mu )$, $\mathcal H_\lambda $ is the map $(\sigma ,\mu )\mapsto (\lambda \sigma ,\lambda \mu )$, with inverse $\mathcal H_{\lambda ^{-1}}$, and taking the atomic part commutes with scaling. For $r\geq 0$,

\[ \min \{ 1,\lambda \} \min \{ 1,r\} \leq \min \{ 1,\lambda r\} \leq \max \{ 1,\lambda \} \min \{ 1,r\} . \]

Apply this to every $C^ k$ norm in (30.67); the total-variation term is multiplied by $\lambda $. The two bounds follow. $\square$
