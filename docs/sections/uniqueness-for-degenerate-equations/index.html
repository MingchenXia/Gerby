12.3 Uniqueness for degenerate equations

Definitions, results, and proofs on this subject.

11 Uniqueness for degenerate equations

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Let $X$ be a connected compact complex manifold of dimension $n\geq 1$, let $\omega $ be a Hermitian form, and let $\mathrm dV$ be a smooth positive volume form. For a nonzero nonnegative density $f\in L^ p(X,\mathrm dV)$, $p>1$, the preceding chapter determines the constant $c$ in $(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^ n=cf\, \mathrm dV$. We now ask whether the potential is determined up to a constant.

For real potentials $u,v$, with the regularity stated in each result, set

\[ A=\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u,\qquad B=\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v,\qquad w=u-v, \qquad T=\sum _{j=0}^{n-1}A^ j\wedge B^{n-1-j}. \]

Then

11.1
\begin{equation} \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\wedge T=A^ n-B^ n. \end{equation}

For $A,B\geq 0$ with equal Monge–Ampère measures, $w$ solves $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\wedge T=0$, whose cofactor may be degenerate or nonclosed. Coefficient products for Sobolev potentials are interpreted almost everywhere. For $C^{1,1}$ solutions with a common continuous density, perturbing $w$ locates its extrema in elliptic regions; the maximum principle propagates their values along the corresponding components.

For $C^2$ potentials, a continuous weight $h>0$ with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(hT)=0$ in distributions gives a global energy identity forcing $\mathrm dw$ to vanish in the positive directions of the cofactor.

For bounded solutions, an energy measure replaces the pointwise gradient calculation. A continuous density whose positive set is connected and dense then gives uniqueness. When the background has invariant mass, ordinary comparison proves uniqueness for nonzero nonnegative $L^ p$ densities, $p>1$, even if the density vanishes on an open set.

The examples on $\mathbb P^3$ have bounded mass and a smooth nonclosed Hermitian background, yet distinct smooth normalized solutions share one density. Its zero set separates the elliptic regions: it can be an open band or two hypersurfaces with quadratic vanishing, even for a real analytic density. Finite upper mass therefore does not ensure uniqueness.

11.1 Extrema and density sublevels

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

To apply the strong maximum principle to $w=u-v$, we first locate a maximum point and a minimum point of $w$ in the region where the linear equation $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\wedge T=0$ is uniformly elliptic. Maximizing a small perturbation of $w$ by a function $\chi $ with $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\chi >0$ does this, even when the Hessians are only measurable. The strong maximum principle then makes $w$ constant on the components of this region that contain these points, and $w$ is constant if the closures of the two components meet.

In this section, $u,v\in C^{1,1}(X)$ satisfy $A,B\geq 0$ almost everywhere and

11.2
\begin{equation} A^ n=B^ n=f\, \mathrm{d}V,\qquad f\in C^0(X),\qquad f\geq 0. \end{equation}

Fix a smooth positive $(1,1)$-form $\beta $, put $H=A+B$, and let $E_{\rm unif}$ be the set of points near which $H$ is uniformly positive:

11.3
\begin{equation} E_{\rm unif} =\left\{ x:\begin{array}{l} \text{there are a neighborhood }V\ni x\text{ and }c>0\\ \text{with }H\geq c\beta \text{ almost everywhere on }V \end{array}\right\} . \end{equation}

This open set does not depend on $\beta $ or on the choice of representatives of the measurable coefficients.

Theorem 11.1.1. Assume (11.2). If $\chi \in C^2(X,\mathbb R)$ satisfies $C:=\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\chi >0$, put

\[ P_\chi =\{ x\in X:f(x)\mathrm{d}V_ x\geq C_ x^ n\} ,\qquad U=\{ f>0\} . \]

Then $P_\chi $ is a nonempty compact subset of $U$, and $U\subset E_{\rm unif}$. There are components $G_+,G_-$ of $E_{\rm unif}$, each meeting $P_\chi $, such that $w=u-v$ satisfies

11.4
\begin{equation} w\equiv \max _ Xw\quad \hbox{on }\overline{G_+},\qquad w\equiv \min _ Xw\quad \hbox{on }\overline{G_-}. \end{equation}

If $w$ is nonconstant, these closures are disjoint. Consequently $w$ is constant if any one of the following holds:

  1. every two components of $E_{\rm unif}$ meeting $P_\chi $ have intersecting closures;

  2. $E_{\rm unif}$ is connected;

  3. $E_{\rm unif}$ is dense and has at most two components.

The assertions about components, including the three criteria, also hold with $U$ in place of $E_{\rm unif}$. In particular, $w$ is constant when $\{ f=0\} $ is a nonempty connected embedded $C^1$ real hypersurface; this hypersurface need not be orientable or coorientable.

Since the Hessians of $C^{1,1}$ functions exist only almost everywhere, the contact argument must use points where they exist. The following lemma finds such points near a maximum; it also provides the strict comparison step in the measurable strong maximum principle.

Lemma 11.1.2. In Euclidean space $\mathbb R^ m$, let $B(p,r)$ be the ball of center $p$ and radius $r>0$, and let $I$ be the identity matrix. Let $g\in C^{1,1}$ near $\overline{B(p,r)}\subset \mathbb R^ m$, suppose $g\leq g(p)$ on the ball, and let $E$ be any subset of full measure in the ball at whose points $g$ has a second-order Taylor expansion. For every $\delta >0$ and $0<\rho <r$, there is $x\in E\cap B(p,\rho )$ such that

\[ D^2g(x)\leq 2\delta I. \]

Proof. Put $g_\delta (x)=g(x)-\delta |x-p|^2$. The gradient map $F=\nabla g_\delta $ is Lipschitz on the ball, so it maps the null set $N=\overline{B(p,r)}\setminus E$ to a null set. Choose $s\in B(0,\delta \rho )\setminus F(N)$ and maximize $g_\delta (x)-s\cdot (x-p)$ on the closed ball. At any point with $|x-p|\geq \rho $, its value is strictly smaller than its value at $p$, because

\[ g(x)-g(p)-\delta |x-p|^2-s\cdot (x-p) \leq -\delta |x-p|^2+|s|\, |x-p|<0. \]

Thus a maximizer $x$ lies in $B(p,\rho )$. The first-order condition at $x$ gives $F(x)=s$, so $x\notin N$. The second-order Taylor expansion at this upper contact point gives $D^2g(x)\leq 2\delta I$. Finally, sets $E$ as in the statement exist: by Rademacher's theorem, applied to the Lipschitz gradient of $g$, the function $g$ has a second-order Taylor expansion almost everywhere. $\square$

Lemma 11.1.3. Let $a=(a^{ij})$ be a measurable symmetric matrix field on a connected domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb R^ m$. For each compact $K\Subset \Omega $, suppose there are constants $0<\lambda _ K\leq \Lambda _ K<\infty $ such that

\[ \lambda _ K I\leq a(x)\leq \Lambda _ KI \]

almost everywhere on $K$, where $I$ is the identity matrix. Repeated indices $i,j=1,\ldots ,m$ are summed, and $z_{ij}=\partial _ i\partial _ jz$. If $z\in W^{2,\infty }_{\rm loc}(\Omega )$, $a^{ij}z_{ij}\geq 0$ almost everywhere, and $z$ attains its supremum at a point of $\Omega $, then $z$ is constant.

Proof. We first prove strict weak comparison on a relatively compact domain: if $b\in C^{1,1}$, $Lb:=a^{ij}b_{ij}\geq \kappa >0$ almost everywhere, and $b\leq M$ on the boundary, then $b\leq M$ inside. Otherwise an interior maximum point lies in a closed ball on which $a\leq \Lambda I$. Apply 11.1.2 to the set of full measure where both the differential inequality and the Taylor expansion hold. Choosing $2\delta m\Lambda <\kappa $ gives the contradiction $\kappa \leq Lb\leq 2\delta \operatorname {tr}a\leq 2\delta m\Lambda $.

Take the $C^{1,1}$ representative of $z$, write $M=\sup _\Omega z$, and suppose that $K=\{ z=M\} $ is a nonempty proper subset of $\Omega $. Near a relative boundary point of $K$, choose $y\notin K$ so that the ball of radius $r=\operatorname {dist}(y,K)>0$ is compactly contained in $\Omega $ and touches $K$ at $x_0$. On the closed annulus $r/2\leq |x-y|\leq r$, use fixed bounds $\lambda I\leq a\leq \Lambda I$ and set

\[ b(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha |x-y|^2}-\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha r^2}. \]

For sufficiently large $\alpha $,

\[ Lb=\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha |x-y|^2} \bigl (4\alpha ^2 a^{ij}(x_ i-y_ i)(x_ j-y_ j) -2\alpha \operatorname {tr}a\bigr )\geq \kappa >0 \]

almost everywhere on the annulus. On its inner sphere $z<M$ uniformly; on its outer sphere $b=0$. Choose $\eta >0$ small enough that $z+\eta b\leq M$ on both spheres. Strict weak comparison, applied to $z+\eta b$, gives this inequality throughout the annulus. At $x_0$, however, $z=M$, $b=0$, $\nabla z=0$, and $b$ increases to first order in the inward radial direction, which contradicts that inequality. Thus $K$ has no relative boundary point in $\Omega $, and connectedness proves the assertion. The argument uses no derivatives of the measurable coefficients. $\square$

At a point where $A,B$ are semipositive, define the cofactor quadratic form on real cotangent vectors

11.5
\begin{equation} Q_ x(\xi )\mathrm{d}V_ x =\mathrm{i}\, \xi ^{1,0}\wedge \overline{\xi ^{1,0}}\wedge T_ x . \end{equation}

Here $\xi ^{1,0}$ is the $(1,0)$-part of $\xi $, and forms of bidegree $(n-1,n-1)$ are ordered by testing against $\mathrm i\xi ^{1,0}\wedge \overline{\xi ^{1,0}}$ in this way. Mixed products of semipositive $(1,1)$-forms are semipositive in this order. Expanding $H^{n-1}$, where $H=A+B$, gives

11.6
\begin{equation} 2^{-(n-1)}H^{n-1}\leq T\leq H^{n-1}. \end{equation}

At a point, choose a $\beta $-unitary coframe $\mathrm{d}z_1,\ldots ,\mathrm{d}z_ n$ that diagonalizes $H$, and write $\lambda _1,\ldots ,\lambda _ n$ for the eigenvalues of $H$. For a $(1,0)$-covector $\alpha =\sum _{k=1}^ n b_ k\, \mathrm{d}z_ k$ with complex coefficients $b_ k$, the coefficient of $\mathrm{i}\alpha \wedge \bar\alpha \wedge H^{n-1}$ in this coframe is a positive constant depending only on $n$, times

11.7
\begin{equation} \sum _{k=1}^ n |b_ k|^2\prod _{\ell \ne k}\lambda _\ell . \end{equation}

For $n\geq 2$, (11.6) and (11.7) show that $Q$ is locally uniformly elliptic exactly on $E_{\rm unif}$, that is, where $H$ has a local essential positive lower bound. The implication from ellipticity of $Q$ to a lower bound for $H$ uses the local upper bound for $H$: testing each eigendirection bounds every product $\prod _{\ell \ne k}\lambda _\ell $ below, and choosing $k$ different from an index of a smallest eigenvalue then bounds that eigenvalue below. For $n=1$ one has $T=1$, so the linearized equation is elliptic everywhere.

of 11.1.1. We first locate the extrema. Fix $s>0$ and consider a maximum point of

11.8
\begin{equation} w-s(v-\chi ). \end{equation}

For smooth data, the complex Hessian of this function is nonpositive at a maximum point, that is,

11.9
\begin{equation} (1+s)B\geq A+sC. \end{equation}

Since $sC>0$, this gives $B>0$, and since $A^ n=B^ n$, also $A>0$. In a $B$-unitary frame put $J=B^{-1/2}AB^{-1/2}$, $K=B^{-1/2}CB^{-1/2}$. Since $\det J=1$, arithmetic–geometric mean and (11.9) give

\[ n+s\operatorname {tr}K\leq \operatorname {tr}J+s\operatorname {tr}K \leq n(1+s). \]

Consequently $\operatorname {tr}K\leq n$, $B\geq C/n$, and $\det K\leq (\operatorname {tr}K/n)^ n\leq 1$. In particular $B^ n\geq C^ n$.

For $C^{1,1}$ potentials, fix a maximum point $p$ of (11.8) and apply 11.1.2 on a coordinate ball, with radii tending to zero and errors $\delta _ j\downarrow 0$. For the set of full measure in that lemma, take the points where both Hessians are defined and semipositive and have the common determinant given by (11.2). With $\beta _0=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}|z|^2$, the resulting points $x_ j\to p$ satisfy

\[ A(x_ j)+s C_ j(x_ j)\leq (1+s)B(x_ j),\qquad C_ j=C-(\delta _ j/s)\beta _0>0 \]

for large $j$. The matrix argument just given yields $f(x_ j)\mathrm{d}V_{x_ j}\geq C_ j(x_ j)^ n$. Since $f$, $\mathrm{d}V$ and $C$ are continuous, letting $j\to \infty $ gives $p\in P_\chi $; no continuity of the Hessians is needed. Thus every maximum point of the perturbed function lies in $P_\chi $. The set $P_\chi $ is closed, by continuity of $f$, $\mathrm{d}V$ and $C$, hence compact, and it is contained in $U$ because $C^ n>0$. Letting $s\downarrow 0$, a limit point of these maximum points is a maximum point of $w$ lying in $P_\chi $. Applying the same argument to $-w$ gives a minimum point of $w$ in $P_\chi $ as well. In particular, $P_\chi $ is nonempty.

On a relatively compact ball in $U$, the determinants of $A$ and $B$ have a positive lower bound, while their coefficients have an essential upper bound. Every eigenvalue is therefore bounded below by the determinant lower bound divided by a fixed power of the upper bound. Thus $U\subset E_{\rm unif}$.

By (11.1), $w$ solves a linear equation in nondivergence form with measurable coefficients, whose principal quadratic form is a fixed positive multiple of $Q$. On each relatively compact coordinate ball in $E_{\rm unif}$, these coefficients are uniformly elliptic. 11.1.3, applied to $w$ and to $-w$, makes $w$ constant on the component of $E_{\rm unif}$ containing the maximum point found above, and constant on the component containing the minimum point found above. Continuity extends the two constant values to the closures, proving (11.4). Distinct extrema force disjoint closures.

The same argument applies on $U$ itself, since $U$ contains the extremal points found in $P_\chi $ and the cofactor is locally uniformly elliptic on $U\subset E_{\rm unif}$. This proves the assertions with $U$ in place of $E_{\rm unif}$.

The component criteria are immediate except for the dense two-component case. If an open dense subset of connected $X$ has at most two components with disjoint closures, those closures are disjoint nonempty closed sets whose union is $X$, contradicting connectedness. Finally, when $Z=\{ f=0\} $ is a connected embedded real hypersurface, flattening charts assign to each point of $Z$ the unordered pair of components containing its two local sides. This pair is locally constant along $Z$, so connectedness makes it independent of the point; the two labels may coincide or be exchanged along a loop. Every component of $X\setminus Z$ has a nonempty boundary in $Z$, since otherwise it would be both open and closed in connected $X$. Near such a boundary point it contains a local side. Thus the pair accounts for every component, and $U$ has at most two components. It is dense because a hypersurface has empty interior. This proves the last assertion without choosing a global side. $\square$

In this argument, the $C^{1,1}$ bound controls the measurable coefficients, and continuity of the density places the limiting contact points in $\{ f>0\} $. The next criterion gives a quantitative condition for $\{ f>0\} $ to be connected.

11.1.1 A square-root threshold

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

A nonnegative $C^{1,1}$ density grows at most quadratically away from each of its zeros. If its zero set separates a small ball, a family of parallel segments crossing the zero set therefore contributes at least a constant times $\sqrt t$ to the volume of $\{ f\leq t\} $ for small $t>0$. Excluding this lower bound makes $\{ f>0\} $ connected, and 11.1.1 then gives uniqueness.

Write $\operatorname {Vol}(E)=\int _ E\mathrm{d}V$ for Borel sets $E$.

Theorem 11.1.4. Let $u,v\in C^2(X)$ satisfy $A,B\geq 0$ and $A^ n=B^ n=f\, \mathrm{d}V$, where $f\geq 0$ is $C^{1,1}$. If

11.10
\begin{equation} \liminf _{t\downarrow 0} \frac{\operatorname {Vol}\{ f\leq t\} }{\sqrt t}=0, \end{equation}

then $\{ f>0\} $ is connected and $u-v$ is constant. In particular this holds when $f^{-1/2}\in L^1(\mathrm{d}V)$, with $f^{-1/2}=+\infty $ on $\{ f=0\} $. The exponent $1/2$ is sharp in every dimension $n\geq 3$.

Proof. Write $Z=\{ f=0\} $. Condition (11.10) first implies $\operatorname {Vol}(Z)=0$. We show that the complement of $Z$ in every sufficiently small coordinate ball is connected. Fix such a ball with convex Euclidean image. If the complement of $Z$ in it were disconnected, choose points $a,b$ in different components and small closed disks centered at $a$ and $b$, perpendicular to the segment $[a,b]$ and contained in those respective components. By making the disks small, all parallel segments between corresponding points stay in the ball. Each segment meets $Z$, since otherwise it would connect the two components.

On a fixed coordinate region containing the closed ball, the gradient of $f$ is Lipschitz with some constant $K>0$. Since $f\geq 0$, we have $\nabla f(z)=0$ at every zero $z$, hence

11.11
\begin{equation} f(x)\leq \frac K2|x-z|^2 \end{equation}

whenever the segment from $z$ to $x$ stays in that region. The endpoint disks are compact and disjoint from $Z$, so the parts of the segments near them contain no zeros. Thus every parallel segment contains a zero at a fixed positive distance from its endpoints. For small $t>0$, (11.11) gives on that segment an interval of length $2\sqrt{2t/K}$ contained in $\{ f\leq t\} $. Integrating over the transverse disk, which has positive measure, and using that $\mathrm{d}V$ is comparable to coordinate volume, we obtain

\[ \operatorname {Vol}\{ f\leq t\} \geq c\sqrt t \]

for all sufficiently small $t$, contradicting (11.10). This proves the local connectedness assertion.

For each such ball, the complement of $Z$ in it is nonempty, since $Z$ has zero volume, and it lies in one component of $U=X\setminus Z$. Two overlapping balls give the same component, because their open overlap contains a point outside $Z$. Since $X$ is connected, all these balls give the same component. Hence $U$ is connected, and 11.1.1 shows that $u-v$ is constant.

If $f^{-1/2}\in L^1$, then, outside the null zero set,

\[ \frac{\operatorname {Vol}\{ f\leq t\} }{\sqrt t} \leq \int _{\{ f\leq t\} }f^{-1/2}\, \mathrm{d}V\longrightarrow 0 \]

by dominated convergence. This proves the sufficient integrability condition. The sharpness assertion follows from the examples in 11.9, as quantified at the end of that section. $\square$

11.2 Polarized cofactors and propagation

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Uniform ellipticity is sufficient for the propagation of an extremum, but it is not necessary: the directions in which the cofactor is positive may already connect enough points to force constancy. For $C^2$ potentials, the quadratic form $Q$ in (11.5) is continuous, and its polarization defines $Q^\sharp :T^*X\to TX$ by $\eta (Q^\sharp \xi )=Q(\xi ,\eta )$. The reachable set of a point consists of the endpoints of absolutely continuous curves starting there whose velocity lies in $\operatorname {im}Q^\sharp $ almost everywhere. The control orbit of a point consists of the endpoints of finite concatenations of flow lines, starting there, of locally Lipschitz vector fields with values in $\operatorname {im}Q^\sharp $.

A positive weight $h$ satisfying $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(hT)=0$ gives an energy identity that controls $\mathrm{d}w$ everywhere. Without such a weight, a degenerate maximum principle gives propagation only from the extremal sets. This distinction accounts for the different density assumptions on reachable sets and on orbits in the following theorem.

Theorem 11.2.1. Let $u,v\in C^2(X)$ satisfy $A,B\geq 0$ and $A^ n=B^ n$, and put $w=u-v$.

  1. If there is a continuous $h>0$ such that $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(hT)=0$ in distributions, then

    11.12
    \begin{equation} \mathrm{i}\, \partial w\wedge \bar\partial w\wedge T\equiv 0. \end{equation}

    It follows that $w$ is constant if the set where $T$ is strictly positive is dense, or if the reachable set of one point is dense.

  2. Without the weight hypothesis, the sets where $w$ attains its maximum and its minimum are invariant under the flow of every locally Lipschitz vector field with values in $\operatorname {im}Q^\sharp $. If every control orbit is dense, then $w$ is constant. For smooth data it is enough that smooth vector fields with values in $\operatorname {im}Q^\sharp $, for example the columns of the symbol $a$ in (11.18) below, together with their iterated Lie brackets, span $TX$ at every point.

11.2.1 Weighted energy

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Testing the weighted cofactor against $w^2$ produces a nonnegative gradient integral. Only distributional $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$-closedness of $hT$ is needed: for a continuous $h>0$, the condition is

11.13
\begin{equation} \int _ X\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\varphi \wedge hT=0\qquad (\varphi \in C^\infty (X,\mathbb R)). \end{equation}

of 11.2.1(1). The product rule gives

11.14
\begin{equation} \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(w^2)=2w\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w+2\mathrm{d}w\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w =2w\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\pi }\partial w\wedge \bar\partial w. \end{equation}

Approximate $w^2$ in $C^2$ by smooth functions $\varphi $ in (11.13). Since $hT$ is continuous and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\wedge T=0$ by (11.1), this gives

11.15
\begin{equation} 0=\frac1\pi \int _ X h\, \mathrm{i}\partial w\wedge \bar\partial w\wedge T. \end{equation}

The continuous integrand is nonnegative, so it vanishes identically, which is (11.12).

By (11.5), (11.12) says that $Q(\mathrm{d}w)=0$. For the nonnegative quadratic form $Q$, this implies $Q(\mathrm{d}w,\eta )=0$ for every $\eta $. Therefore

11.16
\begin{equation} \mathrm{d}w_ x\bigl (Q_ x^\sharp \eta \bigr )=0\qquad (x\in X,\ \eta \in T_ x^*X). \end{equation}

By the chain rule and (11.16), $w$ is constant along every absolutely continuous curve whose velocity lies in $\operatorname {im}Q^\sharp $ almost everywhere. Thus $w$ is constant on each reachable set, and by continuity one dense reachable set suffices. If instead $Q$ is positive definite on a dense set, (11.15) gives $\mathrm{d}w=0$ there, and hence everywhere. $\square$

The argument uses only the test function $w^2$. For general test functions, integration by parts introduces derivatives of $hT$; the condition $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(hT)=0$ alone does not make $L\varphi =(\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\varphi \wedge T)/\mathrm{d}V$ a symmetric operator in divergence form.

11.2.2 The real symbol and exterior-ball propagation

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

To propagate an extremum without a weight, we write the linearized operator in real coordinates. Its symbol is continuous and semipositive. At a point where a ball outside the maximum set touches it, the normal direction must lie in the kernel of the symbol; this forces the maximum set to be invariant under the flows of vector fields with values in the range of the symbol.

In a holomorphic chart $z_ j=x_ j+\mathrm{i} y_ j$, let $C_{\rm cf}$ be the Hermitian matrix characterized by

11.17
\begin{equation} \frac{\bigl (\mathrm{i}\sum H_{j\bar k}\mathrm{d}z_ j\wedge \mathrm{d}\bar z_ k\bigr )\wedge T}{\mathrm{d}V} =\operatorname {Tr}(C_{\rm cf}H) \end{equation}

for every Hermitian matrix $H=(H_{j\bar k})$. Positivity of mixed products makes $C_{\rm cf}$ positive semidefinite. Write $C_{\rm cf}=P_{\rm cf}+\mathrm{i} Q_{\rm cf}$, where $P_{\rm cf}$ is real symmetric and $Q_{\rm cf}$ is real skew-symmetric. In the real coordinate order $(x_1,\ldots ,x_ n,y_1,\ldots ,y_ n)$,

11.18
\begin{equation} L\varphi :=\frac{\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\varphi \wedge T}{\mathrm{d}V} =\operatorname {Tr}(aD^2\varphi ),\qquad a=\frac1{8\pi } \begin{pmatrix} P_{\rm cf} & Q_{\rm cf} \\ -Q_{\rm cf} & P_{\rm cf} \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation}

Indeed $\varphi _{j\bar k}=(\varphi _{x_ jx_ k}+\varphi _{y_ jy_ k} +\mathrm{i}\varphi _{x_ jy_ k}-\mathrm{i}\varphi _{y_ jx_ k})/4$; inserting this expression in (11.17) proves (11.18). For real coordinate vectors $\xi ,\eta \in \mathbb R^ n$, moreover,

\[ (\xi ,\eta )^ Ta(\xi ,\eta ) =\frac1{8\pi }(\xi -\mathrm{i}\eta )^*C_{\rm cf}(\xi -\mathrm{i}\eta )\geq 0. \]

Thus $a$ is a continuous positive semidefinite principal symbol, the operator $L$ has no first-order term in holomorphic coordinates, and the range of $a$ is that of $Q^\sharp $.

An exterior-ball argument propagates the maximum for this operator. Let $W\in C^2$ satisfy $LW\geq 0$ in a chart, suppose $W\leq M$, and put $K=\{ W=M\} $. If $z\in K$ is a point of $K$ nearest to a point $y\notin K$ of the chart, then

11.19
\begin{equation} (y-z)^ Ta(z)(y-z)=0. \end{equation}

To prove this, suppose the left side is positive, set $r_0=|y-z|$, and shrink the tangent ball toward $z$: $y'=z+\theta (y-z)$, $R=\theta r_0$, with $0<\theta <1$ small. The closed ball $\overline{B(y',R)}$ lies in the chart and meets $K$ only at $z$. This follows from the triangle inequality; equality in $|k-y|\leq |k-y'|+|y'-y|\leq r_0$, for $k\in K$, forces $k=z$. For large $\alpha >0$, the function

\[ \psi (x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha R^2}-\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha |x-y'|^2} \]

satisfies

\[ L\psi =2\alpha \mathrm{e}^{-\alpha |x-y'|^2} \bigl (\operatorname {tr}a-2\alpha (x-y')^ Ta(x)(x-y')\bigr )<0 \]

in a neighborhood of $z$, because $z-y'$ is a nonzero multiple of $y-z$, so that $(z-y')^ Ta(z)(z-y')>0$ by assumption. Integration against $w^2$ also measures the obstruction to using the unweighted cofactor, that is, the case $h=1$. For smooth potentials, the error is exactly the pairing of $w^2$ with $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T$. On a surface, the Gauduchon condition makes this error vanish for every pair of potentials; in dimension three, $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T$ contains an additional term depending on the potentials.

Proposition 11.2.2. Let $n\geq 2$, and let $u,v$ be smooth $\omega $-plurisubharmonic functions with $A^ n=B^ n=f\, \mathrm{d}V$ for a smooth function $f\geq 0$. Then

11.20
\begin{equation} \int _ X \mathrm{d}w\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w\wedge T =\frac12\int _ X w^2\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T. \end{equation}

Proof. By (11.1), $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\wedge T=0$. Integrating (11.14) against $T$, and applying Stokes' theorem twice to the left-hand side, yields

\[ \int _ X\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(w^2)\wedge T=\int _ Xw^2\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T. \]

Substitution proves (11.20).

For the uniqueness assertion, apply 11.2.1(1) with $h=1$. At every point where $f>0$, the form $B$ is positive definite, so the term $B^{n-1}$ makes the cofactor $T$ strictly positive. The set where $T$ is strictly positive is therefore dense, as required. $\square$

Proof. Here $T=A+B$ and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T=2\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega =0$. The set $\{ f>0\} $ is dense, so the proposition, applied to the density $cf$, makes the difference of the two solutions constant, and the normalization makes this constant zero. $\square$

11.3 Integral rigidity in dimensions two and three

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

In dimensions two and three, if two positive forms $A,B$ have equal determinants, an algebraic expression quadratic in $A-B$ has a strict sign unless the two forms agree. On a surface its integral vanishes because it is exact. On a threefold the integral is a term involving $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega $, so a sign condition on $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega $ leads to the same conclusion. Approximation in the Sobolev norm justifies the integral identities for $W^{2,n}$ potentials.

In this section $n\in \{ 2,3\} $. Norms of coefficients of forms are taken with respect to a fixed smooth metric and $\mathrm{d}V$.

Theorem 11.3.1. Let $u,v\in W^{2,n}(X,\mathbb R)$, and assume that $A,B\geq 0$ almost everywhere and

\[ A^ n=B^ n=f\, \mathrm{d}V,\qquad f>0\quad \hbox{almost everywhere}. \]

If $n=3$, assume in addition that

11.21
\begin{equation} \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega \wedge \mathrm{i}\eta \wedge \bar\eta \leq 0 \end{equation}

for every $(1,0)$-covector $\eta $; for $n=2$ no condition on $\omega $ is needed. Then $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(u-v)=0$ in the sense of distributions, and $u-v$ is almost everywhere constant. For the $\omega $-plurisubharmonic representatives of $u$ and $v$, the difference is constant everywhere.

Proof. Put

\[ \begin{gathered} s=(u+v)/2,\qquad D=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w=A-B,\\ C=(A+B)/2=\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}s. \end{gathered} \]

The coefficients of $C$ and $D$ lie in $L^ n$, so their products of top degree are integrable by Hölder's inequality. At almost every point the common determinant is positive, so $A$ and $B$ are positive definite there, and we may diagonalize them simultaneously by congruence: for a suitable coframe of $(1,0)$-covectors $\theta _1,\ldots ,\theta _ n$, write $A=\sum e_ j$, $B=\sum \lambda _ j e_ j$, where $e_ j=\mathrm{i}\theta _ j\wedge \bar\theta _ j$, $\lambda _ j>0$, and $\prod \lambda _ j=1$.

In dimension two,

11.22
\begin{equation} D^2=A^2+B^2-2A\wedge B =2(2-\lambda _1-\lambda _2)e_1\wedge e_2\leq 0, \end{equation}

with equality if and only if $A=B$. In dimension three,

11.23
\begin{equation} 2C\wedge D^2=A^3+B^3-A^2\wedge B-A\wedge B^2\leq 0. \end{equation}

Indeed, with $E=e_1\wedge e_2\wedge e_3$, the four terms on the right are

\[ 6E,\qquad 6E,\qquad 2\Bigl (\sum _ j\lambda _ j\Bigr )E, \qquad 2\Bigl (\sum _{i<j}\lambda _ i\lambda _ j\Bigr )E. \]

By the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality and $\prod _ j\lambda _ j=1$, each sum is at least three, and equality forces all $\lambda _ j=1$, that is, $A=B$.

Choose smooth real functions $w_ k\to w$ in $W^{2,n}$ and set $D_ k=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w_ k$; such approximations are obtained by a partition of unity and convolution, and they need not preserve semipositivity. For $n=2$, Stokes' theorem gives $\int _ XD_ k^2=0$, since $D_ k^2=\mathrm{d}(\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w_ k\wedge D_ k)$. For a fixed norm-comparison constant $C_0>0$,

\[ \| D_ k^2-D^2\| _{L^1} \leq C_0\| D_ k-D\| _{L^2}(\| D_ k\| _{L^2}+\| D\| _{L^2})\longrightarrow 0. \]

Thus $\int _ XD^2=0$, and the nonpositive integrand in (11.22) vanishes almost everywhere. By the equality case, $D=0$ almost everywhere.

For $n=3$, also choose smooth functions $s_ k\to s$ in $W^{2,3}$, and put $C_ k=\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}s_ k$. We need the identity

11.24
\begin{equation} \int _ X C_ k\wedge D_ k^2 =\int _ X\omega \wedge D_ k^2 =-\int _ X\mathrm{d}w_ k\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w_ k\wedge \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega . \end{equation}

The first equality holds because $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}s_ k\wedge D_ k^2=\mathrm{d}(\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}s_ k\wedge D_ k^2)$. For the second equality, including its sign, put $J_ k=\mathrm{d}w_ k\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w_ k$. Using $\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}\mathrm{d}=-\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$ and $(\mathrm d^{\mathrm c})^2=0$,

\[ \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}J_ k=-D_ k\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w_ k,\qquad \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}J_ k=-D_ k^2. \]

Stokes, first for $\mathrm{d}(\omega \wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}J_ k)$ and then for $\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}(\mathrm{d}\omega \wedge J_ k)$, gives

\begin{align*} \int _ X\omega \wedge \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}J_ k& =-\int _ X\mathrm{d}\omega \wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}J_ k,\\ 0& =-\int _ X\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega \wedge J_ k-\int _ X\mathrm{d}\omega \wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}J_ k. \end{align*}

Combining them yields (11.24).

With a fixed norm-comparison constant $C_0>0$, strong $L^3$ convergence implies convergence in $L^1$ of all products of three factors. For example,

\[ \| (C_ k-C)\wedge D_ k^2\| _1 \leq C_0\| C_ k-C\| _3\| D_ k\| _3^2\longrightarrow 0, \]

and

\[ \| C\wedge (D_ k^2-D^2)\| _1 \leq C_0\| C\| _3\| D_ k-D\| _3(\| D_ k\| _3+\| D\| _3)\longrightarrow 0. \]

Also $\mathrm{d}w_ k\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w_ k\to \mathrm{d}w\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w$ in $L^{3/2}$, so the right side of (11.24) converges. Consequently

11.25
\begin{equation} \int _ XC\wedge D^2=-\int _ X\mathrm{d}w\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w\wedge \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\omega \geq 0. \end{equation}

The sign follows from $\mathrm{d}w\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w=(\mathrm{i}/(2\pi ))\partial w\wedge \bar\partial w$ and (11.21). The integrand on the left is nonpositive by (11.23), so it vanishes almost everywhere, and the equality case, which applies where the common determinant is positive, gives $D=0$ almost everywhere.

In both dimensions, therefore, $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w=0$ in the sense of distributions. By proposition 1.1.2 with background zero, $w$ has a plurisubharmonic representative on $X$. It attains its maximum on the compact manifold $X$, so the maximum principle and connectedness make it constant.

For the $\omega $-plurisubharmonic representatives, lemma 1.3.3 upgrades the almost-everywhere equality $u=v+\text{constant}$ to equality everywhere. In particular, normalizing the means gives equality in the Sobolev class, and normalizing the suprema gives equality of the plurisubharmonic representatives. $\square$

11.4 Bounded uniqueness and connectedness

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

For a continuous density whose positive set is connected and dense, bounded solutions are unique up to a constant. An energy measure of $w=u-v$ recovers the information supplied by differentiability. Its pushforward under $w$ has a Lipschitz density vanishing at the extreme values. If $w$ were nonconstant, these properties would force both extremal sets to have zero mass for the common Monge–Ampère measure. A smooth test potential with a larger density near one of these sets then contradicts modified comparison.

Theorem 11.4.1. Let $F\in C^0(X)$ satisfy $F\geq 0$, $F\not\equiv 0$, with $U=\{ F>0\} $ connected and dense. If bounded $\omega $-plurisubharmonic functions $u,v$ satisfy

\[ \omega _ u^ n=\omega _ v^ n=F\omega ^ n, \]

Bounded solutions are continuous by corollary 10.4.3, so we may use pointwise extrema throughout the proof. We shall also use modified comparison in the following form. If $b$ is continuous and $\omega _ b\geq \varepsilon \omega $ for some $0<\varepsilon <1$, then $b/(1-\varepsilon )$ is $\omega $-plurisubharmonic, so theorem 10.2.1 applies with $(1-\varepsilon )v=b$. Thus its thin-sublevel estimate applies directly to such a strictly positive comparison potential $b$, and the correction factor tends to one as the height of the sublevel set tends to zero, with $\varepsilon $ fixed.

11.4.1 The energy of the difference

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

In this subsection, $u,v$ are continuous $\omega $-plurisubharmonic functions with $A^ n=B^ n=F\omega ^ n$ for a bounded measurable function $F\geq 0$, and $w=u-v$. Although the cofactor $T$ is now only a current, its product with $\mathrm dw\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w$ still defines a positive measure. The identity $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\wedge T=0$ determines how this energy is distributed over the level sets of $w$.

Proposition 11.4.3. There is a finite positive measure $E=\mathrm{d} w\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w\wedge T$, defined below as a limit of regularizations, with the following properties.

For every smooth real function $\chi $ on $\mathbb R$,

11.27
\begin{equation} \int _ X\chi ''(w)\, \mathrm{d} E=\int _ X\chi (w)\, \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T. \end{equation}

The signed current $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T$ is a finite measure. If $a=\min _ Xw$, $b=\max _ Xw$, and

\[ k(t)=\int _ X(w-t)_+\, \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T, \]

then $k$ is nonnegative, globally Lipschitz, supported on $[a,b]$, and

11.28
\begin{equation} w_*E=k(t)\, \mathrm{d} t. \end{equation}

In particular, $E$ charges no level set of $w$.

Let $V$ be a relatively compact coordinate domain such that $F$ has a positive essential lower bound on a neighborhood of $\overline V$. Then there is $C_ V<\infty $ such that every piecewise $C^1$ Lipschitz function $p$ on $[a,b]$ satisfies

11.29
\begin{equation} \int _ V|\mathrm{d}(p(w))|\, \mathrm{d}\lambda \leq C_ V\left(\int _ V|p'(w)|^2\, \mathrm{d} E\right)^{1/2}, \end{equation}

where $\mathrm{d}\lambda $ is Euclidean coordinate volume.

Proof. Locally, expand $T$ into powers of the smooth form $\omega $ multiplied by closed Bedford–Taylor products of $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u$ and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v$. The operator $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}$ then acts only on the powers of $\omega $, and the local mass bounds in section 1.6 show that $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T$ is a finite signed measure.

We first construct $E$ and prove (11.27). On a coordinate ball, convolve $u$, $v$ and the coefficients of $\omega $ with a common nonnegative radial mollifier. Write the resulting smooth positive forms as $A_\rho =\omega _\rho +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u_\rho $ and $B_\rho =\omega _\rho +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}v_\rho $, and let $T_\rho $ be their mixed sum. On a slightly larger ball, add the same fixed multiple of $|z|^2$ to each potential to obtain plurisubharmonic functions. Their radial regularizations decrease to the originals, so Bedford–Taylor continuity applies to all closed Hessian products. Since $u,v$ are continuous, $u_\rho ,v_\rho ,w_\rho $ converge uniformly on compact subsets.

For a closed smooth product $S_\rho $ appearing in $T_\rho $, the identity

11.30
\begin{equation} \mathrm{d} w_\rho \wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w_\rho \wedge S_\rho =\tfrac 12\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}(w_\rho ^2S_\rho )-w_\rho \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w_\rho \wedge S_\rho \end{equation}

has a distributional limit: both Hessian products on the right converge weakly with uniformly bounded local variation, and their weights converge uniformly. The same holds after multiplication by the convergent smooth factors coming from $\omega _\rho $. Thus the positive measures $E_\rho =\mathrm{d} w_\rho \wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w_\rho \wedge T_\rho $ converge to a finite positive measure $E$. By (11.30), this limit does not depend on the regularization, so the local limits agree on overlapping charts.

For smooth regularizations, the chain rule gives

\[ \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\chi (w_\rho )\wedge T_\rho =\chi ''(w_\rho )E_\rho +\chi '(w_\rho )\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w_\rho \wedge T_\rho . \]

The last current tends to zero, because $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w_\rho \wedge T_\rho =A_\rho ^ n-B_\rho ^ n$ tends weakly to $A^ n-B^ n=0$ with uniformly bounded variation, and $\chi '(w_\rho )$ converges uniformly. Passing to the limit and integrating by parts on the compact manifold $X$ gives (11.27). A partition of unity justifies this passage without a global regularization; the terms containing derivatives of the cutoff functions cancel after summation.

Let $\sigma =w_*(\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T)$ and $\nu =w_*E$. Equation (11.27) says that $\nu ''=\sigma $ as distributions on $\mathbb R$. Stokes' theorem and $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\wedge T=0$ give

\[ \sigma (\mathbb R)=\int _ X\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T=0,\qquad \int _{\mathbb R}s\, \mathrm{d}\sigma (s)=\int _ Xw\, \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}T=\int _ X\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}w\wedge T=0. \]

Consequently $k(t)=\int (s-t)_+\, \mathrm{d}\sigma (s)$ is globally Lipschitz and vanishes outside $[a,b]$. It satisfies $k''=\sigma $, so the compactly supported distribution $\nu -k(t)\, \mathrm{d} t$ has second derivative zero and therefore vanishes. Positivity of $\nu $ and continuity of $k$ imply $k\geq 0$. This proves (11.28), and the assertion about level sets follows because $\nu $ is absolutely continuous.

We now prove (11.29). On a coordinate neighborhood of $\overline V$, set $\beta =\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}|z|^2$ and choose $c_ V>0$ with $F\omega ^ n\geq c_ V\beta ^ n$. Let $A_{\mathrm{ac}}$ denote the absolutely continuous part of the current $A$, whose coefficients are locally integrable functions. Applying lemma 1.7.7 with the constant function $g=c_ V^{1/n}$ and differentiating the coefficient measures, we obtain $\det _\beta A_{\mathrm{ac}}\geq c_ V$ almost everywhere. The local Sobolev regularity in section 1.6 gives $w_\rho \to w$ strongly in $W^{1,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}$, so a subsequence of the gradients converges almost everywhere. Moreover, $A_\rho \to A_{\mathrm{ac}}$ almost everywhere, by differentiation of the coefficient measures.

Since $T_\rho $ contains the term $A_\rho ^{n-1}$ and all its terms are positive, Fatou's lemma, applied to $E_\rho $, yields the measure inequality

11.31
\begin{equation} E\geq \mathrm{d} w\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}w\wedge A_{\mathrm{ac}}^{n-1}. \end{equation}

The cofactor matrix of $A_{\mathrm{ac}}$ has smallest eigenvalue at least $\det A_{\mathrm{ac}}/\operatorname {tr}A_{\mathrm{ac}}\geq c_ V/\operatorname {tr}A_{\mathrm{ac}}$, where traces and determinants are taken with respect to $\beta $. The trace is locally integrable because $A$ has locally finite mass. The chain rule for Sobolev functions, (11.31) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality give

\[ \int _ V|\mathrm{d}(p(w))|\, \mathrm{d}\lambda \leq C\left(\int _ V|p'(w)|^2\, \mathrm{d} E\right)^{1/2} \left(\int _ V\frac{\operatorname {tr}A_{\mathrm{ac}}}{c_ V}\, \mathrm{d}\lambda \right)^{1/2}. \]

The last factor is finite and is absorbed into $C_ V$. The values of $p'$ at the finitely many corners of $p$ are immaterial, because $E$ charges no level set of $w$. $\square$

Lemma 11.4.4. In the setting of proposition 11.4.3, assume in addition that $F$ is continuous and that $U=\{ F>0\} $ is connected and dense. If $w=u-v$ is nonconstant, then

\[ F\omega ^ n(\{ w=\min _ Xw\} )=F\omega ^ n(\{ w=\max _ Xw\} )=0. \]

11.5 Comparison and invariant mass

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

11.6 Smooth nonuniqueness for a degenerate density

Here $A$ and $B$ denote coordinates, not the forms of (11.1); the two solution forms will be written $A_0$ and $B_0$.

Theorem 11.6.1. On $X=\mathbb P^3$ there exist a smooth Hermitian form $\omega $ with $\mathrm{d}\omega \ne 0$, a smooth nonnegative nonzero function $f$, and distinct smooth functions $u,v\in \mathrm{PSH}(X,\omega )$ such that

11.6.1 Three rank-one directions

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

On $U=\{ A\ne 0,B\ne 0\} $ put

\[ r=\log \frac{|A|^2}{|B|^2},\qquad P=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log |A|^2,\quad Q=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\log |B|^2,\quad R=\mathrm{d}r\wedge \mathrm d^{\mathrm c}r. \]

The logarithms are computed in a local homogeneous lift, but their Hessians do not depend on the lift. The forms $P,Q$ are pullbacks of Fubini–Study forms from the two projective lines $\mathbb P(\mathbb C^2)$. All three forms are semipositive of rank one, and

\begin{equation} P^2=Q^2=R^2=0,\qquad P\wedge Q\wedge R>0. \end{equation}

\[ \partial r=\frac{\bar a\, \mathrm{d}a}{1+|a|^2} -\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau }{\tau } -\frac{\bar b\, \mathrm{d}b}{1+|b|^2}. \]

Its $\mathrm{d}\tau $ component is nonzero, so the three rank-one forms correspond to independent directions. In particular, for nonnegative $x,y,z$,

\begin{equation} (xP+yQ+zR)^3=6xyz\, P\wedge Q\wedge R, \end{equation}

\begin{equation} \mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}G(r)=G'(r)(P-Q)+G''(r)R. \end{equation}

\begin{equation} \omega _ X=\sigma (r)P+\sigma (-r)Q+\sigma '(r)R. \end{equation}

11.6.4 Restoring strict positivity of the background

The forms $A_0,B_0$ already have the required common measure. We obtain a Hermitian background by adding an exact correction $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho $ to their average $\omega _0$ and subtracting $\rho $ from both potentials. The correction must restore positivity at $M_\pm $ while preserving it in the transition regions.

Take $\kappa \in C_ c^\infty ((-2,2))$, equal to one on $[-3/2,3/2]$. For a parameter $\varepsilon >0$ to be chosen, set

\[ \rho (r)=\varepsilon (r^2-1)\kappa (r),\qquad \omega =\omega _0+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho . \]

11.6.5 A continuum of radial solutions

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

The open band where the density vanishes allows more solutions than the two smooth ones constructed above. Writing a further potential as $G(r)-\rho $, with $G$ a function of one real variable, reduces the equation on the band to choosing the slope $G'$ so that one of the three coefficients vanishes. Switching the vanishing coefficient at two suitable levels produces a continuum of $C^{1,1}$ solutions for the same background $\omega $ and density $f$.

Recall that $q=p'$ and $z_0\geq |q|$. The strict inequality $z_0>|p'|$ near zero, where $\chi >0$, gives

11.47
\begin{equation} \int _0^1z_0(r)\, \mathrm{d}r>\int _0^1|p'(r)|\, \mathrm{d}r=p(0). \end{equation}

Proposition 11.6.2. For the smooth background $\omega $ and the smooth density $f$ of theorem 11.6.1, the equation

\[ \mathrm{MA}_\omega (u)=f\omega _ X^3 \]

has uncountably many distinct $C^{1,1}$ solutions normalized by $\sup _ Xu=0$. If the cutoff $\kappa $ defining $\rho $ is chosen even, one of them, constructed explicitly, is invariant under the block exchange $\iota [A:B]=[B:A]$.

Proof. Consider pairs $-1<I<0<J<1$ satisfying

11.48
\begin{equation} \int _ I^ J z_0(r)\, \mathrm{d}r=p(I)+p(J). \end{equation}

We first show that such pairs exist, with $I$ ranging over an interval. Define

\[ K(I,J)=\int _ I^ Jz_0(r)\, \mathrm{d}r-p(I)-p(J). \]

We have $K(0,0)=-2p(0)<0$ and $K(0,1)>0$ by (11.47). By continuity, there is $\epsilon >0$ such that $K(I,0)<0<K(I,1)$ for every $I\in [-\epsilon ,0]$. For $0<J<1$,

\[ \partial _ JK(I,J)=z_0(J)-p'(J)>0. \]

The intermediate value theorem therefore gives a unique $J=J(I)\in (0,1)$ satisfying (11.48) for every $I\in [-\epsilon ,0)$; we may assume $\epsilon <1$.

For any pair satisfying (11.48), define the slope on $[-1,1]$ by

\[ h_{I,J}(r)= \begin{cases} p(r),& -1\leq r\leq I,\\ p(I)-\displaystyle \int _ I^ r z_0(s)\, \mathrm{d}s,& I\leq r\leq J,\\ -p(r),& J\leq r\leq 1, \end{cases} \]

and extend it by zero outside that interval. The balance identity makes it continuous at $J$, and continuity at $I$ is immediate. Each piece has bounded derivative, so $h_{I,J}$ is Lipschitz on $\mathbb R$; it vanishes to infinite order at $\pm 1$. Let $G_{I,J}$ be a primitive of $h_{I,J}$. It is $C^{1,1}$ and constant near each end of the real line. Consequently $G_{I,J}(r)$ extends to a $C^{1,1}$ function on $X$ that is smooth near $X\setminus U$.

On the middle interval $[I,J]$ the slope lies between $-p$ and $p$. Indeed,

\[ (h_{I,J}-p)'=-z_0-p'\leq 0, \qquad (h_{I,J}+p)'=-z_0+p'\leq 0. \]

The first difference vanishes at $I$, and the second vanishes at $J$. Since both are nonincreasing, $h_{I,J}\leq p$ and $h_{I,J}\geq -p$ on $[I,J]$.

Put $\widetilde u_{I,J}=G_{I,J}(r)-\rho $. By (11.41), almost everywhere on $U$,

11.52
\begin{equation} \omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\widetilde u_{I,J} =\omega _0+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}G_{I,J}(r) =(x+h_{I,J})P+(y-h_{I,J})Q+(z+h_{I,J}')R. \end{equation}

On $I<r<J$, the first two coefficients are nonnegative because $x=p+a$, $y=p+b$ and $-p\leq h_{I,J}\leq p$, and the third is zero. On $-1<r<I$, we have $h_{I,J}=p$ and $b=0$, since $I<0$; so the second coefficient is zero, and the others are nonnegative because $z_0+p'\geq 0$. On $J<r<1$, we have $h_{I,J}=-p$ and $a=0$; so the first coefficient is zero, and the others are nonnegative because $z_0-p'\geq 0$. Outside the band, $h_{I,J}=h_{I,J}'=0$, so the form equals $\omega _0$.

Formula (11.52) has no additional term supported on the hypersurfaces $r=I$ and $r=J$: the function $G_{I,J}$ is $C^{1,1}$, so its first derivative is continuous at both transition points, and its distributional Hessian is its bounded almost-everywhere Hessian. The preceding coefficient inequalities therefore prove $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\widetilde u_{I,J}\geq 0$ as a current.

By proposition 1.7.4, its Monge–Ampère measure is the pointwise cube of (11.52), computed almost everywhere. By the rank-one identities (11.39) and (11.40), this cube is zero almost everywhere on the band, since one coefficient vanishes there, and it equals $\omega _0^3$ off the band. These are exactly the values of $f\omega _ X^3$ in the preceding construction. Thus $\widetilde u_{I,J}$ solves the equation, and subtracting its supremum gives a normalized solution $u_{I,J}$.

Different values of $I\in [-\epsilon ,0)$ give different normalized solutions. If $I_1<I_2<0$ and $I_1<r<I_2$, then the slope belonging to $I_2$ is $p(r)$, whereas

\[ p(r)-h_{I_1,J(I_1)}(r) =\int _{I_1}^ r(p'(s)+z_0(s))\, \mathrm{d}s>0, \]

because $p'>0$ on $(-1,0)$. The two primitives therefore do not differ by a constant. Since the interval $[-\epsilon ,0)$ is uncountable, this proves the first assertion. None of these slopes equals $p$ or $-p$ on all of $(-1,1)$, so the constructed solutions are also distinct from the two smooth solutions of theorem 11.6.1.

Finally, the function

\[ L(t)=\int _0^ t z_0(r)\, \mathrm{d}r-p(t),\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1, \]

is negative at zero, positive at one, and strictly increasing on $(0,1)$. Its unique zero $t_*$ gives a symmetric pair $I=-t_*$, $J=t_*$ satisfying (11.48). Since $z_0$ and $p$ are even, $h_{I,J}$ is odd: on the middle interval it equals $-\int _0^ r z_0(s)\, \mathrm{d}s$. Choose its primitive even. Since $\iota $ changes $r$ into $-r$, if $\rho $ is even, the potential $G_{I,J}(r)-\rho $ and its normalization are fixed by $\iota $. This proves the last assertion. $\square$

For these symmetric data, the normalized solutions of the equations with a positive exponent converge, as the exponent tends to zero, to the symmetric solution just constructed; see theorem 14.19.3.

11.7 Nonuniqueness with a zero set of empty interior

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

We now keep the Hermitian background of theorem 11.6.1, but change the density so that it is positive off $M_-\cup M_+$, where $M_\pm =\{ r=\pm 1\} $. Theorem 11.7.1. There are a smooth nonclosed Hermitian form $\omega $ on $\mathbb P^3$ and a smooth nonnegative density $g$, positive off two disjoint smooth real hypersurfaces, such that the equation

Lemma 11.7.2. There exists $\eta _0>0$ such that for every $0<\eta <\eta _0$ the initial-value problem

Near $-1$ the coefficients are exactly

\[ x=1+p,\qquad y=p,\qquad z=q>0. \]

\[ x=p,\qquad y=1+p,\qquad z=-q>0, \]

11.8 Quadratic density zeros

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

Infinite-order vanishing of the density is not needed for smooth nonuniqueness. We change the coefficients of $\omega _0$ so that its coefficient of $R$ stays positive and one of its coefficients of $P$ and $Q$ vanishes quadratically at each of the two hypersurfaces $M_\pm $. The resulting singular differential equation forces the slopes of differences of solutions to decay like $\exp (-A/d)$, where $d$ is the distance to the hypersurface and $A>0$. The slopes therefore extend smoothly by zero, even though the density vanishes only to finite order.

Theorem 11.8.1. On $\mathbb P^3$ there exist a smooth nonclosed Hermitian form $\omega $ and a smooth nonnegative density $g$ such that:

  1. The zero set of $g$ consists of two disjoint smooth real hypersurfaces. Near either hypersurface, $g$ is the square of a smooth defining function times a smooth strictly positive function.

  2. The equation $(\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u)^3=g\omega _ X^3$ has uncountably many distinct smooth $\omega $-plurisubharmonic solutions normalized by $\sup _ Xu=0$.

The background $\omega $ has bounded mass.

We keep the rank-one forms $P,Q,R$ and the function $r$ of 11.6.1, and use new scalar coefficients.

Choose a smooth function $a$ valued in $[0,1]$, equal to one for $r\leq -2/3$ and zero for $r\geq -1/3$, and put $b(r)=a(-r)$. Choose a smooth even cutoff $\chi $ valued in $[0,1]$, zero for $|r|\leq 5/4$ and one for $|r|\geq 3/2$. Set $p(r)=(1-r^2)^2$ and

Since these coefficients coincide with those of (11.42) for $|r|\geq 3/2$, the form $\omega _0$ extends by $\omega _ X$ across the two omitted projective lines. The coefficient $z$ is strictly positive, and $x,y$ are strictly positive except that $y(-1)=0$ and $x(1)=0$. Thus $\omega _0$ is positive off $M_\pm =\{ r=\pm 1\} $ and has rank two there.

Choose a smooth cutoff $\kappa \in C_ c^\infty ((-2,2))$ equal to one on $[-3/2,3/2]$. For a parameter $\varepsilon >0$ to be chosen, define the correction

\[ \rho =\varepsilon (r^2-1)\kappa (r),\qquad \omega =\omega _0+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho . \]

For sufficiently small $\varepsilon >0$, the form $\omega $ is strictly positive. Indeed, on $[-3/2,3/2]$ its coefficients are $x+2\varepsilon r$, $y-2\varepsilon r$ and $z+2\varepsilon $. The first is positive for $r\geq 0$, and on $[-3/2,0]$ the function $x$ has a positive minimum, so it remains positive for small $\varepsilon $. Reflection gives the positivity of the second coefficient, and that of the third is immediate. On $3/2\leq |r|\leq 2$, the form $\omega _0$ is strictly positive on a compact set and the correction is $O(\varepsilon )$. Near the projective ends there is no correction. \[ \mathrm{d}\omega =\mathrm{d}r\wedge \bigl ((x'-z)P+(y'+z)Q\bigr )\ne 0. \]

\[ g\omega _ X^3=\omega _0^3. \]

It is smooth and positive exactly off $M_-\cup M_+$. By (11.42) and (11.40), $g=xyz/(\sigma (r)(1-\sigma (r))\sigma '(r))$. Near either hypersurface, $xyz=(1+p)p$, and hence

\[ g(r)=\frac{(1+p(r))(1-r^2)^2}{\sigma (r)(1-\sigma (r))\sigma '(r)}. \]

The denominator is smooth and strictly positive at $\pm 1$, and $1-r^2$ is a defining function for $M_\pm $ near these hypersurfaces. This proves the asserted quadratic vanishing. The potential $-\rho $ is one smooth solution.

11.62
\begin{equation} h'=\frac{xyz}{(x+h)(y-h)}-z,\qquad h(0)=-\eta \end{equation}

has a smooth solution on $(-1,1)$ with $-x<h<y$ and $h<0$. There are a constant $0<c<1$ and fixed neighborhoods of both endpoints, independent of $\eta $, on which

\[ -cp<h<0. \]

Proof. The right-hand side $F(r,h)$ is smooth on $-x<h<y$, and $h=0$ is a solution. Near $-1$, $(x,y,z)=(1+p,p,1)$; near $1$, $(x,y,z)=(p,1+p,1)$. Fix $0<c<1$. For the trial function $-cp$, near the left endpoint

\[ F(r,-cp)+cp' =\frac{1+p}{(1+(1-c)p)(1+c)}-1+cp' \longrightarrow -\frac{c}{1+c}<0, \]

whereas near the right endpoint

\[ F(r,-cp)+cp' =\frac{1+p}{(1-c)(1+(1+c)p)}-1+cp' \longrightarrow \frac{c}{1-c}>0. \]

as $r\to \pm 1$, since $p,p'\to 0$ at both endpoints. The strict inequalities therefore hold on fixed neighborhoods of the endpoints, and $-cp$ is a lower barrier for the equation solved backwards near $-1$ and forwards near $1$.

Choose a compact interval $[r_ L,r_ R]\ni 0$ with endpoints in those neighborhoods. Continuous dependence on initial values, around the solution $h=0$, gives $\eta _0>0$ such that for every $0<\eta <\eta _0$ the solution exists on $[r_ L,r_ R]$, stays in the domain of $F$, and lies above $-cp$ at $r_ L$ and $r_ R$. By uniqueness of solutions, it cannot meet the solution zero, so $h<0$. The barrier argument used in the proof of lemma 11.7.2 then gives $-cp<h<0$ near both ends. On each compact subinterval of $(-1,1)$ the trapped graph stays at a positive distance from the boundary of the domain of $F$; hence, by the continuation theorem, the solution exists on all of $(-1,1)$.

The equation factors as

11.63
\begin{equation} h'=h\, L(r,h),\qquad L(r,h)=\frac{z(x-y+h)}{(x+h)(y-h)}. \end{equation}

Since $h<0$, we have $(\log (-h))'=L(r,h)$. Near the left endpoint, the trapping bounds and the explicit coefficients imply

\[ L(r,h)=\frac{1+h}{(1+p+h)(p-h)}\geq \frac{C_1}{p} \]

after shrinking that neighborhood, with $C_1>0$. Near the right endpoint they give

\[ L(r,h)=\frac{-1+h}{(p+h)(1+p-h)}\leq -\frac{C_2}{p}, \qquad C_2>0. \]

Indeed, on the left, $1+h\geq 1-cp\geq 1/2$ and the denominator is at most $(1+p)(1+c)p$; on the right, the numerator is at most $-1$, and the denominator is at most $p(1+(1+c)p)$. 11.64
\begin{equation} |h(r)|\leq C \mathrm{e}^{-A/d} \end{equation}

near either endpoint. The constants may depend on the solution; uniformity in $\eta $ is not needed for the smooth extension.

It remains to control all derivatives. On the trapped graph, all partial derivatives in $(r,h)$ of the rational function $L$ grow at most polynomially in $d^{-1}$. Indeed, near an endpoint $x,y$ are polynomials in $r$, $z=1$, $h$ is bounded, and of the two factors in the denominator, one is bounded below by a positive constant and the other by $(1-c)p$. Repeated differentiation of the quotient therefore only introduces finite powers of $p^{-1}$.

At each of the hypersurfaces $M_\pm $, the difference of two of these solutions agrees to infinite order with a constant, because its derivative is a difference of slopes that vanish to infinite order there. The constants at the two hypersurfaces need not agree. Thus the singular differential equation produces differences that are flat at $M_\pm $, even though the density has only quadratic zeros.

Put $d_*(r)=f_*(\sigma (r))(\sigma '(r))^2$. Since $a=\sigma (-1)$, $b=\sigma (1)$, define the positive smooth divided differences

and $H_*(r)=k_-(r)k_+(r)\sigma '(r)>0$. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives

11.67
\begin{equation} \sigma (r)-a=(r+1)k_-(r),\quad \sigma (r)-b=(r-1)k_+(r),\quad d_*=(1-r^2)^2H_*^2. \end{equation}

These formulas show that $H_*$ is smooth and positive also at the endpoints, without any division by a vanishing function. The symmetry $\sigma (-r)=1-\sigma (r)$ makes $d_*$, and hence $H_*$, even.

Choose the function

\[ p(r)= \begin{cases} \mathrm{e}^{-1/(1-r^2)},& |r|<1,\\ 0,& |r|\geq 1, \end{cases} \qquad q=p'. \]

\begin{align} z_ b& =\sqrt{q^2+2H_*^2},\qquad S_* =\sqrt{r^2+4p^2+2z_ b(1-r^2)^2},\\ x_ b& =(S_*-r)/2,\qquad y_ b=(S_*+r)/2. \end{align}

11.72

\[ S_*^2-(2p+|r|)^2=2(1-r^2)^2(z_ b-|q|)>0, \]

are strictly positive there.

11.9.2 Extension over the projective ends

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

The coefficients just constructed have the right product but need not extend across the projective ends. Away from the zero levels of $f_*(t)$, we interpolate the coefficients of $P$ and $Q$ to those of $f_*(t)^{1/3}\omega _ X$, and determine the coefficient of $R$ by the prescribed product.

Choose a smooth even cutoff $0\leq \eta \leq 1$, equal to one on $|r|\leq 3/2$ and zero on $|r|\geq 2$. On $|r|<3/2$ set $(x,y,z)=(x_ b,y_ b,z_ b)$. On $|r|>5/4$, where $f_*(\sigma (r))>0$, put

\begin{align*} \kappa & =f_*(\sigma (r))^{1/3},\\ x& =\eta x_ b+(1-\eta )\kappa \sigma (r),\\ y& =\eta y_ b+(1-\eta )\kappa \sigma (-r),\qquad z=d_*/(xy). \end{align*}

These coefficients are smooth and positive on the outer region. On the overlap $5/4<|r|<3/2$, we have $\eta =1$, $p=q=0$ and $x_ by_ bz_ b=d_*$, so the two definitions agree. For $|r|\geq 2$ they equal $\kappa (\sigma (r),\sigma (-r),\sigma '(r))$. Thus

\[ \omega _0=xP+yQ+zR \]

extends smoothly across both omitted projective lines, being equal near them to $f_*(t)^{1/3}\omega _ X$, which is smooth and positive there since $f_*(0)=f_*(1)=a^2b^2>0$. On $X$, the form $\omega _0$ is semipositive, positive away from $r=\pm 1$, and of rank two on these levels.

The function

\[ F(r)=2\int _0^ r p(s)\, \mathrm{d}s \]

is constant on each of the intervals $(-\infty ,-1]$ and $[1,\infty )$, and so $F(r)$ extends to a smooth nonconstant function on $X$. By (11.41), half of its complex Hessian is $H_0=\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}F/2=p(P-Q)+qR$. By (11.74) and (11.66), the forms $A_0=\omega _0-H_0$, $B_0=\omega _0+H_0$ satisfy

\[ A_0^3=B_0^3=f_*(t)\omega _ X^3 \]

11.9.3 A positive equation background

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

As in the first example, an exact correction makes the average form strictly positive without changing the two solution forms. Here the coefficient of $R$ is already positive, and the correction restores the missing coefficient of $P$ or $Q$ at each endpoint. Let $\varepsilon >0$ be a parameter, restricted below.

Let $h(r)=(r^2-1)\eta (r)$, extend it by zero on the tails, and set $\rho =\varepsilon h$. The coefficients of $\omega =\omega _0+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho $ are

11.75
\begin{equation} (x+\varepsilon h',\ y-\varepsilon h',\ z+\varepsilon h''). \end{equation}

On $|r|\leq 3/2$, we have $h'=2r$ and $h''=2$. For $0\leq r\leq 3/2$, $x+2\varepsilon r>0$, since the only zero of $x$ there is at $r=1$, where the correction is positive. On $[-3/2,0]$, $x$ has a positive minimum $m_0$, so positivity holds if $3\varepsilon <m_0$. Reflection proves positivity of the second coefficient, and the third is $z+2\varepsilon >0$. On the compact transition region $3/2\leq |r|\leq 2$, all original coefficients have a common positive lower bound $m_1$. If $M_1$ bounds $|h'|+|h''|$, taking also $\varepsilon <m_1/(M_1+1)$ preserves their positivity there. For $|r|\geq 2$, the background equals $f_*(t)^{1/3}\omega _ X>0$. Hence $\omega $ is strictly positive on $X$.

The correction is exact, so $\mathrm{d}\omega =\mathrm{d}\omega _0$. At $r=0$, where $x=x_ b$ and $y=y_ b$, evenness of $S_*$ gives $x'(0)=-1/2$ and $y'(0)=1/2$. Hence (11.46) gives

\[ \mathrm{d}\omega _0=(z_ b(0)+1/2)\, \mathrm{d}r\wedge (Q-P)\ne 0. \]

Indeed, if $\mathrm{d}r\wedge (Q-P)=0$, wedging with $P$ and $\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}r$ would contradict $P\wedge Q\wedge R>0$. Thus the Hermitian background $\omega $ is not closed.

Now put $\widetilde u=-F/2-\rho $ and $\widetilde v=F/2-\rho $. Then $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\widetilde u=A_0$, $\omega +\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\widetilde v=B_0$. Subtracting their respective suprema gives $u,v$ with $\sup _ Xu=\sup _ Xv=0$, and (11.65) holds. Their difference is nonconstant because $F$ is nonconstant.

11.9.4 Zeros and higher dimensions

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory

It remains to examine the regularity of the density and the geometry of its zero levels. Taking a product with projective space then preserves the equality of the Monge–Ampère measures and gives examples in every higher dimension.

The function $t$ is real analytic in every affine chart of $X$, being a quotient of real polynomials with positive denominator. Hence $f_*(t)$ is real analytic on $X$. Its two zero levels are regular hypersurfaces because $t=\sigma (r)$, $\sigma '>0$, and $\mathrm{d}r\ne 0$. Each level is connected, since it is the quotient of the product of two three-spheres of fixed nonzero radii by the diagonal action of the circle. Each level is compact and has zero volume. The divided differences in (11.67) show near the two levels that

11.76
\begin{equation} f_*(\sigma (r))=(r+1)^2g_-(r),\qquad f_*(\sigma (r))=(r-1)^2g_+(r), \end{equation}

respectively, with smooth strictly positive functions $g_\pm $.

For $n\geq 4$, let $\theta $ be the Fubini–Study form on $Y=\mathbb {P}^{n-3}$, let $\pi _1:X\times Y\to X$ and $\pi _2:X\times Y\to Y$ be the projections, and set, on $X\times Y$,

\[ \omega _ n=\pi _1^*\omega +\pi _2^*\theta ,\qquad \Omega _ n=\pi _1^*\omega _ X+\pi _2^*\theta ,\qquad u_ n=\pi _1^*u,\quad v_ n=\pi _1^*v. \]

In the expansion of an $n$-fold product, only the terms with exactly three factors pulled back from $X$ survive. Consequently

\[ (\omega _ n+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}u_ n)^ n =\binom n3\pi _1^*(A_0^3)\wedge \pi _2^*(\theta ^{n-3}) =f_*(\pi _1^*t)\Omega _ n^ n, \]

and the same holds for $v_ n$. On the two product levels, the solution forms have rank $2+(n-3)=n-1$. Restriction to a slice $X\times \{ y\} $ recovers $\mathrm{d}\omega \ne 0$, so $\omega _ n$ is not closed. Normalization, distinctness and real analyticity are preserved by pullback.

Write $\operatorname {Vol}$ for the volume with respect to $\omega _ X^3$, or $\Omega _ n^ n$ in dimension $n$. The quadratic zeros complete the proof of the sharpness assertion in 11.1.4. In finitely many tubular charts around the zero levels, the density is bounded above and below by positive multiples of the square of a real transverse coordinate, and the volume form has a bounded positive density with respect to coordinate volume. Away from these charts, the density is bounded below by a positive constant. Thus, for $s>0$ and $\alpha >0$,

11.77
\begin{equation} \operatorname {Vol}\{ f_*\leq s\} \asymp \sqrt s\quad (s\downarrow 0), \qquad f_*^{-\alpha }\in L^1\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \alpha <\tfrac 12. \end{equation}

Thus, for every $0<\alpha <1/2$ and every dimension $n\geq 3$, there are smooth examples of nonuniqueness whose density $f$ satisfies $f^{-\alpha }\in L^1$; this condition is weaker than the hypothesis $f^{-1/2}\in L^1$ of 11.1.4.

Remark 11.9.2.For this example, the positive-density set $\{ f_*(t)>0\} $ has three components: the middle band and the two outer regions. The two solution forms have the same missing direction on each zero level, so $E_{\rm unif}=\{ f_*(t)>0\} $. The maximum and the minimum of the difference of the two potentials are attained on the two outer components, whose closures are disjoint, in accordance with 11.1.1. Although the closure of the middle component meets both outer closures, the extremal values do not propagate through the middle component.
