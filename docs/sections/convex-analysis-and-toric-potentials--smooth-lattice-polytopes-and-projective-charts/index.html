21.8 Smooth lattice polytopes and projective chartsF916BA03200F

F916BA03200F

So far a convex body $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ has served as a bound for the slopes of convex functions. When $P$ is a lattice polytope, it also determines a compactification of the complex torus with character group $M$. If $P$ is smooth, the primitive edge directions at each vertex form a basis of $M$, and the corresponding characters become holomorphic coordinates on a chart isomorphic to $\mathbb C^ n$, which reaches across the boundary of the torus.

Definition 21.8.1.E11C7A4F96AC A lattice polytope in $M_{\mathbb R}$ is the convex hull of finitely many points of $M$. A face of a lattice polytope $P$ is a set of the form $\{ m\in P:\langle m,x\rangle =\min _{m'\in P}\langle m',x\rangle \} $ with $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$; faces are nonempty, and $P$ itself is the face for $x=0$. Vertices, edges and facets are the faces of dimension zero, of dimension one and of codimension one, respectively. A nonzero vector of a lattice is primitive if it is not of the form $ky$ with $y$ in the lattice and an integer $k\geq 2$. Let $P$ have nonempty interior. Its vertices are lattice points, each edge is a segment $[v,v']$ between two vertices, and a vertex contained in an edge is one of its endpoints (lemma 21.8.2). Thus an edge $[v,v']$ containing a vertex $v$ determines the primitive edge direction at $v$, the unique primitive vector of $M$ that is a positive multiple of $v'-v$ (see below). The polytope $P$ is smooth, or Delzant, if at each vertex its primitive edge directions form a basis of $M$. In dimension zero the single point is smooth, with the empty basis.

Every nonzero vector $y$ of a lattice is a positive multiple of exactly one primitive vector. Indeed, if $d$ is the greatest common divisor of the coordinates of $y$ in a basis of the lattice, then $y/d$ is primitive, since its coordinates are coprime. If $y'$ and $y''$ are primitive and $y'=cy''$ with $c>0$, then $c$ is a quotient of integer coordinates, say $c=a/b$ with coprime positive integers $a$ and $b$, and $by'=ay''$ shows that $b$ divides all coordinates of $y''$ and $a$ all those of $y'$; hence $a=b=1$.

The cone $\operatorname {cone}(S)$ generated by a set $S$ consists of its finite nonnegative linear combinations. A ray is a half-line $\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$ with $y\neq 0$, and a ray contained in a cone $C$ is extreme if, whenever a vector of the ray is the sum of two vectors of $C$, both summands lie on the ray.

An affine function $\ell $ that is nonnegative on a convex set $C$ and vanishes at a point $b\in \operatorname {ri}C$ vanishes on $C$. Indeed, for $y\in C$ the point $b-\varepsilon (y-b)$ lies in $C$ for small $\varepsilon >0$, and $0=\ell (b)$ is a convex combination, with positive weights, of the nonnegative numbers $\ell (y)$ and $\ell (b-\varepsilon (y-b))$; hence $\ell (y)=0$.

Lemma 21.8.2.41C54729DBFD Let $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ be a lattice polytope; in (2)–(4) assume moreover that $P$ has nonempty interior.

  1. The vertices of $P$ are lattice points, $P$ is their convex hull, every face of $P$ is the convex hull of the vertices it contains, and $P$ has finitely many faces. Every edge of $P$ is a segment $[v,v']$ between two distinct vertices, and a vertex contained in an edge is one of its endpoints.

  2. $P$ has an irredundant facet presentation

    \[ P=\{ m:\ell _\rho (m)=\langle m,u_\rho \rangle +a_\rho \geq 0 \text{ for every facet }\rho \} , \]

    where, for each facet $\rho $, the normal $u_\rho \in N$ is primitive and inward pointing, $a_\rho \in \mathbb Z$, and $\rho =\{ m\in P:\ell _\rho (m)=0\} $.

  3. At a vertex $v$, the tangent cone

    \[ C_ v=\{ y:\langle y,u_\rho \rangle \geq 0 \text{ for the facets }\rho \ni v\} =\operatorname {cone}(P-v) \]

    is generated by its extreme rays, and these are the rays spanned by the edge directions at $v$.

  4. If $P$ is smooth and $v$ is a vertex with primitive edge basis $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$, then exactly $n$ facets contain $v$, and their normals $u_1,\ldots ,u_ n$ form the dual basis of $N$, that is, $\langle e_ i,u_ j\rangle =\delta _{ij}$. Thus

    \[ m-v=\sum _ i t_ i e_ i,\qquad t_ i=\ell _ i(m)\geq 0\quad (m\in P), \]

    and $v+e_ i\in P\cap M$ for each $i$.

Proof. (1) Let $P$ be the convex hull of a finite set $S\subset M$, and discard points of $S$ one at a time as long as some point lies in the convex hull of the others; this does not change $P$. If only one point remains, $P$ is that point, it is the only face, and all assertions hold. Otherwise, for a remaining point $s$, lemma 21.2.3(1) gives $x\in N_{\mathbb R}$ with $\langle s,x\rangle <\langle s',x\rangle $ for all $s'$ in the convex hull of $S\setminus \{ s\} $. Every point of $P$ is a convex combination $\lambda s+(1-\lambda )s''$ with $s''$ in that hull, so $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ attains its minimum on $P$ only at $s$, and $\{ s\} $ is a vertex. Conversely, let $Q$ be any face, the set where $\langle \cdot ,x'\rangle $ is minimal for some $x'\in N_{\mathbb R}$, and write a point $m\in Q$ as a convex combination of points of $S$: all points with positive coefficient minimize $\langle \cdot ,x'\rangle $, hence lie in $Q$. Thus $Q$ is the convex hull of the points of $S$ it contains. Applied to a vertex $\{ v\} $, this shows $v\in S$. Hence the vertices are exactly the remaining points of $S$, and in particular lattice points. Every face is the convex hull of the vertices it contains, so there are only finitely many faces.

Let $\{ v\} $ be a vertex, the set where some $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ is minimal on $P$. Then $v$ is not of the form $(1-t)a+tb$ with $a\neq b$ in $P$ and $0<t<1$: the values $\langle a,x\rangle $ and $\langle b,x\rangle $ are at least $\langle v,x\rangle $, and their weighted mean equals it, so $a$ and $b$ would also be minimum points, that is, $a=b=v$. An edge is the convex hull of the finitely many vertices it contains, which lie on a line, so it is the segment between the two outermost ones; a vertex in the edge is not an interior point of the segment, hence it is an endpoint.

(2) We first show that every boundary point $b$ of $P$ lies in a facet. By lemma 21.2.3(2), $b$ lies in a face $Q$ where some $x\neq 0$ is minimal; this face is proper, since $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ is not constant on the full-dimensional set $P$. Suppose that $Q$ has dimension $d<n-1$, and let $c$ be the minimum of $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ on $P$. The linear functionals vanishing on the directions of $Q$ form a space of dimension $n-d\geq 2$, which contains $x$; choose $y$ in it, independent of $x$, and put $x_ s=x+sy$. On $Q$ the functional $\langle \cdot ,y\rangle $ is constant, say equal to $c'$, and for each vertex $w\notin Q$ consider the gap $g_ w(s)=\langle w,x_ s\rangle -(c+sc')=(\langle w,x\rangle -c)+s(\langle w,y\rangle -c')$, which is positive at $s=0$. Some vertex $w\notin Q$ has $\langle w,y\rangle \neq c'$, since otherwise $y$ would be constant on $P$ and hence zero; replacing $y$ by $-y$ if necessary, we may assume that $\langle w,y\rangle <c'$ for some such $w$. Let $s_*>0$ be the first value of $s>0$ at which some gap vanishes. Then $x_{s_*}\neq 0$ by independence, all vertices of $Q$ and at least one vertex $w\notin Q$ minimize $\langle \cdot ,x_{s_*}\rangle $ on the vertices, and hence, by (1), on $P$. The new face contains $Q$ and $w$, and $w$ does not lie in the affine span of $Q$, because $\langle \cdot ,x\rangle $ equals $c$ on that span and is larger at $w$. So the dimension of the face has increased, and after finitely many steps we reach a facet containing $b$.

A facet $\rho $ contains $n$ affinely independent vertices, which are lattice points by (1). The linear functionals vanishing on the differences of these points form a line, which is defined by linear equations with integral coefficients in the coordinates of bases of $M$ and $N$; Gaussian elimination over $\mathbb Q$ produces a nonzero rational solution, and clearing denominators gives a nonzero vector of $N$ on the line. Since $\rho $ is a face, some $x\neq 0$ attains its minimum on $P$ exactly on $\rho $; this $x$ lies on the line, and we let $u_\rho $ be the primitive vector of $N$ that is a positive multiple of $x$, which exists and is unique by the observation following definition 21.8.1. Then $a_\rho =-\langle v,u_\rho \rangle $, for any vertex $v\in \rho $, is an integer, $\ell _\rho \geq 0$ on $P$, and $\rho =\{ m\in P:\ell _\rho (m)=0\} $.

Next we show that $P$ is the intersection of the half-spaces $\{ \ell _\rho \geq 0\} $. Let $q\notin P$ and let $p$ be an interior point of $P$. The set of $s\in [0,1]$ with $p+s(q-p)\in P$ is an interval $[0,s_0]$ with $s_0<1$, and $b=p+s_0(q-p)$ is a boundary point of $P$, hence lies in some facet $\rho $. The affine function $s\mapsto \ell _\rho (p+s(q-p))$ is positive at $s=0$, because the nonconstant affine function $\ell _\rho $, which is nonnegative on $P$, cannot attain its minimum at the interior point $p$. It vanishes at $s_0<1$, so $\ell _\rho (q)<0$.

For irredundancy, let $b$ be a point of the relative interior of a facet $\rho $, which exists by lemma 21.2.3(4). If $\ell _{\rho '}(b)=0$ for another facet $\rho '$, then $\ell _{\rho '}$, which is nonnegative on $\rho $, vanishes on $\rho $ by the observation preceding the lemma. Then $u_{\rho '}$ is constant on $\rho $, whose differences span a hyperplane, so $u_{\rho '}$ lies on the line of $u_\rho $, and $u_{\rho '}=\pm u_\rho $, because both vectors are primitive. If $u_{\rho '}=-u_\rho $, then $\ell _{\rho '}=-\ell _\rho $, since both vanish on $\rho $; as both are nonnegative on $P$, $\ell _\rho $ would vanish on $P$, which is impossible at an interior point. Hence $\ell _{\rho '}=\ell _\rho $ and $\rho '=\rho $. Thus all other facet functions are positive at $b$, and a point near $b$ on the side where $\ell _\rho <0$ violates only the inequality of $\rho $.

(3) If $m\in P$ and $v\in \rho $, then $\langle m-v,u_\rho \rangle =\ell _\rho (m)\geq 0$; hence $\operatorname {cone}(P-v)\subset C_ v$. Conversely, if $y\in C_ v$, then $v+sy\in P$ for all small $s>0$, because the inequalities of the facets containing $v$ hold along this half-line and the finitely many other facet functions are positive at $v$; hence $y\in \operatorname {cone}(P-v)$. The cone $C_ v$ contains no line: if $y\neq 0$ and $\pm y\in C_ v$, then $v\pm sy\in P$ for small $s>0$, and $v$ would be the midpoint of two points of $P$, which is impossible for a vertex. Let $u$ be the sum of the normals $u_\rho $ of the facets containing $v$. If $y\in C_ v$ and $\langle y,u\rangle =0$, then $\langle y,u_\rho \rangle =0$ for all these facets, so $-y\in C_ v$ and $y=0$. Consequently, the slice $\Sigma =\{ y\in C_ v:\langle y,u\rangle =1\} $ is bounded (otherwise a limit of normalized unbounded points would give a nonzero $y\in C_ v$ with $\langle y,u\rangle =0$), and $C_ v=\operatorname {cone}(\Sigma )$.

Call a point $y\in \Sigma $ basic if the normals $u_\rho $ of the facets $\rho \ni v$ with $\langle y,u_\rho \rangle =0$ span a space of dimension $n-1$. Every point $y\in \Sigma $ is a convex combination of basic points. Indeed, if the active normals of $y$, together with $u$, span a space of dimension less than $n$, choose $d\neq 0$ annihilated by all of them. For small $|s|$ the points $y+sd$ remain in $\Sigma $, and since $\Sigma $ is bounded there are maximal $s_\pm >0$ with $y\pm s_\pm d\in \Sigma $. At $y\pm s_\pm d$ some new facet function $\langle \cdot ,u_{\rho '}\rangle $ vanishes, and $\langle d,u_{\rho '}\rangle \neq 0$, so $u_{\rho '}$ is not in the span of the previous active normals and $u$. Thus $y$ is a convex combination of two points with a larger span of active normals and $u$. Induction on the codimension of this span therefore proves the claim, because a point $y\in \Sigma $ whose active normals together with $u$ span $N_{\mathbb R}$ is basic: its active normals annihilate $y\neq 0$, so they span a space of dimension at most $n-1$, and at least $n-1$, since adding $u$ raises the dimension by at most one.

Next, $y\in \Sigma $ is basic if and only if $\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$ is an extreme ray. If $y$ is basic, its active normals annihilate exactly the line $\mathbb Ry$; if $y=y_1+y_2$ with $y_1,y_2\in C_ v$, then for each active normal both pairings $\langle y_ k,u_\rho \rangle $ are nonnegative with sum zero, hence zero, so $y_1,y_2\in \mathbb Ry\cap C_ v=\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$. Conversely, if $\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$ is extreme, write $y$ as a convex combination of basic points; extremality forces each of them to lie on the ray, hence to equal $y$. Therefore $C_ v$ is generated by its extreme rays.

It remains to identify the extreme rays with the edges at $v$. If $y\in \Sigma $ is basic, the set $\{ m\in P:\ell _\rho (m)=0\text{ for the active facets of }y\} $ is a face, namely the set where the sum of the corresponding functionals $\langle \cdot ,u_\rho \rangle $ is minimal; it contains $v+sy$ for small $s>0$ and is contained in $v+(C_ v\cap (\mathbb Ry))=v+\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$. Hence it is an edge at $v$ with direction $y$. Conversely, let $E$ be an edge at $v$ with direction $y$, so $y\in C_ v$. If $y=y_1+y_2$ with $y_1,y_2\in C_ v$, then for small $s>0$ the point $v+sy\in E$ is the midpoint of $v+2sy_1$ and $v+2sy_2$, which lie in $P$. Since $E$ is the set where some linear functional is minimal on $P$, both points lie in $E\subset v+\mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$, and so $y_1,y_2\in \mathbb R_{\geq 0}y$. Thus the ray of $y$ is extreme.

(4) Let $P$ be smooth and $v$ a vertex. By (3), $C_ v=\operatorname {cone}(e_1,\ldots ,e_ n)$. Let $e_1^*,\ldots ,e_ n^*\in N$ be the dual basis, so that $C_ v=\{ y:\langle y,e_ i^*\rangle \geq 0\text{ for all }i\} $. If $\rho $ is a facet containing $v$, then $u_\rho \geq 0$ on $C_ v$, so $u_\rho =\sum _ ic_ ie_ i^*$ with $c_ i=\langle e_ i,u_\rho \rangle \geq 0$. The set $\rho -v$ lies in $C_ v\cap u_\rho ^\perp =\operatorname {cone}\{ e_ i:c_ i=0\} $ and spans a space of dimension $n-1$, so exactly one coefficient $c_ i$ is positive. Since $u_\rho $ and $e_ i^*$ are primitive, $u_\rho =e_ i^*$. Distinct facets have distinct normals, because a facet is the set where its normal is minimal; so at most $n$ facets contain $v$. If some $e_ i^*$ were not the normal of a facet through $v$, the vector $-e_ i$ would satisfy all the inequalities defining $C_ v$, although $-e_ i\notin \operatorname {cone}(e_1,\ldots ,e_ n)$. Hence exactly $n$ facets contain $v$, and numbering them suitably, $u_ i=e_ i^*$. For $m\in P$ this gives $m-v=\sum _ i\langle m-v,u_ i\rangle e_ i=\sum _ i\ell _ i(m)e_ i$, because $\ell _ i(v)=0$. Finally, the edge in direction $e_ i$ is a segment $[v,v']$ with $v'$ a lattice point, so $v'-v=ke_ i$ for a positive integer $k$, and $v+e_ i\in [v,v']\subset P$. In dimension zero there are no facets and no edges, and the assertions are empty. $\square$

For the rest of the chapter we fix a smooth lattice polytope $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$, which by definition has nonempty interior, with the notation $\ell _\rho $, $u_\rho $ and $a_\rho $ of lemma 21.8.2, and we put $A=P\cap M$, a finite set containing the vertices. Let $T=\operatorname {Hom}(M,\mathbb C^*)$ be the group of homomorphisms from $M$ to the multiplicative group $\mathbb C^*$, and $T_ c=\operatorname {Hom}(M,S^1)$ its compact subgroup, where $S^1=\{ \zeta \in \mathbb C:|\zeta |=1\} $. Each $m\in M$ defines a character $\chi ^ m\colon T\to \mathbb C^*$, $\chi ^ m(t)=t(m)$, so that $\chi ^{m+m'}=\chi ^ m\chi ^{m'}$. A basis $m_1,\ldots ,m_ n$ of $M$ identifies $T$ with $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ through $t\mapsto (\chi ^{m_1}(t),\ldots ,\chi ^{m_ n}(t))$, since a homomorphism on $M$ is determined by its values on a basis, which may be arbitrary. The resulting structure of complex manifold on $T$ does not depend on the basis, because a change of basis acts on the coordinates by a monomial map whose inverse is again monomial. In these coordinates $\chi ^ m$ becomes the Laurent monomial $z_1^{c_1}\cdots z_ n^{c_ n}$ for $m=\sum _ jc_ jm_ j$, and $T_ c$ becomes $(S^1)^ n$, acting on $T=(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ by multiplication, that is, by separate rotations of the coordinates. The tropicalization map $\operatorname {Trop}\colon T\to N_{\mathbb R}$ is defined by

\[ \langle m,\operatorname {Trop}(t)\rangle =\log |\chi ^ m(t)|^2\qquad (m\in M); \]

in the coordinates above, with $N_{\mathbb R}$ identified with $\mathbb R^ n$ by the dual basis, it is $z\mapsto (\log |z_1|^2,\ldots ,\log |z_ n|^2)$. By corollary 21.2.9, the $T_ c$-invariant plurisubharmonic functions on $T$ are exactly the functions $F\circ \operatorname {Trop}$ with $F$ finite and convex on $N_{\mathbb R}$. (In dimension zero, $T$ and $N_{\mathbb R}$ are points, and by definition 5.8.1 both classes consist of the real constants.)

Let $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$ be the complex projective space of lines in $\mathbb C^ A$, with homogeneous coordinates $Z=[Z_ m]_{m\in A}$. By example 10.6.6, with homogeneous coordinates indexed by the finite set $A$, it is a compact complex manifold of dimension $|A|-1$, with the affine charts $\{ Z_{m_0}\neq 0\} $, $m_0\in A$, and the coordinates $Z_ m/Z_{m_0}$, $m\neq m_0$. It carries the hyperplane bundle $\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^{|A|-1}}(1)$, dual to the tautological line bundle, with the Fubini–Study metric, and each linear form $Z\mapsto Z_ m$ defines a holomorphic coordinate section of $\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^{|A|-1}}(1)$, which we denote again by $Z_ m$. Define

\[ \iota \colon T\longrightarrow \mathbb P^{|A|-1},\qquad t\longmapsto [\chi ^ m(t)]_{m\in A}, \qquad X_ P=\overline{\iota (T)}. \]

The torus $T$ acts on $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$ by $\tau \cdot [Z_ m]=[\chi ^ m(\tau )Z_ m]$. Since $\iota (\tau t)=\tau \cdot \iota (t)$, this action preserves $\iota (T)$ and hence its closure $X_ P$.

Theorem 21.8.3.E49F0370E203 The set $X_ P$ is an $n$-dimensional complex submanifold of $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$; it is the common zero set of finitely many homogeneous polynomials and is irreducible, so it is a smooth projective variety of dimension $n$. The map $\iota $ is a biholomorphism of $T$ onto a dense open subset of $X_ P$. For each vertex $v$ of $P$, with primitive edge basis $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$, the set $U_ v=X_ P\cap \{ Z_ v\neq 0\} $ is open in $X_ P$, and

\[ w=(w_1,\ldots ,w_ n),\qquad w_ i=Z_{v+e_ i}/Z_ v, \]

is a biholomorphism of $U_ v$ onto $\mathbb C^ n$ satisfying $w_ i\circ \iota =\chi ^{e_ i}$. These vertex charts cover $X_ P$. For $m\in A$ write $m-v=\sum _ i b_ i e_ i$; then the $b_ i$ are nonnegative integers and $Z_ m/Z_ v=\prod _ i w_ i^{b_ i}$ on $U_ v$. Finally, the restriction $L=\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^{|A|-1}}(1)|_{X_ P}$ of the hyperplane bundle is very ample, and the restriction of the Fubini–Study metric is a smooth quadratic metric on $L$ with positive definite curvature form.

Proof. Every point of $X_ P$ has a nonzero vertex coordinate. Let $m\in A$. By lemma 21.8.2(1), $m$ lies in the convex hull of the vertices of $P$, so by lemma 21.2.3(3) it is a convex combination of affinely independent vertices. The coefficients of this combination are the unique solution of a linear system with integral coefficients, hence rational. Clearing denominators, we obtain

\[ d m=\sum _ v c_ v v,\qquad c_ v\in \mathbb Z_{\geq 0},\quad \sum _ v c_ v=d\geq 1. \]

Since $\chi ^{dm}=\prod _ v(\chi ^ v)^{c_ v}$, the homogeneous equation $Z_ m^ d=\prod _ v Z_ v^{c_ v}$ of degree $d$ holds on $\iota (T)$, hence on its closure $X_ P$. If all vertex coordinates of a point of $X_ P$ vanished, these equations would force all its coordinates to vanish, which is impossible.

The vertex charts. Fix a vertex $v$. For $m\in A$, lemma 21.8.2(4) gives $m-v=\sum _ ib_ i(m)e_ i$ with $b_ i(m)=\ell _ i(m)=\langle m,u_ i\rangle +a_ i$, a nonnegative integer; we write $b(m)=(b_1(m),\ldots ,b_ n(m))$. In particular $b(v+e_ i)$ is the $i$th unit vector, and on $\iota (T)$

\[ \frac{Z_ m}{Z_ v}=\chi ^{m-v}=\prod _ i(\chi ^{e_ i})^{b_ i(m)}=\prod _ iw_ i^{b_ i(m)}. \]

Let $\Phi _ v\colon \mathbb C^ n\to \{ Z_ v\neq 0\} $ be the map whose coordinates $Z_ m/Z_ v$ are the monomials $\prod _ iw_ i^{b_ i(m)}$. Its image $\Gamma _ v$ is the graph of a polynomial map $\gamma $ from the coordinates $w_ i=Z_{v+e_ i}/Z_ v$ of the affine chart $\{ Z_ v\neq 0\} $ to the remaining coordinates $y=(Z_ m/Z_ v)_{m\neq v,v+e_1,\ldots ,v+e_ n}$. Hence $\Gamma _ v$ is closed in the chart, and in the holomorphic coordinates $(w,y-\gamma (w))$ of the chart it is the coordinate plane $\{ y-\gamma (w)=0\} $; so $\Gamma _ v$ is a closed complex submanifold of the chart, and $w$ is a biholomorphism of $\Gamma _ v$ onto $\mathbb C^ n$, with inverse $\Phi _ v$. Since $e_1,\ldots ,e_ n$ is a basis of $M$, the map $t\mapsto (\chi ^{e_1}(t),\ldots ,\chi ^{e_ n}(t))$ is a bijection of $T$ onto $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$, and the last display shows that $\iota (T)=\Phi _ v((\mathbb C^*)^ n)$. As $(\mathbb C^*)^ n$ is dense in $\mathbb C^ n$ and $\Gamma _ v$ is closed in the chart, the closure of $\iota (T)$ in the chart is $\Gamma _ v$; that is, $U_ v=\Gamma _ v$. By the first step the sets $U_ v$ cover $X_ P$, so $X_ P$ is a complex submanifold of dimension $n$ with the asserted charts. The coordinates $w_ i$ recover $t$ from $\iota (t)$, so $\iota $ is injective with holomorphic inverse $w$ on $\iota (T)$, and $\iota (T)=\Phi _ v((\mathbb C^*)^ n)$ is open in $U_ v$. Thus $\iota $ is a biholomorphism of $T$ onto a dense open subset of $X_ P$.

Algebraicity and irreducibility. In addition to the equations of the first step, consider for each vertex $v$ and each $m\in A\setminus \{ v\} $ the homogeneous equation

\[ Z_ m Z_ v^{\sum _ i b_ i(m)-1}=\prod _ i Z_{v+e_ i}^{b_ i(m)}, \]

where $\sum _ ib_ i(m)\geq 1$ because $m\neq v$. It holds on $\iota (T)$, hence on $X_ P$. Let $Y$ be the common zero set of all these equations in $\mathbb P^{|A|-1}$, so that $X_ P\subset Y$. By the equations of the first step, every point of $Y$ has a nonzero vertex coordinate $Z_ v$, and on $\{ Z_ v\neq 0\} $ the equations for $v$ say exactly that $Z_ m/Z_ v=\prod _ iw_ i^{b_ i(m)}$; thus $Y\cap \{ Z_ v\neq 0\} \subset \Gamma _ v=U_ v$. Hence $Y=X_ P$. To prove irreducibility, suppose that $X_ P=Y_1\cup Y_2$, where $Y_1,Y_2\subsetneq X_ P$ are common zero sets of homogeneous polynomials, and choose homogeneous polynomials $f_1,f_2$ with $f_ k=0$ on $Y_ k$ and $f_ k\not\equiv 0$ on $X_ P$. Then $g_ k(t)=f_ k((\chi ^ m(t))_{m\in A})$ is a Laurent polynomial in the coordinates of $T\cong (\mathbb C^*)^ n$. It is not identically zero: otherwise $f_ k$ would vanish on $\iota (T)$, hence on its closure $X_ P$, since the zero set of a homogeneous polynomial is closed. On the other hand $g_1g_2=0$ on $T$, because $f_1f_2=0$ on $X_ P$. This is impossible. Indeed, since $g_1$ is continuous and not identically zero, it does not vanish on some nonempty open set $W\subset (\mathbb C^*)^ n$, and $g_2$ vanishes on $W$. A suitable monomial multiple of $g_2$ is a polynomial that vanishes on $W$, so all its derivatives vanish at a point of $W$; being the sum of its Taylor expansion at that point, it is zero. Hence $g_2=0$, a contradiction. Thus $X_ P$ is irreducible.

The line bundle. The coordinate sections $s_ m=Z_ m|_{X_ P}$, $m\in A$, of $L$ have no common zero and define the inclusion $X_ P\subset \mathbb P^{|A|-1}$. By theorem 21.9.5 with $k=1$, whose proof uses only the vertex charts constructed above, they form a basis of $H^0(X_ P,L)$; in the notation of that theorem, $s_ m=\chi ^ ms_0$. Hence the full space of sections of $L$ defines an embedding, and $L$ is very ample in the sense of section 10.6. On $U_ v$ the section $s_ v$ has no zeros, and the Fubini–Study metric of example 10.6.6, example 10.6.7, restricted to $L$, gives it the squared norm

\[ |s_ v|^2=\frac{|Z_ v|^2}{\sum _{m\in A}|Z_ m|^2}=\mathrm e^{-H_ v},\qquad H_ v=\log \sum _{m\in A}|w^{b(m)}|^2, \]

where $w^ b=\prod _ iw_ i^{b_ i}$. The curvature on $U_ v$ is $\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}H_ v$, by definition 6.14.9. The complex Hessian of $H_ v$ is positive definite. Indeed, put $f_ m=w^{b(m)}$ and $S=\sum _ m|f_ m|^2$, and for $\xi \in \mathbb C^ n$ write $\xi f_ m=\sum _ j\xi _ j\partial f_ m/\partial w_ j$. Then

\[ \sum _{j,k}\frac{\partial ^2H_ v}{\partial w_ j\partial \bar w_ k}\xi _ j\bar\xi _ k =\frac{S\sum _ m|\xi f_ m|^2-\bigl |\sum _ m\bar f_ m\, \xi f_ m\bigr |^2}{S^2}\geq 0 \]

by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, with equality only if $(\xi f_ m)_ m=\lambda (f_ m)_ m$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb C$. The constant monomial $f_ v=1$ forces $\lambda =0$, and then the monomials $f_{v+e_ i}=w_ i$ give $\xi _ i=0$ for every $i$. Thus the restricted Fubini–Study metric is a smooth quadratic metric on $L$ with positive definite curvature form. In dimension zero $X_ P$ is a point and there is nothing to prove. $\square$
