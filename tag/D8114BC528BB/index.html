The invariant projective solution

From Singularities in Hermitian pluripotential theory · 14.19.1 The invariant projective solution

We keep the symmetric projective data of corollary 14.19.2. Thus $X=\mathbb P^3$ with block coordinates $[A:B]$ and Fubini–Study form $\omega _ X$; on $U=\{ A\ne 0,\ B\ne 0\} $ we use the radial coordinate $r$, the rank-one forms $P,Q,R$ and the coefficients $x,y,z,p,z_0$ of 11.6.1, 11.6.2. The function $\rho (r)$ is the even correction of 11.6.4, not a barrier, and $\omega =\omega _0+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}\rho $ is the resulting Hermitian form, with $\omega _0=xP+yQ+zR$; the density $f$ is that of theorem 11.6.1. Let $K$ be the group generated by the block unitary group $U(2)\times U(2)$ and the exchange $\iota [A:B]=[B:A]$. Both preserve $\omega $ and $f\omega _ X^3$. Denote by $u_*$ the normalized $\iota $-invariant solution of proposition 11.6.2, given by the symmetric transition pair $(-t_*,t_*)$.

Theorem 14.19.3. For the symmetric projective data, $u_*$ is the unique normalized bounded $K$-invariant solution of

\[ \mathrm{MA}_\omega (u)=f\omega _ X^3. \]

If $u_\lambda $ is the unique solution of

\[ \mathrm{MA}_\omega (u_\lambda ) =\mathrm{e}^{\lambda u_\lambda }f\omega _ X^3,\qquad \lambda >0, \]

then

\[ u_\lambda -\sup _ Xu_\lambda \longrightarrow u_* \quad \hbox{uniformly as }\lambda \downarrow 0. \]

The selected limit $u_*$ is $C^{1,1}$.

Uniqueness holds only within the $K$-invariant class: by proposition 11.6.2, the same equation has uncountably many normalized solutions. Since a cluster limit is a priori only bounded, the proof needs the radial form of the equation for bounded invariant solutions, which we derive first. For a real scalar $s$, define the coefficient polynomial $A(r,s)$ and its primitive $\Phi (r,s)$ by

\[ A(r,s)=(x(r)+s)(y(r)-s),\qquad \Phi (r,s)=\int _0^ s A(r,t)\, \mathrm{d}t. \]

Partial derivatives of $\Phi $ with respect to $r$ below keep $s$ fixed.

Lemma 14.19.4. Suppose $u$ is a bounded block-unitary-invariant solution of the zero-exponent equation. On $U$ write $u+\rho =G(r)$. Then $G$ is locally Lipschitz, its derivative $h=G'$ has locally bounded variation, and its one-sided representatives satisfy

\[ -x(r)\leq h(r\pm )\leq y(r),\qquad \mathrm{d}h+z\, \mathrm{d}r\geq 0. \]

In fact $h$ is continuous. With

\[ k(r)=\begin{cases} 0,& |r|\leq 1,\\ x(r)y(r)z(r),& |r|>1,\end{cases} \]

it satisfies the distributional identity on $\mathbb R$

14.67
\begin{equation} \label{eq:numa-flux-identity} \mathrm{d}\bigl (\Phi (r,h(r))\bigr ) =\bigl (k(r)-A(r,h(r))z(r) +\partial _ r\Phi (r,h(r))\bigr )\, \mathrm{d}r. \end{equation}

On every open interval where $A(r,h(r))>0$, $h$ is smooth and

\[ h'=\frac{k(r)}{A(r,h)}-z. \]

Proof. Reduction to one variable. Block-unitary orbits in $U$ are exactly the level sets of $r$: normalize the sum of the squared norms of the two coordinate blocks, and map the two vectors of the same respective lengths by their unitary groups. Thus invariance gives a function $G$ of one real variable.

On a compact $r$-interval, the radial direction annihilates $P,Q$, while $R$ is strictly positive in that direction. Testing positivity of $\omega _0+\mathrm{dd}^{\mathrm{c}}G(r)$ in this direction gives $G''+z\geq 0$ as a one-dimensional distribution. One may obtain this first by smooth approximation of the scalar distribution and then by testing with nonnegative functions. Adding a twice integrated primitive of $z$ makes $G$ convex. A finite bounded convex function on an interval is locally Lipschitz, and its derivative is locally of bounded variation. The quasi-plurisubharmonic representative is the corresponding continuous representative. The horizontal directions give $x+h\geq 0$, $y-h\geq 0$ almost everywhere. Continuity of $x,y$ and the one-sided limits of a function of bounded variation give these bounds for both one-sided representatives. The radial inequality is precisely $\mathrm{d}h+z\, \mathrm{d}r\geq 0$; in particular jumps of $h$ can only be upward.

The radial form of the measure. We next justify the formula for the measure, including possible jumps. Identify $U$ smoothly with $\mathbb R\times Y$, where $Y=\{ r=0\} $, by real scaling of one block. The forms $P,Q$ are independent of $r$ in this identification. So is $\mathrm d^{\mathrm c}r$: in local coordinates it is the angular differential of the relative complex scale plus fixed base terms. Therefore

\[ P\wedge Q\wedge R=\mathrm{d}r\wedge \nu , \]

where $\nu $ is a fixed smooth positive volume form on the compact orbit $Y$, with the orientation chosen by this equality.

For smooth $G$, the rank-one wedge formula gives the radial coefficient of its Monge–Ampère measure, after dividing by $6\nu $, as

\[ A(r,h)(z+h')\, \mathrm{d}r =A(r,h)z\, \mathrm{d}r+\mathrm{d}\bigl (\Phi (r,h)\bigr ) -\partial _ r\Phi (r,h)\, \mathrm{d}r. \]

This identity passes to a locally Lipschitz $G$ with the preceding lower Hessian bound. Indeed, convolve $G$ on a slightly larger compact interval. The derivatives $h_\epsilon $ are uniformly bounded and converge to $h$ almost everywhere and in every finite $L^ q$ space. Their second derivatives have a uniform lower bound. Consequently the functions $G_\epsilon (r)$ have a common quasi-plurisubharmonic lower Hessian bound on compact subsets of $U$, and converge uniformly to $G(r)$. A fixed smooth local correction and bounded Bedford–Taylor continuity give convergence of their signed products. On the right-hand side of the formula, the two ordinary terms converge in $L^1$, and $\Phi (r,h_\epsilon )\to \Phi (r,h)$ in $L^1$, giving distributional convergence of its derivative. This proves the same formula for the bounded product of $G$.

Continuity of $h$. After the same division by $6\nu $, the right-hand side $f\omega _ X^3$ has radial coefficient $k(r)\, \mathrm{d}r$. This gives (14.67), initially for the almost-everywhere representative of $h$. If $h$ jumped at $r_0$, the atom of its left side would be

\[ \Phi (r_0,h(r_0+))-\Phi (r_0,h(r_0-)) =\int _{h(r_0-)}^{h(r_0+)}(x(r_0)+s)(y(r_0)-s)\, \mathrm{d}s. \]

The right side has no atom. An upward jump of positive size lies in $[-x(r_0),y(r_0)]$, where the integrand is strictly positive in the interior. Since $x+y>0$ everywhere, such an integral is strictly positive. Thus there are no jumps. A locally bounded-variation function whose jumps all vanish has a continuous representative here: its distributional derivative consists of an absolutely continuous measure and a nonnegative singular measure, so its one-sided representatives coincide at every point. We henceforth use this representative. In particular $G$ is $C^1$ and $G'=h$ everywhere.

All coefficients on the right of (14.67) are now continuous, so $\Phi (r,h(r))$ is $C^1$. Where $\partial _ s\Phi =A>0$, the implicit function theorem gives $h\in C^1$ and the stated ordinary differential equation. Smoothness then follows by the smooth coefficients of that equation. The function $k$ is smooth, since the original density and its flat joins are smooth. $\square$

of theorem 14.19.3. We first show that a normalized bounded $K$-invariant solution $u$ coincides with $u_*$, by computing its slope $h$ on the band $|r|\leq 1$ and outside it. Apply lemma 14.19.4. Because $\rho $ and $u$ are invariant under block exchange, $G$ is even, so $h$ is odd and $h(0)=0$.

The slope on the band. On $0<r<1$ we have $x=p>0$ and $y=p+b\geq p>0$. Starting from zero, as long as $-p<h<y$, the equation is $h'=-z_0$. Thus $h(r)=-\int _0^ r z_0(s)\, \mathrm{d}s$. This decreasing function cannot meet the positive upper boundary $y$. Its first meeting with the lower boundary $-p$ occurs at the unique $t_*\in (0,1)$ satisfying

\[ \int _0^{t_*}z_0(s)\, \mathrm{d}s=p(t_*), \]

whose existence and uniqueness were proved in proposition 11.6.2.

After that meeting, $h$ must stay on $-p$ until $r=1$. To prove it, suppose a connected component $(a_0,b_0)$ of $\{ h+p>0\} $ lies to its right. Its left endpoint has $a_0\geq t_*$ and $h(a_0)=-p(a_0)<0$. Near that endpoint, $h<0<y$; hence on the component the interior equation applies for as long as $h<0$. It forces $h'=-z_0<0$, so $h$ cannot first reach zero. The equation therefore holds throughout the component. But then

\[ (h+p)'=p'-z_0<0, \]

contradicting positivity of $h+p$ immediately to the right of its zero at $a_0$. Thus $h=-p$ on $[t_*,1]$. Oddness determines the entire negative half of the band. The slope on $[-1,1]$ is exactly that of the symmetric explicit solution.

The slope outside the band. Here boundedness of $u$ at the projective ends matters. On $r>1$, $k=xyz>0$. Neither horizontal coefficient can vanish at a finite point of this interval. For the lower one define

\[ \Psi (r)=\int _{-x(r)}^{h(r)}A(r,s)\, \mathrm{d}s\geq 0. \]

Equation (14.67) and the smooth lower endpoint show that $\Psi $ is $C^1$. If $h(r_0)=-x(r_0)$, its derivative at that point is $k(r_0)>0$: the $Az$ term vanishes, and the two partial-$r$ terms agree at the common endpoint. This contradicts that the nonnegative $C^1$ function $\Psi $ has an interior minimum zero there. The upper-endpoint integral $\int _{h(r)}^{y(r)}A(r,s)\, \mathrm{d}s$ gives the same contradiction, with derivative $-k(r_0)$, when $h=y$. Thus $-x<h<y$ everywhere on $r>1$, and the smooth ordinary differential equation of the lemma applies.

For $r\geq 2$ the background is Fubini–Study:

\[ x=\sigma ,\qquad y=1-\sigma ,\qquad z=\sigma ', \qquad \sigma =\frac{\mathrm{e}^ r}{1+\mathrm{e}^ r}. \]

Set $a=\sigma +h\in (0,1)$. Its equation is

\[ a(1-a)a'=\sigma (1-\sigma )\sigma '>0. \]

Hence $a$ is increasing and has a limit in $(0,1]$ at $+\infty $. If that limit were less than one, $h=a-\sigma $ would approach a negative constant and its primitive $G$ would be unbounded below, contrary to boundedness of $u$ and $\rho $. The limit is therefore one. Integrating the equation gives

\[ \frac{a^2}{2}-\frac{a^3}{3} =\frac{\sigma ^2}{2}-\frac{\sigma ^3}{3}+C. \]

Taking the limit at infinity gives $C=0$. The scalar function $s^2/2-s^3/3$ is strictly increasing on $(0,1)$, so $a=\sigma $ and $h=0$ for $r\geq 2$. Ordinary uniqueness propagates this zero solution throughout $r>1$, since the coefficients are smooth and the two horizontal coefficients stay positive there. Oddness gives $h=0$ on $r<-1$ as well.

We have proved that $G'$ agrees everywhere with the symmetric explicit slope. Thus $u$ differs from $u_*$ by a constant on $U$. The two quasi-plurisubharmonic functions then agree up to that constant on $X$ by their canonical representatives. Their supremum normalization removes the constant. This proves uniqueness within the bounded $K$-invariant class.

Convergence. Finally proposition 14.19.1 gives uniform precompactness of the normalized positive-exponent family. Every cluster limit solves the zero-exponent equation and is $K$-invariant, because the data are $K$-invariant. The scalar is one for these data. The uniqueness just proved identifies every cluster limit with $u_*$. If convergence of the whole family failed, a sequence staying a fixed positive uniform distance from $u_*$ would have a convergent subsequence, contradicting that identification. This proves uniform convergence and the asserted $C^{1,1}$ regularity of the limit. $\square$
