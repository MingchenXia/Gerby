Harmonic functions and the Dirichlet problem

From Foundations of pluripotential theory · 2 Harmonic functions and the Dirichlet problem

Harmonic functions are the solutions of the Laplace equation $\Delta h=0$. In potential theory they play the role that affine functions play for convex functions: the subharmonic functions of chapter 3 are studied by comparison with them, and the fundamental solution of the Laplacian, together with the Green functions obtained from it by harmonic corrections, defines the potentials and energies of chapter 4, chapter 5. Unless stated otherwise, $\Omega $ denotes an open subset of $\mathbb R^ d$, where $d\geq 1$.

We begin with mean values. After introducing surface measure, polar coordinates and the divergence theorem on balls, we show that a continuous function is harmonic exactly when its value at the center of every closed ball in its domain equals its mean over the boundary sphere (theorem 2.1.2). Averaging against a mollifier then shows that harmonic functions are smooth, with interior estimates for all their derivatives. A distribution in the sense of chapter 1 need not have pointwise values; nevertheless, Weyl's lemma (theorem 2.1.4) shows that a distribution with vanishing Laplacian is a smooth harmonic function. The fundamental solution of the Laplacian (proposition 2.2.1) fixes the sign conventions for all later potentials.

The Dirichlet problem asks for a harmonic function with prescribed continuous boundary values. On a ball it is solved explicitly by the Poisson integral (theorem 2.3.1), whose kernel also yields the Harnack inequalities and the compactness of families of nonnegative harmonic functions. On a general bounded open set there is no such formula, and we use Perron's method instead. The candidate solution is the pointwise supremum of all continuous subsolutions whose boundary values do not exceed the data; replacing a subsolution on a small ball by the Poisson integral of its values shows that this envelope is harmonic (theorem 2.4.2). The envelope also depends linearly on the data (theorem 2.4.4). A boundary point is regular if the envelope attains the prescribed value there for all data. Regularity is decided by the existence of a barrier, which need only be constructed near the point (theorem 2.4.6, proposition 2.4.7); for $d\geq 2$, regularity fails at the center of a punctured ball. When every boundary point is regular, the solution operator is integration against harmonic measure, and correcting the fundamental solution by the harmonic function with the same boundary values gives the Green function (theorem 2.5.2).

Historical remarks and references for this chapter are collected in section B.2.
